# Do Perturbation Predictors Learn Gene Interactions? Diagnosing Extrapolation in Agentic Screening 

Ziyuan Gao 

Department of Computer Science and Technology University of Cambridge 

zg344@cam.ac.uk 

# Abstract 

High-throughput perturbation screens increasingly use machine-learning models to predict the effects of gene combinations that were never measured. A difficult case arises when both genes in a test pair were seen individually during training, but their combination was not. In this setting, standard OOD methods often fail because neither gene appears unfamiliar on its own. We therefore compare each model prediction with a simple additive prediction obtained by summing the two observed single-gene effects. The difference between them, which we call addi-tive disagreement (AD), measures how much extra interaction effect the model introduces for an unseen combination. Across eight prediction methods and three Perturb-seq datasets, specialised neural predictors do not consistently outperform this simple additive baseline. More importantly, predictions with larger deviations from the additive baseline tend to have larger errors. Conventional output-based and embedding-based confidence signals are inconsistent across datasets and mod-els, whereas AD remains informative across all evaluated compositional settings. We further study how this signal can guide decisions in an agentic perturbation-screening workflow. The code is available at https://github.com/zygao930/OOD. 

# 1 Introduction 

High-throughput perturbation screens have become a central experimental paradigm in functional genomics, enabling systematic measurement of how genetic interventions reshape cellular states at single-cell resolution [1–4]. These datasets have driven the development of virtual cell models that aim to predict cellular responses to perturbations not directly observed during training [5–8]. As such models are increasingly used to prioritise candidate interventions and narrow experimental search spaces [9, 10], and to support emerging agentic workflows that autonomously design perturbation experiments, query predictors, and rank candidates for follow-up [11–15], their reliability becomes a practical concern rather than merely an academic one. When high-confidence but unreliable predictions are used to guide costly follow-up experiments, they can, in the worst case, waste limited resources, distort candidate rankings, and lead subsequent analyses towards misleading biological interpretations [16–19]. Recent perturbation predictors include graph-based models [5], compositional approaches [6], and single-cell foundation models [7, 8, 20], yet benchmarks show that these specialised methods do not consistently outperform simple linear baselines on unseen perturbations and combinations [21, 18, 22, 23]. Standard OOD and uncertainty methods, including maximum softmax probability [24], kNN [25], Mahalanobis distance [26], deep ensembles [27], MC dropout [28], and selective prediction [29], are not designed for compositional OOD [30, 31], where familiar components form an unseen combination. In perturbation prediction, both genes may therefore be familiar while their joint effect is not, making unreliable extrapolation difficult to detect from input proximity alone.

To understand where prediction errors originate, we decompose each model’s output into a component supported by observed single-gene effects and a residual interaction term that the model extrapolates without direct training signal. The magnitude of this residual, which we call additive disagreement 

(AD), quantifies how strongly a prediction departs from an experimentally grounded additive reference. We evaluate this decomposition across eight prediction methods spanning six modelling paradigms on two combinatorial perturbation datasets, Norman and Joung–Zhang, together with Adamson as a complementary single-gene OOD setting [2, 32, 33]. We compare AD with eight alternative reliability signals spanning embedding-based, output-based, and ensemble-based approaches. We further evaluate the practical utility of these reliability signals in an agentic perturbation-screening setting, examining whether they can identify predictions that rely on unsupported interaction extrapolation. Our main findings are: (1) we show across eight prediction methods that specialised neural predictors do not consistently outperform simple additive baselines on unseen gene combinations, and identify the co-expression graph as a major source of GEARS’s extrapolation failures; (2) we show that generic reliability signals, including kNN distance, Mahalanobis distance, and ensemble uncertainty, are substantially weaker and less consistent than AD under compositional shift; and (3) by separating observed additive effects from model-implied interactions, we show that AD provides the most consistent indicator of prediction failure across the two compositional datasets, suggesting that current models do not reliably learn interactions that transfer to unseen combinations. 

# 2 Method 

As illustrated in Figure 1, predictions for novel gene combinations contain an observed additive component and an extrapolated interaction component. Existing evaluations [21, 18, 22, 23] typically treat the prediction as a single point estimate, obscuring which component drives the error. We formalise this distinction to diagnose prediction failures. When a model predicts the effect of combining genes A and B, both genes have been observed individually during training and the model has learned representations for each. The challenge is therefore not that the individual inputs are unfamiliar, but that the model must predict how A and B

interact despite never observing this particular combination. We evaluate this setting on CRISPRa perturbations in K562 cells from Norman et al. [2] and on a second combinatorial perturbation dataset from Joung–Zhang et al. [33]. We additionally use the single-gene CRISPRi dataset from Adamson et al. [32] to examine whether generic reliability signals remain informative under a different OOD setting. Dataset and split details are in Appendix B. Unlike generic uncertainty measures, AD directly targets the component that requires compositional generalisation and has a clear biological interpretation as the mean magnitude of the model-implied departure from additivity. Standard OOD methods may fail under compositional shift because kNN [25] and Mahalanobis distance [26] measure input novelty, while both genes are individually observed and the novelty lies in their interaction. We therefore evaluate eight prediction methods, from Ridge and the additive prior to MLP, Transformer [34], CPA [6], scGPT based predictors [7], and GEARS [5]. Across models, we compare AD with eight alternative reliability signals, including output uncertainty and embedding based OOD scores. Performance is assessed by failure detection AUROC, error rank correlation, and MAE improvement under selective abstention [29] (Appendix B). As perturbation predictors are increasingly integrated into agentic pipelines for experimental design [11, 12], identifying predictions that rely on unsupported interaction extrapolation becomes practically important. Any system querying a predictor can simultaneously compute the additive reference, δA + δB , and compare it with the model output using only single-gene effect estimates.

# 4 Discussion 

We test whether the observed failures arise from weak interaction signal, interaction complexity, model design, or evaluation choice. The failures are not due to weak interactions: the interaction component accounts for 68% of the total effect on Norman and 104% on average on Joung–Zhang, with no pair below 10%. Interaction magnitude also does not consistently predict difficulty. GEARS excess error increases with interaction magnitude on Norman ( ρ = 0 .527 , p < 10 −4), whereas several other models show weak or negative correlations, and all evaluated models show strong negative correlations on Joung–Zhang, including GEARS ( ρ = −0.749 , p < 10 −4). The interactions nevertheless exhibit shared structure: on Norman, the first principal component explains 31.3% of the variance, four explain 50%, and 21 explain 90%; on Joung–Zhang, the corresponding values are 21.3%, four, and 15 components. Thus, interactions are substantial and structured, but their difficulty depends on both model and dataset rather than interaction strength alone. The Norman analyses further implicate the co-expression graph in GEARS’s failures. Co-expression degree correlates with both predicted interaction magnitude ( ρ = 0 .633 ) and prediction error ( ρ =0.626 ). Removing the co-expression graph reduces MAE from 0.0336 to 0.0228, while removing both graphs yields 0.0259. This suggests that co-expression message passing amplifies erroneous corrections around highly connected genes rather than capturing transferable pair-specific interactions, although graph-ablated variants still underperform the additive baseline. Overall, the evidence points primarily to modeling choices. The evaluated architectures do not consistently generalize structured interaction effects to unseen combinations, and in GEARS, co-expression message passing is a major contributor to failure. We also find an evaluation-dependent limitation of additive disagreement: on Norman, its failure-detection AUROC falls from 0.990 under transcriptome-wide MAE to 0.619 under Top20 MAE, showing that reliability over the full transcriptome does not necessarily transfer to a restricted gene set. 5References

8A Additional Analysis of Reliability Signals 

Why embedding-based novelty can miss compositional shift. Under compositional shift, both constituent genes A and B have been observed individually during training, although their combina-tion has not. Pair representations constructed from familiar gene embeddings may therefore remain close to the training distribution. This is particularly likely when pair embeddings are formed through smooth aggregation functions such as averaging or pooling, because the resulting representation primarily reflects component-level familiarity rather than whether the interaction has been observed. Consequently, kNN distance, Mahalanobis distance, and cosine similarity may assign low novelty to an unseen combination even when its interaction prediction is unreliable. Embedding-based detec-tion need not fail in every setting, but component-level familiarity weakens the alignment between representation-space novelty and interaction-level novelty. 

Relationship between AD and prediction error. Let the true interaction residual be 

Itrue (A, B ) = y(A, B ) − (δA + δB ). (4) Since 

fθ (A, B ) − y(A, B ) = ˆIθ (A, B ) − Itrue (A, B ), (5) the triangle inequality gives 

∥ ˆIθ (A, B )∥1,G − ∥ Itrue (A, B )∥1,G ≤ ∥ fθ (A, B )−y(A, B )∥1,G ≤ ∥ ˆIθ (A, B )∥1,G +∥Itrue (A, B )∥1,G .

(6) In the additive limiting case, Itrue (A, B ) = 0 , prediction error is exactly equal to AD. More generally, AD measures how strongly the model departs from the experimentally grounded additive reference. A large AD does not necessarily imply a large error, because a large correction may be accurate if it matches the true interaction. AD should therefore be interpreted as a risk signal for unsupported interaction extrapolation, rather than as a deterministic estimate of prediction error. Empirically, AD is strongly associated with GEARS prediction error on both compositional datasets, with Spearman correlations of ρ = 0 .982 on Norman and ρ = 0 .830 on Joung–Zhang. Across all evaluated models, its correlations range from 0.528 to 0.982 on Norman and from 0.767 to 0.939 

on Joung–Zhang. Thus, although the bounds do not guarantee a monotonic relationship, large model-implied interaction corrections are consistently associated with higher error in the evaluated compositional settings. 

Prediction magnitude versus additive disagreement. Because 

fθ (A, B ) = ( δA + δB ) + ˆIθ (A, B ), (7) PM and AD may induce similar rankings when variation in the additive component is small relative to variation in the model-implied interaction correction. This occurs for GEARS on Norman, where their Spearman correlations with error are 0.978 and 0.982 , respectively. However, PM also increases for perturbations with large but experimentally supported single-gene effects. It therefore conflates the magnitude of the additive response with the magnitude of the extrapolated interaction correction. This distinction becomes clearer across models and datasets. On Joung–Zhang, PM correlates strongly with error for Ridge ( ρ = 0 .812 ), but is close to zero for Transformer ( ρ = 0 .041 ) and scGPT (ρ = 0 .021 ). AD remains positively associated with error for every evaluated model. By subtracting the observed additive component, AD more specifically targets interaction-level extrapolation. 

Dependence on the evaluation metric. The reliability of AD depends on how prediction error is defined. On Norman, AD achieves a failure-detection AUROC of 0.990 when failure is measured by full-transcriptome MAE, but only 0.619 under Top20 MAE. The same pattern holds for squared error, with AUROC decreasing from 0.961 under full-transcriptome MSE to 0.597 under Top20 MSE. This difference follows from the gene sets used by the signal and the evaluation metric. Full-transcriptome AD aggregates departures from the additive reference across all evaluated genes and is therefore well aligned with full-transcriptome error. Top20 metrics evaluate only the most differentially expressed genes for each pair. A prediction may accumulate small deviations across many genes while remaining accurate on the Top20 set, or may have a modest global deviation but large errors on a few selected genes. Reliability established at the transcriptome-wide level therefore does not automatically transfer to a restricted gene set. Applications focused on biomarkers, pathways, or selected genes should define and validate reliability over the same target set. 9B Experimental Details 

B.1 Datasets Datasets. We evaluate on three Perturb-seq datasets. 

Norman (CRISPRa, K562). After filtering through the GEARS preprocessing pipeline, the dataset contains 89,357 cells and 5,045 genes [5]. It includes 105 single-gene and 131 combinatorial perturbation conditions [2]. Among the combinatorial conditions, 128 pairs have both constituent genes represented among the observed single-gene perturbations and are therefore eligible for compositional OOD evaluation. This provides the primary setting in which both constituent genes are observed during training but their combination is held out. 

Joung–Zhang (combinatorial). We use the combinatorial perturbation dataset from Joung et al. [33]. After requiring both constituent genes of a double perturbation to have corresponding single-gene conditions, 44 pairs remain eligible for evaluation. This provides a second compositional OOD setting with a different perturbation library and experimental context. 

Adamson (CRISPRi, K562). We use the single-gene perturbation dataset from Adamson et al. [32] as a complementary single-gene OOD setting. Here, the held-out perturbation gene itself is absent from training. The evaluation contains 48 held-out single-gene perturbations across three splits. All three datasets (Norman, Joung–Zhang, Adamson) are publicly available and cited to their original publications, and the model implementations we build on (GEARS, scGPT, CPA) are used under their respective open-source licenses. 

B.2 Preprocessing 

For each perturbation condition c, we represent its effect relative to control as 

δc = μc − μctrl , (8) where μc and μctrl denote the mean expression profiles of the perturbation and control cells, re-spectively. A double perturbation (A, B ) is eligible for compositional evaluation only when both corresponding single-gene conditions are observed, allowing the additive reference 

δA + δB (9) to be constructed. Ridge, MLP, Transformer, CPA, and scGPT are trained on condition-level mean effect vectors, whereas GEARS is trained on single-cell expression profiles through its original data pipeline. For Top20 MAE, the 20 genes with the largest absolute observed effect are selected separately for each held-out perturbation. 

Splits. For Norman, each seed (42 , 43 , 44) partitions the eligible pairs into 20% test pairs, 20% validation pairs, and 60% training pairs. Each split contains 25 test pairs and 25 validation pairs, yielding 75 pair-seed test evaluations across the three seeds. All single-gene conditions and non-eligible combinatorial conditions remain in training. For Joung–Zhang, each of the 44 eligible double perturbations is held out once in leave-one-out cross-validation. All remaining conditions are used for training, and each model is retrained independently for every fold. For Adamson, single-gene perturbations are held out across three predefined splits, yielding 48 test evaluations in total. 10 B.3 Prediction Methods

18 D Limitations and Future Directions 

The present framework has several limitations. First, additive disagreement requires observed single-gene effects for both constituent perturbations. It is therefore defined for compositional OOD, where the components are individually observed, but not for settings in which the perturbed gene itself is unseen, such as the Adamson evaluation. Second, AD measures the magnitude of the model-implied interaction correction rather than its biological correctness. A large correction may be accurate when it matches the true interaction, and a small correction may still be wrong. AD should therefore be interpreted as a risk signal for unsupported extrapolation, not as a direct estimate of interaction error. Third, reliability depends on the evaluation target. AD performs strongly under full-transcriptome MAE on Norman but transfers poorly to Top20 error. A global transcriptome-wide score may therefore be insufficient for applications focused on particular genes, pathways, or cell states. Gene-level and pathway-level disagreement measures are a natural extension. Finally, the present study establishes the framework for pairwise perturbations across distinct compo-sitional settings. Extending it to higher-order combinations will require richer references than simple pairwise additivity, potentially using hierarchical, pathway-aware, or mechanism-informed priors. The same decomposition may also generalize to other compositional scientific settings, including drug combinations and multimodal interventions.
