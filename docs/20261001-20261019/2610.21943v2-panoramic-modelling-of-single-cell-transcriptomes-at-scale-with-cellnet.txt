SUMMARY
Comprehensively characterizing cellular landscapes to elucidate cell diversity is a central goal in
biology. Recent advances in single-cell profiling have created unprecedented opportunities for this
endeavor by capturing diverse transcriptomes with remarkable breadth. However, current founda-
tion models generally struggle to leverage such holistic and valuable information to represent a
cell. Here we present CellNet, which enables panoramic modelling of single-cell transcriptomes
at scale by integrating DNA-derived gene identities and measured RNA expression into a shared
image representation. Pretrained on 231 million image-based cell profiles across 40 species and
covering over one million genes, CellNet improves the recognition of rare and condition-associated
cellular states, enables the study of genes beyond experimentally measured panels, and uncovers
associations between poorly characterized non-coding RNAs and known biological programmes.
CellNet paves the way for AI-enabled cell-biology discovery at the whole-transcriptome scale. The
CellNet website is available at https://www.cellnet.top.
INTRODUCTION
Elucidating cellular diversity is essential for understanding tissue development, identifying disease-
associated cell populations, and investigating gene programmes linked to specialized functions1–4.
Single-cell transcriptomic atlases have enabled this by characterizing cell identities and states
across tissues and species5–7. However, the molecular view provided by these atlases depends
on which transcripts are measured. Conventional poly(A)-based profiling underrepresents many
RNA classes, hindering the study of their expression and relationships with other genes. Ad-
vanced technologies, e.g., VASA-seq and TotalX, overcome this limitation by providing a more
comprehensive view of the transcriptome, spanning both coding and non-coding RNAs6,8. This
creates unprecedented opportunities to uncover new cell-type markers, developmental expression
patterns, and regulatory modules.
Single-cell foundation models have made considerable progress in learning transferable
representations from transcriptomic data, enabling a wide range of applications including cell an-
notation, gene-network analysis, and cross-species comparison9,10. Geneformer11 demonstrated
how large-scale pretraining enables predictions in network biology, while scGPT12 extended

generative modelling to single-cell multi-omics. UCE13 and TranscriptFormer14 learned universal
cell representations across datasets and species. However, the molecular information these
models leverage to represent a cell remains constrained, owing to the longstanding trade-off
between gene coverage and computational efficiency, although advanced sequencing assays
have captured diverse transcriptomes with remarkable breadth. Protein-derived identities cannot
directly describe genes without protein products, whereas predefined gene vocabularies are
ill-suited to open-set cellular representation. Moreover, gene-as-token paradigms make broader
coverage more computationally demanding; as a compromise, UCE, for instance, samples 1,024
expressed genes per cell13. Consequently, critical transcripts measured by advanced assays may
fall outside the molecular context available to models, leaving a substantial information gap that
constrains the model’s capacity in key tasks such as cell-state recognition, expression prediction,
and gene-association discovery.
A representation that keeps pace with these measurements must accommodate diverse gene
identities while not making cellular context prohibitively expensive to models. Genomic sequence
supplies a unified identity for both coding and non-coding genes, independent of any protein
product15–18. An image-based layout offer a feasible approach to organizing numerous genes into
a structured cellular representation19,20. In addition, the inherent sparsity and noise of single-cell
measurements motivates a learning strategy that not only captures stable cellular information but
also preserves underlying gene-level expression variations21,22. These principles together provide
a route towards representing the full transcriptomic space across biological scales, spanning cells,
tissues, and species.
Here we present CellNet, a single-cell foundation model that enables panoramic modelling
of single-cell transcriptomes at scale by integrating DNA-derived gene identities and cellular
expression into a common image representation. DNA sequence-derived gene features determine
gene layouts in the image, while a multiview joint-embedding predictive architecture promotes
consistent representations under noisy readouts. Gene-conditioned expression prediction is
progressively introduced in self-supervised pretraining to encourage the retention of gene-level
information in cellular representations. Alongside the foundation model, we publicly release a
collection of 231 million transcriptomic images from 40 species, providing a large-scale, reusable
resource for image-based single-cell studies. Experimental results show that CellNet improves
identification of rare cell populations and condition-associated states and remains competitive on
other representation-based tasks. Moreover, it recovers withheld coding-gene expression and
selected non-coding transcripts in total-RNA data, with extensions to spatial measurements and
targets without expression-pretraining exposure. Finally, its gene representations retrieve known
molecular modules and developmental or neural programmes, including from sparsely detected
non-coding RNA queries. These observations suggest that CellNet provides a foundation for
cell-biology discoveries at the whole-transcriptome scale through its panoramic modelling of
single-cell transcriptomes.

Each gene occupied a distinct pixel on a 384 × 384 canvas, with positions fixed across cells
of the same species. Expression measurements were mapped to these positions alongside
assay-specific masks distinguishing measured zeros from unmeasured genes.
Each transcriptome image was divided into non-overlapping patches of 16 × 16 pixels, yielding
up to 576 patch tokens (Fig. 1b). Within each patch, aggregated DNA-derived gene identities
were combined with local expression information before entering the DINOv3 backbone23. This
image-based organization allows many genes to contribute to a shared cellular context without
allocating a separate global token to every gene. The encoder learns a cell-level representation
while retaining contextual features that can be queried for individual gene predictions.
To learn cellular representations from sparse, noisy measurements, we trained CellNet using
multiview latent-space learning (Fig. 1c). Two lightly masked global views and six more extensively
masked local views were generated from each cell. Their latent representations were encouraged
to agree, while Gaussian distribution regularization mitigated representation collapse24.
To retain gene-level expression information, we paired latent-space learning with a gene-
conditioned expression decoder (Fig. 1c). Given the DNA-derived identity of a target gene, the
decoder uses the cell representation and visible patch context to predict whether the gene is
detected and its expression magnitude. The expression-prediction objective was introduced after
an initial warm-up period, with its weight gradually increased during pretraining. This design trains
the shared encoder for both cellular representation learning and gene-level prediction.
We pretrained CellNet on approximately 231 million cell profiles spanning 40 species and
publicly released the corresponding transcriptomic image collection. For pretraining, we con-
structed DNA-derived identity representations for over one million distinct genes across these
species, including both protein-coding and non-coding genes. The main pretraining run took
approximately two weeks on eight NVIDIA A800 GPUs. In comparison, UCE was trained for
40 days on 24 A100 GPUs13, while scLong reported 35 GPU days on a cluster of 96 A100
GPUs25. TranscriptFormer reported training on a CZI computing cluster equipped with 1,000
H100 GPUs14. Broad molecular coverage, efficient image-based encoding and complementary
pretraining objectives make CellNet a practical approach to panoramic transcriptome modelling at
scale, enabling researchers to investigate rare cell states, predict expression for open-set genes,
and uncover gene associations and coordinated gene programmes across a wider transcriptomic
landscape (Fig. 1d).
Cellular representations across tissues and species
A broader view of the transcriptome is most useful when molecular information can be interpreted
in the context of distinct cellular identities. We therefore evaluated whether CellNet retained these
identities across tissues and donors. Using 549,018 annotated cells from 18 tissues in Tabula
Sapiens v226, we compared seven pipelines based on frozen foundation-model representations
under donor-held-out evaluation11–14,25,27. CellNet achieved a tissue-equally weighted Macro-F1 of
0.621, exceeding the strongest comparison pipeline, TranscriptFormer (0.589; Fig. 2a). Improve-
ments were observed across multiple organs, including bone marrow, liver and subcutaneous
adipose tissue (Supplementary Fig. S.1a).
Reliable cellular representations must also preserve biological differences across experiments
while accommodating donor-associated variation. We assessed this balance across 26 multi-
donor tissues comprising 682,741 cells using the scIB framework28. CellNet achieved the highest
biological-conservation score (AvgBIO = 0.684, compared with 0.680 for UCE), while maintaining
a batch-mixing score of 0.886 (Fig. 2c). Tissue-level results across all 26 tissues are shown in
Supplementary Fig. S.1b,c.

We further examined whether CellNet could represent cellular identities across species
with different gene repertoires. Evaluations covered immune, germline and plant references,
including within-species identification and directional cross-species label transfer7,13,14 (Fig. 2b;
Supplementary Fig. S.2). Within-species recognition was particularly strong across seven immune
species, with a mean Macro-F1 of 0.871 versus 0.809 for TranscriptFormer. Cross-species transfer

and TranscriptFormer performed better in several animal tasks. Across the evaluated datasets
and organisms, CellNet retained useful cellular identity information while extending the molecular
scope available for modelling.
Resolving rare cell populations and condition-associated states
Cellular diversity extends beyond abundant cell types. Rare populations may have specialized
functions, while cells sharing the same broad identity can exhibit distinct transcriptional states.
Recognizing these differences is important for identifying the cellular populations and molecular
signatures associated with tissue function and disease. We therefore examined whether CellNet
could resolve such fine-grained diversity.
We first evaluated rare-cell recognition in independently collected human kidney and lung
atlases29,30, comprising 107,344 and 69,650 cells, respectively. Under donor-disjoint evaluation,
CellNet achieved the highest rare-class Macro-F1 among seven comparison pipelines in both
tissues: 0.646 in kidney and 0.762 in lung, compared with 0.638 and 0.737 for TranscriptFormer
(Fig. 2d,e). Class-level analyses showed recognition of low-abundance populations, including
kidney mast cells, parietal epithelial cells and lung myofibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. S.4a). Pre-
cision–recall analyses further characterized rare-population recovery (Supplementary Fig. S.3b,c).
We next examined transcriptional differences within shared cellular identities using CD4 T-cell
polarization31 and WI-38 fibroblast senescence-induction experiments32. In donor-held-out CD4
T cells, CellNet distinguished Th0, Th2 and Th17 conditions with a Macro-F1 of 0.936, compared
with 0.911 for TranscriptFormer (Fig. 2f,g). Complete-library holdouts in WI-38 cells yielded a
Macro-F1 of 0.919 versus 0.891 across three induction conditions (Fig. 2h). These advantages
were observed across individual conditions and biological replicates (Supplementary Fig. S.5a–c).
The non-coding RNA NEAT1 also exhibited consistent condition-associated expression differences
across CD4 donors, illustrating a molecular feature of these states (Supplementary Fig. S.5d).
CellNet preserves both broad cellular identities and finer distinctions among rare populations
and condition-associated states, providing a cellular representation for subsequent analyses of
gene expression and molecular relationships.

evaluated this capability by withholding target genes from TotalX profiles of human peripheral
blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and developing brain8, using 5,000 query cells from each
dataset.

We next examined non-coding RNAs, many of which are poorly represented in conventional
single-cell measurements and existing foundation models. With all non-coding targets withheld
simultaneously, CellNet inferred their expression using only the observed coding transcriptome as
cellular context (Fig. 3c). For non-coding targets detected in at least 20% of cells, mean all-cell
PCCs reached 0.285 in PBMCs and 0.265 in developing brain. Among cells with detected target
expression, conditional intensity PCCs reached 0.716 and 0.577, respectively, exceeding scGPT,
scLong and CellFM33 on the corresponding targets (Fig. 3d). Recovered expression profiles
retained population-associated patterns for selected non-coding transcripts, including LINC00861,
LINC00926 and NEAT1 in PBMCs, and MEG3, MIAT and SOX2-OT in developing brain (Fig. 3e).
Beyond the shared accuracy benchmarks, DNA-derived identities enabled predictions for 18,502
PBMC and 26,113 brain non-coding RNA targets, substantially extending the molecular scope of
the decoder (Supplementary Fig. S.7).
We further investigated whether limited reference measurements could refine these predictions.
In a separate reference-adaptation evaluation using fixed sets of 108 PBMC and 323 brain non-
coding targets, adapting the native decoder with 500 reference cells increased mean all-cell
PCC from 0.190 to 0.255 and from 0.174 to 0.206, respectively (Fig. 3f). Conditional intensity
prediction also exceeded matched-reference Ridge regression and nearest-neighbour baselines,
with PCCs of 0.669 and 0.642 (Fig. 3g). Increasing reference size yielded further improvements
(Supplementary Fig. S.8). CellNet can use observed cellular expression to predict coding and
non-coding targets, with limited reference data further refining gene-level inference.
Extending expression inference across species and assays
The genes available for molecular analysis vary across organisms34, while experimental technolo-
gies measure different portions of the transcriptome35. We therefore investigated whether CellNet
could extend gene-level prediction across species, including genes absent from expression
pretraining, and recover expression patterns under distinct measurement conditions.
Across six nonhuman species from the spermatogenesis atlas36, frozen CellNet achieved
measured-positive expression-intensity PCCs of 0.672–0.767, compared with 0.093–0.179 for
scPRINT-2 (Fig. 3h). Adaptation with 500 within-species reference cells yielded conditional
intensity PCCs of 0.681–0.772. We further evaluated genes without expression-pretraining
exposure in bonobo, mouse and chicken, obtaining frozen conditional intensity PCCs of 0.696,
0.718 and 0.671, respectively (Fig. 3i). These targets retained DNA-derived identities despite
lacking expression-pretraining exposure, allowing CellNet to generate predictions beyond the
gene targets used in its expression training. Complete-expression correlations, target coverage
and exposure-stratified results were evaluated separately (Supplementary Fig. S.9).
Targeted spatial transcriptomics offers an additional test of expression prediction37. Because
these assays use limited gene panels38, accurate inference of genes of interest is particularly
valuable39. We examined whether CellNet could recover such molecular information using
a Xenium mouse-brain section40, jointly withholding 20 predefined genes from 12,000 cells
while retaining the remaining measured genes as input. Without spatial coordinates, frozen
CellNet reproduced characteristic spatial expression patterns for Slc17a7, Gad1, Aqp4 and Mog,
corresponding to major neuronal and glial populations (Fig. 3j). Across all 20 targets, mean
all-cell PCC reached 0.478 and increased to 0.665 following same-section reference adaptation
8


(Supplementary Fig. S.10). Even when the observed panel was reduced to 1,000 genes, adapted
predictions retained a mean PCC of 0.606 (Supplementary Fig. S.11a). Measured-positive
comparisons and additional marker maps supported recovery across panel sizes and neuronal,
glial, myeloid and vascular populations (Supplementary Fig. S.11b,c).
These evaluations show that CellNet can infer gene expression from incomplete molecular
observations across diverse gene repertoires and assays. By extending prediction beyond
the transcripts supplied as input, panoramic modelling offers a means to investigate additional
molecular features in existing single-cell datasets.
Distinct RNA inputs inform coding and non-coding gene predictions
Predicting the expression of unmeasured genes allows researchers to investigate transcripts
beyond those captured by an assay. Understanding which measured RNAs contribute to these
predictions may also help identify the cellular programmes associated with genes of interest41,42.
We therefore examined the relationships learned by CellNet between observed RNA expression
and predictions for coding and non-coding genes.
We first examined the biological organization of the learned gene representations. A network
constructed from 3,279 genes in 126 monocytes contained eight communities enriched for pro-
cesses including translation, RNA splicing and chromatin modification (Fig. 4a and Supplementary
Fig. S.12). The network also linked the MHC-II genes HLA-DRA and HLA-DPA1, whose measured
expression was correlated (r = 0.552). We then traced predictions of the protein-coding gene
CST3 and long non-coding RNA NEAT1, withholding both targets in 24 cells spanning monocyte,
T-cell, B-cell and natural killer-cell populations. Integrated-gradient attribution43 mapped their
predictions to measured RNA inputs (Fig. 4b,c). Even within the same monocyte image, CST3
predictions emphasized CD74, HLA-DRA, HLA-DPA1, IFI30 and HLA-DMB, whereas NEAT1
predictions emphasized DUSP1, FOS, IER2 and ZFP36. These patterns are consistent with
reported links between cystatin C and MHC-II processing44, as well as the involvement of NEAT1
and the identified response-associated genes in stress-related programmes45,46. Contributions to
NEAT1 predictions also varied across cell populations (Supplementary Fig. S.13).
To test whether these attributed RNAs contributed to the predictions, we selectively removed
their expression from the input while preserving other information. Removing each target’s 32
strongest positive contributors reduced its predicted expression more than removing the other
target’s contributors or expression-matched random inputs (Fig. 4d). Relative to random removal,
the additional decreases averaged 0.165 log-expression units for CST3 and 0.406 for NEAT1, with
positive contrasts across all 24 cells. Removing the identified antigen-presentation and response-
associated gene sets similarly produced target-specific decreases of 0.096 and 0.029 log units
beyond matched random removal in six monocytes, with negligible cross-target effects (Fig. 4e
and Supplementary Fig. S.16). Additional input-removal controls are presented in Supplementary
Fig. S.17 and Section S.1.13. Related analyses linked CD4 T-cell polarization scores31 and cellular
representation features to established expression programmes47 (Supplementary Figs. S.18 and
S.19).
These analyses show that CellNet organizes gene representations into biologically coherent
modules, uses distinct measured-RNA inputs to predict coding and non-coding targets, and
captures reproducible cell-state programmes in its cellular representations.

Non-coding RNA queries retrieve developmental and neural programmes
Many long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) show cell-type-specific expression and are detected in
only a subset of cells, potentially complicating direct expression-correlation analysis55. Because
CellNet defines gene identities independently of protein products, these RNAs can serve as
queries within the same representation used for coding genes. We investigated whether this
capability could recover coding-gene programmes associated with cellular development and
specialization.
In VASA-seq mouse embryos6, the non-coding RNA Foxl2os retrieved mesenchymal-
programme genes including Foxd1 and Eya1; C130021I20Rik retrieved neural/ectodermal genes
including En1; and Gm50321 retrieved early vascular-lineage genes including Igf2, Frem1 and
Itga8 (Fig. 5j). Full-ranking AP exceeded RNA correlation for all three queries, reaching 0.132
versus 0.018 for the vascular programme (Supplementary Fig. S.21c,d).
We further examined SERTAD4-AS1 in human neural induction56. Although detected in
only 13 of 3,423 non-targeting control cells, CellNet retrieved the published neural stem-cell
programme genes WNT5B, DLK1 and SERPINF1 among its top 20 neighbours (Fig. 5k). Using
all control cells as expression context, CellNet achieved an AP of 0.122, compared with 0.034 for
RNA correlation. Panoramic modelling makes sparsely measured non-coding RNAs accessible
to gene-programme analysis, extending biological interpretation beyond the molecules routinely
represented by protein-centred models.

DISCUSSION
CellNet combines DNA-derived gene identities with image-based transcriptome representations
to place diverse RNA molecules in a shared cellular context while retaining gene-level representa-
tions. It improves recognition of rare populations and condition-associated states and performs
competitively in dataset integration and cross-species evaluations. These results show that broad
molecular coverage can coexist with useful cellular representations across tissues and biological
conditions.
A key application of panoramic transcriptome modelling is predicting genes omitted from
individual experiments. CellNet predicts withheld expression from partial transcriptomes, including
selected non-coding RNAs and targets without expression-pretraining exposure, and recovers
spatial expression patterns for genes outside targeted measurement panels. Its gene representa-
tions also recover established molecular modules and retrieve coding-gene programmes using
non-coding RNA queries. For example, the sparsely detected SERTAD4-AS1 retrieved genes
from a published neural stem-cell programme. These results make underrepresented RNAs
more accessible to biological analysis and provide hypotheses about their expression and cellular
associations.
The shared representation also provides opportunities to investigate the molecular information
underlying model predictions. Input-attribution and targeted-removal analyses identified distinct
sets of measured RNAs contributing to predictions of different coding and non-coding targets. Cell-
contextual gene representations further recovered known partners and coordinated programmes
across diverse cellular backgrounds.
Computational tractability is essential for extending molecular coverage at scale. By organizing
multiple genes within image patches, CellNet avoids assigning a separate global token to every
gene. Multiview latent learning captures shared information across incomplete observations, while
gene-conditioned expression prediction retains gene-level information. This design supported
pretraining on 231 million cell profiles spanning 40 species using eight NVIDIA A800 GPUs over
approximately two weeks.
Limitations of the study
Prediction accuracy nevertheless varies across molecular classes, particularly for sparsely de-
tected non-coding transcripts, and inferred associations require independent functional validation.
As single-cell profiling continues to expand, more comprehensive RNA measurements and pertur-
bation datasets will offer opportunities to refine these predictions and investigate their regulatory
significance. CellNet provides a scalable framework for leveraging this growing molecular in-
formation to characterize cellular diversity and explore the gene programmes associated with
specialized functions.

STAR METHODS
Method details
Overview and pretraining collection
CellNet combines DNA-derived gene identities with image-based expression encoding to learn
cellular representations and gene-level predictions within a shared model. Each transcriptome is
represented by an expression image and a measurement mask. A transformer encodes these
inputs into cellular context, which a gene-conditioned decoder uses to predict specified transcripts
and generate contextual gene representations.
The main pretraining collection comprised 231,190,557 cell profiles from 26,467 datasets
spanning 40 species. Genes were indexed using species-aware identifiers, and the model’s
molecular representation contained 1,015,163 genes. Expression processing, sampling and
optimization are described in Supplementary Methods S.2.4-S.2.9.
DNA-informed transcriptome images
We represented gene identity using nucleotide sequence features from Evo 215. For gene g,
sequence features were averaged over nucleotide positions, yielding a 1,920-dimensional vector
eg. A frozen principal-component transform provided the 256-dimensional representation ag used
for trainable adaptation:
eg = Pool(fDNA(sg)) ,
ag = PT(eg −µ).
(1)
Here, sg denotes the gene-associated nucleotide sequence, fDNA the pretrained sequence
encoder, and P and µ the fixed projection basis and centring vector. The sequence features
remained frozen during transcriptome pretraining, while downstream identity projections were
learned jointly with the cellular encoder.
A fixed, species-specific layout was derived from the DNA sequence features by principal-
component projection followed by t-SNE. The resulting coordinates were scaled to the 384 × 384
canvas and assigned to distinct pixels using balanced greedy k-d tree assignment20.
The
assignment distributed genes across 16 × 16 patches while retaining one gene per pixel. For
cell c, normalized expression xcg was placed at the pixel assigned to gene g. A corresponding
14


indicator mcg recorded whether the gene was measured by the assay. The same layout was used
across cells, retaining measured zero-expression genes and accommodating protein-coding and
non-coding identities through the sequence-based interface.
The image was divided into non-overlapping 16 × 16 patches. Image organization allowed
multiple genes to contribute to each contextual token, providing a bounded patch grid as molecular
coverage increased.

Cellular and molecular readouts
The pretrained encoder remained frozen for cellular classification, integration and reference
mapping. Molecular association analyses used the frozen gene decoder. Expression completion
compared frozen predictions with separately specified reference-based decoder adaptation
(Supplementary Methods S.2.11). Cellular classification used layer normalization of the sum
of the final eight raw cellular summary states. Shrinkage linear discriminant analysis (LDA)
was the default cellular classification readout for CellNet, motivated by the isotropic Gaussian
regularization used during representation learning. The six comparison models used logistic
regression, a conventional linear probing approach in single-cell representation evaluation60.
16


These heads were fitted using training labels, with feature standardization restricted to the
corresponding training partition. The Gaussian regularizer acted in the pretraining projection
space; downstream LDA estimated class means and a shared covariance from the encoder
features.
Integration used the raw cellular summary from the sixth block counted from the encoder
output. Reference mapping used the eighth block counted from the output and a shared distance-
weighted nearest-neighbour classifier. These readouts and evaluation partitions are specified in
Supplementary Methods S.2.10.

observed genes. Withheld targets were absent from encoder expression, gene identities and mea-

all query cells. Conditional intensity used Mcg and was scored separately on measured-positive
observations. Total-RNA, multispecies and spatial protocols are described in Supplementary

Supplementary Information
S.1
Supplementary evidence
S.1.1
Tissue-resolved cellular identity and AvgBIO
The tissue-level annotation matrix localizes the variation underlying the aggregate Tabula Sapiens
results (Supplementary Fig. S.1a). CellNet achieved a Macro-F1 of 0.537 in bone marrow,
compared with 0.469 for UCE, and 0.634 in liver, compared with 0.575 for TranscriptFormer.
AvgBIO was examined jointly with donor mixing across all 26 evaluated tissues (Supplementary
Fig. S.1b). Overall AvgBIO was 0.684 for CellNet and 0.680 for UCE; the corresponding batch-
mixing scores were 0.886 and 0.915. Tissue-level AvgBIO and batch-mixing results across all 26
tissues are shown in Supplementary Fig. S.1b,c; for example, lymph-node AvgBIO was 0.711 for
CellNet and 0.606 for UCE.
S.1.2
Species-resolved identity and directional reference mapping
Within-species identification retained all 32 task–species combinations, including 18 with strictly
refined labels and 14 with native labels at coarse resolution (Supplementary Fig. S.2a). CellNet
had the highest mean score in all seven immune species. Fine-label F1 in Ciona intestinalis
was 0.780, compared with 0.555 for TranscriptFormer; fine-label germline recognition reached
0.895 in human and 0.951 in mouse. Brassica multi-tissue fine-label Macro-F1 averaged 0.247
for CellNet, 0.233 for UCE, 0.321 for TranscriptFormer and 0.325 for scPRINT-2. The five-species
root-atlas and GSE268881 variants yielded CellNet means of 0.671 and 0.671; these variants
shared biological cells and represented different task-specific feature caches.
Directional reference mapping evaluated coarse labels separately for the complete immune,
germline and three plant tasks (Supplementary Fig. S.2b–f). CellNet’s mean F1 over evaluable
directions was 0.229 for immune and 0.552 for germline transfer; UCE and TranscriptFormer
scored higher in these animal tasks. Across the three plant variants, CellNet achieved the highest
directional means among the four methods: 0.069 for multi-tissue Brassica, 0.215 for the root
atlas and 0.220 for GSE268881. Results are shown by reference and target species. Of 42
possible immune directions, 22 had shared evaluable labels; all germline and plant directions were
evaluable. Within-species and directional transfer used different split and k-nearest-neighbour
protocols.
S.1.3
Kidney and lung annotation and rare-population detection
The kidney and lung evaluations retained all 57 and 59 author-defined fine labels, respectively
(Supplementary Fig. S.3a). CellNet achieved complete-label Macro-F1 of 0.647 in kidney and
0.635 in lung, compared with 0.640 and 0.620 for TranscriptFormer. Rare-population detection
was then resolved into precision and recall using the same held-out predictions (Supplementary
Fig. S.3b,c). In kidney, CellNet’s macro precision and recall were 0.632 and 0.697; scGPT had
higher precision at 0.670, whereas CellNet had the highest recall. In lung, CellNet led both
endpoints, with precision of 0.803 and recall of 0.777. The precision–recall plots show how
rare-cell recovery contributed to the F1 results, with enlarged insets distinguishing methods with
similar mean values.

S.1.4
Class-resolved rare-population performance and error outcomes
Class-resolved analysis retained every eligible identity, together with cell abundance, held-out
support, donor coverage and eligible-fold count (Supplementary Fig. S.4a). Kidney mast cells
(MAST) constituted 0.026% of the cohort and achieved CellNet F1 of 0.658, compared with 0.609
for Geneformer V2. Kidney parietal epithelial cells (PEC) achieved F1 of 0.622, compared with
0.513 for TranscriptFormer. Lung myofibroblasts comprised 300 cells and achieved F1 of 0.806,
compared with 0.769 for Geneformer V2.
Pooling eligible rare truth cells across test folds, CellNet correctly assigned 61.8% of kidney
cells and 73.1% of lung cells (Supplementary Fig. S.4b,c). The highest comparator proportions
were 53.8% for scGPT in kidney and 66.6% for Geneformer V2 in lung. These cell-weighted
proportions describe correct identities and error destinations; class-macro performance is reported
in Fig. 2d,e. A projection of all 69,650 lung cells locates the 17 eligible author identities within the
frozen CellNet representation (Supplementary Fig. S.4d).
S.1.5
Condition recognition across donors and libraries
Donor-resolved CD4 evaluation yielded CellNet Macro-F1 values of 0.935, 0.941, 0.926 and 0.943,
leading the comparison methods in every donor holdout (Supplementary Fig. S.5a). CellNet also
had the lowest pooled error percentage for each directed condition pair (Supplementary Fig. S.5b).
For example, 6.2% of Th0 cells were assigned to Th2, compared with 9.5% for TranscriptFormer.
WI-38 accuracy was highest for CellNet in five of six held-out libraries (Supplementary Fig. S.5c),
with values ranging from 0.854 to 0.992 across both author culture replicates of the three
conditions.
Measured NEAT1 expression provided a non-coding-transcript correlate of the CD4 conditions
(Supplementary Fig. S.5d). Mean log1p-normalized expression followed Th17 > Th0 > Th2 in all
four donors. Th17–Th0 differences ranged from 0.022 to 0.388, and Th2–Th0 differences ranged
from −0.332 to −0.137. These measurements describe expression associated with the author-
defined conditions; classification labels were taken from the original experimental metadata.

S.1.6
Testable coding outputs across target prevalence
Coding threshold curves evaluated both all-cell recovery and intensity among measured-positive
cells (Supplementary Fig. S.6a,b). All twelve prescribed immune markers were retained without a
detection cutoff, testing lineage-associated predictions beyond the representative heatmap. Their
CellNet PCCs ranged from 0.547 to 0.880 and exceeded both recorded comparators. Measured
detection ranged from 1.92% to 53.92% (Supplementary Fig. S.6c).
S.1.7
Non-coding identities as prediction targets
Measured-positive threshold curves and transcript-class summaries tested expression output
across ncRNA prevalence and classes (Supplementary Fig. S.7a,b). The DNA-identity extension
supplied actual predictions for every annotated assay ncRNA while preserving the original
accuracy pools (Supplementary Fig. S.7c). The two analyses established predictive accuracy on
shared targets and output availability across the assay’s wider molecular repertoire.
S.1.8
Refinement of molecular predictions with reference expression
Four matched reference sizes tested whether additional cellular examples improved ncRNA
outputs on fixed target pools (Supplementary Fig. S.8). At full reference, all-cell PCC reached
0.333 in PBMCs and 0.334 in brain. Measured-positive intensity PCC reached 0.698 and 0.689,
respectively, with an advantage over Ridge and nearest-neighbour prediction across all reference
sizes.
S.1.9
Prediction scope across gene identities and expression exposure
Frozen CellNet exceeded scPRINT-2 in mean all-cell PCC for both expression-pretraining-seen
and unseen bonobo and chicken targets (Supplementary Fig. S.9a). Species support counts
described the original registered target universes independently of accuracy (Supplementary
Fig. S.9b). Detection and vocabulary intersections yielded 2,108 bonobo, twelve mouse and 533
chicken unseen targets, specifying the evaluated extension beyond expression-training exposure
(Supplementary Fig. S.9c).
S.1.10
Spatial recovery across the prescribed marker repertoire
All twenty prescribed targets were evaluated with physical-block uncertainty estimates (Sup-
plementary Fig. S.10). Every marker’s all-cell PCC increased after same-section adaptation,
extending the quantitative evidence beyond the four frozen maps in the main figure. With 4,986
observed genes, adapted CellNet achieved mean all-cell PCC of 0.665, compared with 0.620 for
Ridge and 0.563 for nearest neighbours.
S.1.11
Prediction under restricted spatial measurement
With 1,000 observed genes, adaptation increased mean all-cell PCC from 0.166 to 0.606 (Supple-
mentary Fig. S.11a). Measured-positive intensity PCC reached 0.831 with 4,986 observed genes
and 0.792 with 1,000, exceeding both supervised comparators (Supplementary Fig. S.11b). Maps
of Pvalb, Pdgfra, Aif1 and Pecam1 tested recovery of additional population-associated spatial
patterns (Supplementary Fig. S.11c). These analyses assessed dependence on measured input
breadth and biological coverage within the same tissue section.

S.1.12
Biological organization of gene representations
The network used 126 CD14-positive monocytes with observed RNA available. Its 3,279 query
genes were selected before network analysis. The preparation cohort contained 126 platelets
and 300 cells each from naive B, naive T and monocyte populations. We combined the 2,048
protein-coding and 512 other annotated genes with the highest detection rates across these 1,026
cells, all 1,625 supported coding genes detected in at least seven platelets, and 83 prespecified
platelet-reference and query genes. Removing duplicates and retaining supported, unambiguous
gene mappings yielded the 3,279-gene panel. The 512-gene annotated group was not restricted
to non-coding RNA. The network queries did not restrict the full measured-RNA input.
Gene vectors were averaged across monocytes, centred across the query panel and normal-
ized to unit length. Connecting each gene to its 15 highest positive-cosine neighbours, retaining a
link selected in either direction, produced 36,638 links and eight weighted Leiden communities.
All eight communities showed Reactome enrichment after global Benjamini–Hochberg correction
over 10,720 tests against the 3,279-gene background (Supplementary Fig. S.12). Representative
enrichments included translation elongation (q = 6.28×10−79) and mRNA splicing (q = 2.64×10−9).
The HLA-DRA–HLA-DPA1 link joined curated MHC-II pathway members with measured RNA
r = 0.552. Across the network, 3.96% of links had RNA r ≥0.30, compared with 0.38% of
random non-edge partners matched to the source gene. These analyses characterize biological
organization of the representations. Prediction attribution used the separate hidden-target design
below.
S.1.13
Target-specific input evidence and removal controls
The prediction analysis used 24 cells from one SSTX PBMC library, six each from monocyte, T-cell,
B-cell and natural killer-cell groups. Within each population, cells were selected at equally spaced
ranks spanning the 55th–90th percentiles of summed original CST3 and NEAT1 predictions. The
entire 7,302-gene target partition, including both targets, was withheld from input expression,
measurement indicators and input gene identities; 29,197 measured genes supplied the image.
Expression was log-transformed after normalization to 10,000 observed-gene counts per cell. The
monocyte displayed in Fig. 4c was subsequently selected for measured targets and the strongest
combined two-target removal contrast among the selected monocytes. The four NEAT1 examples
in Supplementary Fig. S.13 required detected targets and absolute prediction errors no greater
than 0.4 log units, and were selected by the largest removal contrast within each group, excluding
the main monocyte. All 24 cells contributed to the summaries and complete map collections.
Integrated gradients43 followed a 24-step path from zero to observed expression, with measure-
ment indicators, gene identities and detection information fixed. Signed gene contributions were
summed within 16 × 16 image patches. In Supplementary Fig. S.13, full signed gene-contribution
vectors were more similar within than between monocyte, B-cell and natural killer-cell groups;
the T-cell contrast was approximately zero. The complete CST3 and NEAT1 collections use a
common scale across all 48 cell/target maps (Supplementary Figs. S.14 and S.15).
Expression-only removal set selected input values to zero while retaining measurement
information, gene identity and the original normalization. Eight random sets were averaged within
each cell before comparison. Complete hiding additionally removed measurement, identity and
detection information. For the 32-gene sets, target-selected removal exceeded random removal
in every cell under complete hiding; patch-removal contrasts were positive in 23 or 24 of 24
cells, depending on target and removal protocol (Supplementary Fig. S.17). Summed integrated
gradients approximated the prediction change from the zero-expression reference with a median
relative residual of 1.18%. The maximum residual was 98.15%, with large relative errors in
low-contrast CST3 cases. The figure retains all residuals and expression-matching diagnostics.

S.1.14
Cell-state evidence across donors and expression-associated fea-
tures
For the Th17–Th2 analysis, a linear discriminant classifier was trained on three donors and applied
to the fourth, rotating across four donors. Attribution explained each held-out cell’s Th17-minus-
Th2 log-posterior score. Gradient multiplied by input expression gave signed contributions; positive
values favoured Th17. The 96 attribution cells comprised 12 cells per state and donor. Within-
state donor-mean gene contributions had Spearman correlations of 0.717–0.752 (Supplementary
Fig. S.18). The rotating classifiers shared training donors. Two displayed examples were selected
retrospectively from correctly classified cells by the largest existing patch-removal contrasts.
Removing the 10% strongest supporting patches was compared with random removal of the
same patch count. Of six random sets per cell, only sets matching removed expression mass and
detected-gene count within 10% were averaged. This strict matching yielded 38 eligible cells, all
Th17, and each showed a larger score decrease after targeted removal. Ribosomal genes were
prominent among the classifier’s contributing inputs.
Separately, six-component sparse nonnegative matrix factorization was fitted to cell represen-
tations from 2,700 cells sampled across donors D1–D3 and Th0, Th17 and Th2 conditions, then
applied to all 9,777 cells. D4 was held out from fitting and gene ranking. Four selected features
were associated with DNA replication, mitosis, endoplasmic-reticulum stress and interferon pro-
grammes (Supplementary Fig. S.19). The eight genes per feature with the highest within-donor
expression correlations in D1–D3 were drawn from genes detected in at least 488 cells. All 32
selected correlations remained positive in D4, and the training-defined high/low state polarity
persisted across all four donors. Feature activations also covaried with total RNA counts, as
shown for each feature.
S.1.15
Module and partner retrieval across RNA classes
Module recovery used a 1,020-gene query panel whose expression and measurement indicators
were withheld from the input. Thirty reference genes were excluded from the 343 ranked non-
coding candidates, which contained 117 additional published members and 226 candidates
outside the member list. Scores averaged candidate-to-reference model cosine similarities or
absolute RNA Pearson correlations. Average precision sums precision over the complete ranking,
weighted by each increase in reference-member recall. Across naive B, naive T and monocyte
populations, it was 0.601, 0.611 and 0.624 for CellNet, compared with 0.275, 0.341 and 0.325 for
RNA correlation. Module membership was defined by the published TotalX coexpression analysis
from the same resource8.
Other neighbourhood and programme analyses retained measured query RNA. Population-
mean gene vectors were centred across the analysed gene panel and normalized before cosine
scoring; query self-matches were excluded. RNA comparators used absolute Pearson correlation
of log-transformed expression in the same cells, including measured zeros. These rankings
measured associations between representations. TCP1 retrieved CCT4, CCT5, CCT2, CCT6A

and CCT3 at ranks 1–5 in both HEK293T and untreated Huh7, with CCT7 at rank 17 or 9,
respectively (Supplementary Fig. S.20a,b). RNA correlation retrieved no CCT partners in either
top 20. In the external PBMC cohort, HLA-DRA’s partners CD74, HLA-DPB1 and HLA-DPA1
retained ranks 4, 7 and 11. The complete discovery lists include recurrent and non-recurrent
neighbours (Supplementary Fig. S.20c,d).
The four cell-context examples were selected after an exploratory screen, including compar-
ison with baseline ranks. Each primary population contained 126 cells with matched protocol
composition: eight 10X-dUTSO, 74 SSTX and 44 SSTX-pA+ cells. All used the same 2,736
coding candidates. Their observed similarities ranged from 0.211 to 0.369, compared with −0.088
to 0.034 after a single within-cell expression-value shuffle (Supplementary Fig. S.20e). Paired,
protocol-stratified cell bootstrap intervals used 1,000 resamples of the observed and shuffled
populations and describe that cohort and shuffle realization. All four partners remained within the
top 20 in both disjoint 63-cell halves. Support across sequencing protocols varied: MALAT1–AFF3
ranked 27 in SSTX-pA+, while its half-cell ranks were 17 and 15 and its expanded-population
rank was 15 (Supplementary Fig. S.20f).
S.1.16
Host programme organization and non-coding entry points
The Huh7 analysis contained 500 untreated and 500 DENV-exposed cells. Host-expression
scores assigned 267 exposed cells to an active-like group and 233 to a quiescent-like group
(Supplementary Fig. S.21a,b). Scores averaged gene-wise standardized log-expression within
predefined signatures; standardization used all 1,000 cells. Five scores defined the exposed-cell
annotations, and the stress-granule score supplied additional expression context. Each displayed
programme had a higher mean within-programme cosine than the mean of 200 size-matched
random gene sets. The gene cosine and RNA Pearson scores in Fig. 5i summarize programme
organization within their respective association spaces.
Developmental references came from the published VASA-seq combined-stage marker table6.
Coding markers with adjusted P < 0.01 and log fold change greater than 1 were ranked by the
published marker score, retaining up to 60 genes before measurement-support filtering. The
Foxl2os, C130021I20Rik and Gm50321 analyses contained 60 of 2,149, 60 of 2,152 and 46
of 2,117 reference members/candidates, respectively. Coding candidates required detection
in at least max(5, ⌈0.05N⌉) of the context’s N cells; non-coding queries required at least five
RNA-positive cells. All cells contributed, including those with zero measured query expression.
VASA-seq contributed to pretraining; these analyses evaluated recovery of known programmes in
the specified developmental contexts.
The complete developmental top-20 lists contained eight, seven and three reference mem-
bers, compared with six, four and zero for RNA correlation (Supplementary Fig. S.21c). Each
method used identical cells and fixed full-population candidate and reference sets in the dis-
joint half-population comparisons. CellNet retained higher average precision in all six halves
(Supplementary Fig. S.21d).
The human neural analysis used the published neural stem-cell state-12 marker programme56.
Coding markers with adjusted P < 0.01 and average log2 fold change greater than 0.25 were
ranked by the published fold change, retaining the top 60 before coverage filtering. The eligible
reference contained 59 genes among 2,082 coding candidates. All 3,423 verified non-targeting
controls contributed to both methods’ associations; 13 had measured SERTAD4-AS1. WNT5B,
DLK1 and SERPINF1 were recovered at ranks 7, 10 and 17. The original perturbation study
supplied the programme reference; this retrieval analysis used its control cells.

S.2
Supplementary Methods
S.2.1
Datasets
We used a public pretraining collection and fifteen public datasets or dataset collections spanning
human tissues, cell lines, vertebrate and invertebrate cell atlases, plant tissues and spatial
transcriptomics. Table S.1 summarizes the datasets and adopted cohort sizes; their sources,
biological material and measurement details are described below. Species-level composition
of the multispecies atlases is provided in Table S.2. Original donor, sample, cell-type and
experimental-condition annotations were retained. Data partitioning, analysis-specific sampling
and evaluation procedures are described in S.2.10–S.2.14 and S.2.3.
Main pretraining collection. The main collection contained 231,190,557 cell or nucleus
profiles from 26,467 constituent expression-matrix entries, representing 40 source species desig-
nations, 1,015,163 species-qualified gene identities and 2,233 harmonized cell-type categories.
Data were obtained from GEO, ArrayExpress, BioProject-associated repositories and CELLx-
GENE Discover, a curated and interoperable single-cell data platform61,62. Constituent entries
denote expression matrices rather than independent publications or biological replicates. Source
quality control and measured-gene annotations were retained; expression normalization and
species-aware gene harmonization are described in S.2.4-S.2.6.
Tabula Sapiens 2.0. We used the Tabula Sapiens 2.0 human multi-organ atlas, retaining
source tissue, donor and cell-type annotations. The dataset is available from Figshare. The
adopted collections comprised a 692,102-profile matrix and a 549,018-profile labelled collection
from nine donors; nine profiles without valid labels were excluded from the latter. These numbers
describe the supplied subsets used here rather than the complete atlas. Eligibility of tissues and
labelled cells is specified in S.2.10.
Human kidney atlas. The atlas of healthy and injured human kidney was obtained from the
corresponding CELLxGENE collection. The adopted cohort contained 107,344 profiles from 45
donors and 57 author-provided cell labels. Donor identities and injury annotations were retained.
Proximal-tubule subsets were drawn from this same atlas.
Human lung atlas. We used the Travaglini et al. human lung atlas, available from its
CELLxGENE collection, combining its 10x and Smart-seq2 lung transcriptomes. After excluding
blood samples, the adopted cohort comprised 69,650 cells from three donors with 59 author-
provided labels. The original donor identities and cell annotations were retained.
Cano-Gamez CD4 T-cell dataset. The Cano-Gamez et al. CD4 T-cell dataset was obtained
from BioStudies accession S-BSST2978. We retained the author-defined Naive starting population
in 10x technical batch 1 under Th0, Th2 and Th17 conditions. The resulting collection contained
9,777 cells from four donors and 20,953 measured genes. Condition labels were taken from the
experimental metadata.
WI-38 senescence dataset.
The WI-38 fibroblast transcriptomes were obtained from
GSE226225. Six 10x libraries, GSM7068355–GSM7068360, represented replicative senescence,
irradiation-associated senescence and etoposide-associated senescence, with two libraries per
condition. The adopted matrix contained 14,457 cells and 36,601 measured genes after the
quality-control criteria described in S.2.10. Library replicates were derived from the same human
cell line.
Circulating immune-cell atlas. Author-annotated circulating immune-cell matrices were
obtained from the seven-species Figshare collection 28008611. Species comprised human,
rhesus macaque, Chinese softshell turtle, Pacific white shrimp, Ciona intestinalis, half-smooth
tongue sole and whitespotted bamboo shark. The adopted labelled cohort contained 19,727
profiles in total (Table S.2). The human matrix was supplied as counts per million without raw
counts and retained its source expression convention.
51


Spermatogenesis atlas. We used the single-nucleus transcriptomes from36, The molecular
evolution of spermatogenesis across mammals, with data available through the author portal.
The adopted atlas encompassed human, chimpanzee, bonobo, gorilla, gibbon, rhesus macaque,
common marmoset, mouse, opossum, platypus and chicken, with 96,464 labelled nuclei in total
(Table S.2). Original cell annotations were retained. Gibbon is reported using the established
common species designation for the analysed collection.
Brassicaceae multi-tissue atlas. The adopted author-annotated matrices, deposited under
GEO accession GSE297576, contained 86,015 profiles from Arabidopsis thaliana, Arabidopsis
lyrata, Brassica oleracea and Capsella rubella (Table S.2). Source tissue and sample annotations
were retained, together with the species-specific cell labels and shared coarse labels supplied
with the matrices.
Brassicaceae root atlas. Root transcriptomes were obtained from GSE268881. The adopted
collection contained 184,951 profiles from Arabidopsis thaliana, Camelina sativa, Eutrema sal-
sugineum, Schrenkiella parvula and Sisymbrium irio, spanning control and author-defined stress
conditions. Source condition, replicate and cell-type annotations were retained. The root atlas
and its accession refer to the same underlying collection.
TotalX dataset collection. The TotalX study provided human PBMC, developing-brain,
HEK293T and Huh7 total-RNA transcriptomes.
Processed matrices and annotations were
obtained from Figshare 29803628. Raw brain and PBMC sequencing data were deposited
under Synapse accession syn72382564, and cell-line sequencing data under GSE315939. The
PBMC and brain source matrices contained 58,467 and 301,515 profiles, respectively. PBMC
assay groups 10X-dUTSO, SSTX and SSTX-pA+ contained 19,431, 18,497 and 20,539 profiles.
These are technical capture groups rather than distinct biological conditions. Cell-line collections
included untreated and author-defined DENV-exposed Huh7 profiles. Original cell-type, sample
and assay annotations were retained.
Xenium Prime 5K mouse-brain dataset. We used the public 10x Xenium Prime fresh-frozen
mouse-brain dataset40, comprising one coronal section with 63,173 cells and 5,006 assayed
genes. Direct gene measurements, cell annotations and original micrometre coordinates were
retained. Query and reference subsets from this single section are specified in S.2.11.
Purified PBMC dataset. The Zheng et al. purified human PBMC collections51 were obtained
in the processed form distributed through scvi-tools. The supplied matrix contained 105,868
cells, 21,932 measured genes and ten annotated populations. Original population and sample
annotations were retained.
Perirhinal cortex dataset. Human perirhinal-cortex nuclear transcriptomes were obtained
from the Human Brain Cell Atlas v1.0 collection, using the processed release distributed with
the scGPT datasets. The source matrix represented the rostral subdivision of perirhinal area 35
and contained 17,535 nuclei, 59,357 measured genes, ten annotated cell types and two sample
groups.
VASA-seq developmental dataset. Mouse developmental total-RNA transcriptomes were
obtained from the VASA-seq study, accession GSE176588. The adopted profiles spanned em-
bryonic stages E6.5, E7.5 and E8.5, retaining author-provided lineage and developmental-stage
annotations. Published coding-gene programmes were obtained from the original Supplementary
Table 5. This source contributed to pretraining; the developmental programme analyses are
described in S.2.14.
Human neural-induction dataset. We used the Wu et al. human neural-induction study,
accession GSE150062. The adopted transcriptomes comprised all 3,423 author-verified non-
targeting control cells. Source log-transformed expression and published neural-state marker
annotations were retained. The non-targeting controls supplied observed transcriptomes; the
original study supplied the programme reference.

S.2.2
Third-party comparison methods
Model releases and task scope. Released pretrained models and their original inference
procedures supplied frozen cell representations or native expression predictions. Gene mapping
followed each model’s supported identities and species conventions. Unsupported species or
genes had unavailable predictions rather than zero scores. Geneformer V2, scGPT, scLong,
scPRINT-2, TranscriptFormer and UCE formed the six cellular comparators in Fig. 2. Expression
comparisons in Fig. 3 additionally included CellFM. The model subset varied with the task and
native output support.
Geneformer V2. The V2-104M release63 used Ensembl-mapped expression and the authors’
rank-value tokenization. Its frozen 768-dimensional CLS representation used the official second-
to-last-layer extraction setting. Downstream labels trained only the classifier described in S.2.10.
scGPT. The whole-human release12 used its supported vocabulary and official binning. Cell
analysis used 512-dimensional CLS features and maximum sequence length 1,200. Completion
used 51 expression bins and at most 600 nonzero visible-context genes. Hidden expression was
excluded from bin construction. Native predictions were inverse-binned through visible-expression
boundaries to log1p counts per 10,000 without query-truth calibration.
scLong. The released model25 retained the fixed 27,000-gene axis used for these analyses.
Cell features mean-pooled merged gene decodings, excluding the terminal padding position.
Completion retained the frozen native expression-regression head. Missing expression on the
fixed gene axis followed the authors’ input convention; target measurements were excluded from
visible-context normalization and input.
scPRINT-2. The small-v2 release27 used official gene encoding, positional encoding, ex-
pression preprocessing and generative inference. Cell analysis used the 256-dimensional base
summary representation, at most 1,200 context genes and the released expression-dependent
sampling procedure. Expression inference used visible raw counts and explicit gene queries.
Human comparisons jointly normalized generated target counts and observed counts. Current
multispecies comparisons normalized predictions to observed-input library size only, with depth
multiplier one. With native intensity µ and zero-inflation logit z, complete output before normaliza-
54


tion was µ[1 −sigmoid(z)] and the intensity endpoint used µ. The latter was not interpreted as the
strict mean conditional on a positive observed count.
Gorilla, platypus and legacy chicken identities were added through the official frozen gene-
encoding and positional-encoding extension without updating pretrained weights. Bonobo assay
identifiers followed the chimpanzee namespace used by the comparison. Exact gene support
fixed multispecies target membership before reading new prediction scores; identifier conventions
did not change the biological species of the cells.
TranscriptFormer. TF-Metazoa14 used raw counts, supported protein-sequence-derived
identities and official inference. Its 2,048-dimensional cell representations supplied annotation,
integration and reference mapping without task-specific representation fitting. Species and gene
support were retained for each atlas.
UCE. The released 33-layer model13 used official raw-expression preprocessing and protein-
sequence-derived identities. Inference sampled 1,024 gene tokens with padded length 1,536
and returned 1,280-dimensional cell representations. Additional cell filtering was disabled to
preserve the common cohort; gene eligibility followed native vocabulary support. Features
supplied classification, integration and reference mapping.
CellFM. Completion used the released 800-million-parameter model33 and its original scalar
cell-wise expression decoder. Weights were frozen, with no new head or query-truth recalibration.
Human vocabulary support defined eligible targets; human identities were not substituted for
unsupported nonhuman identities. CellFM therefore entered human completion comparisons
without fabricated nonhuman scores.
Classification and mapping. Third-party cell representations used training-fold standard-
ization and logistic regression with C = 1, L-BFGS, at most 2,000 iterations, no class weighting.
CellNet used automatically regularized LDA, as in S.2.10. Reference mapping used a common
cosine kNN classifier for CellNet, scPRINT-2, TranscriptFormer and UCE, with identical partitions,
class-frequency correction and validation-selected settings.
Reference-supervised expression. Ridge used at most 512 high-variance visible genes
selected from reference cells, reference-fitted standardization and α = 100, with the target mean
as intercept. Cosine kNN used the same feature selection and ten neighbours. Expression
predictions were weighted averages of measured reference expression, with weights proportional
to nonnegative cosine similarity, normalized to sum to one; zero total similarity gave uniform
weights. Predictions were constrained to nonnegative log-expression values. Reference member-
ship matched the adapted decoder for each budget. These methods received target-expression
supervision from reference cells and were distinguished from frozen pretrained prediction.
Molecular associations. Expression comparison used absolute Pearson correlation over
the same selected cells, candidate genes and measured zeros. Static identity comparison used
cosine similarity between learned 64-dimensional gene-identity vectors. Published reference sets
and full eligible candidate rankings were shared. These comparisons measured co-expression or
context-independent identity similarity, respectively.
S.2.3
Evaluation metrics and statistical summaries
Classification. Accuracy was the fraction of correctly labelled query cells. For class k, precision
was Pk = TPk/(TPk+FPk) and recall was Rk = TPk/(TPk+FNk). Class F1 was 2PkRk/(Pk+Rk),
with undefined class contributions assigned zero. Macro-F1 averaged class F1 equally. Tabula
Sapiens tissue-conditioned evaluation used biological labels present in each true test fold, first
averaging donor folds within tissue and then weighting eighteen tissues equally. Global all-
class scores used their predefined union of true and predicted labels; seen-class results were
reported separately. Balanced accuracy averaged recall over true test classes. Experimental-
55


state classification reported macro-F1 and balanced accuracy averaged equally over held-out
donor/library folds; per-state scores, predicted probabilities and confusion matrices were retained.
Rare-cell F1 used labels with training prevalence at most 1%, at least twenty cohort cells
across at least three donors, and positive training and test support in the fold. Eligible labels could
differ by fold. Eligible-class F1 was averaged within each fold, then across the three donor-held-out
folds equally rather than pooling predictions. The separately reported 5% minority threshold
did not define the primary rare-cell endpoint. Cell counts and donor- or library-held-out scores
described distinct sampling and evaluation units.
Reference mapping. Within-species F1 used predefined fine labels; cross-species F1 used
shared coarse labels. Coverage was the fraction of all target-test cells with a true label present in
the source reference. F1 was evaluated on this eligible subset. Empty subsets retained coverage
but undefined F1. Within-species family means weighted species equally; cross-species means
weighted eligible ordered directions equally. Shared cross-species kNN settings were selected by
validation macro-F1, weighting the four comparison methods and five development task families
equally. SD across five overlapping splits described partition sensitivity rather than independent
biological replication.
Integration. Metrics followed scIB definitions28. NMI normalized mutual information between
graph clusters and cell labels by the arithmetic mean of their entropies. ARI corrected pairwise
cluster–label agreement for chance. Cell-label silhouette width was rescaled as (s + 1)/2. The
equal-weight mean of NMI, ARI and rescaled cell-type silhouette defined AvgBIO. Batch silhou-
ette used 1 −|sbatch| within labels, averaged within each label and then across eligible labels.
Graph connectivity averaged each label’s fraction of cells in its largest connected neighbour-
hood component. Their arithmetic mean defined batch performance. Higher values indicated
better conservation or mixing. Aggregate scores weighted eligible tissues equally. Metrics used
high-dimensional representations and graphs; UMAP supplied visualization64.

S.2.5
Sequence features, image mapping and augmentation
Gene-associated DNA sequences were matched to species-aware gene identifiers before feature
extraction. Sequence features were extracted from the 1-billion-parameter Evo 2 base model,
using the output of the third linear projection in the multilayer perceptron of block 20. Features
at valid nucleotide positions were mean-pooled; long sequences were encoded as overlapping
chunks, whose pooled features were then averaged to obtain one 1,920-dimensional vector
per gene. A frozen incremental principal-component transform reduced these vectors to 256
dimensions. Independent learned projections generated the 64-dimensional mean-identity and
expression-modulated identity features in equation (2).
For image mapping, DNA-derived gene vectors were first reduced to 64 dimensions by PCA
and then projected to two dimensions by t-SNE, using cosine distance, perplexity 30, PCA
initialization and an automatic learning rate65. The coordinates were scaled to the image grid.
Balanced greedy k-d tree assignment placed genes at nearby available pixels while favouring less
occupied patches, producing a fixed, species-specific mapping with at most one gene per pixel.
For each cell, normalized expression and the assay measurement indicator were written at each
gene’s assigned pixel. Thus, measured zero-expression genes remained distinguishable from
genes not measured by the assay.
The expression branch used two channels, expression and visibility. The expression-modulated
branch used 64 identity channels, with δ = 0.02. Each branch projected to 768 dimensions. During
training, adjacent horizontal positions could be swapped within a patch. Patches were selected
with probability 0.5 and neighbouring pairs with probability 0.3. Gene identities and their expression
values moved together. The permutations were disabled at inference.
Each cell generated two global views with patch-mask fractions sampled from [0, 0.1], and six
local views with fractions from [0.35, 0.8]. Additional global/local augmentation parameters are
summarized in Table S.3.
