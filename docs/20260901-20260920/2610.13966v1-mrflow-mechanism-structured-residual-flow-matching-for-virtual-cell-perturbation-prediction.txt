Abstract
Predicting cellular responses to chemical and genetic perturbations is a central test
for AI virtual cells, yet many single-cell models reduce each condition to an average
expression shift. This target is misaligned with perturbation biology: rare states,
resistant subpopulations, and context-dependent effect sizes may coexist under the
same intervention. MRFlow treats virtual-cell prediction as conditional transport
between empirical single-cell measures. Control and perturbed cells are embedded
in a variational latent state space, greedy low-cost supervision is constructed over
detached minibatch states, and a conditional flow-matching vector field transports
control populations toward perturbed populations. The field decomposes into a
mechanism-structured affine dictionary, which recombines shared latent response
operators with a low-rank condition-specific correction, and a spectrally controlled
residual field for nonlinear cell-level variation. Genetic perturbations are encoded
through a regulatory-graph GCN, chemical perturbations through Morgan finger-
prints, and both enter the same conditional dynamics interface. Across eight gene
and drug perturbation benchmarks, MRFlow attains the highest composite score
while preserving competitive raw performance on differential-expression and dis-
tributional metrics. The results support latent distribution transport as a principled
and empirically effective formulation for perturbation-aware virtual cell modeling.

Introduction
Accurate prediction of single-cell responses to interventions is a concrete test of whether AI virtual
cells can move beyond transcriptomic annotation toward predictive perturbation modeling. Large
Perturb-seq and chemical transcriptomics screens now measure the transcriptional effects of genetic
and pharmacological perturbations at single-cell resolution [Dixit et al., 2016, Adamson et al., 2016,
Norman et al., 2019, Replogle et al., 2022, Srivatsan et al., 2020, McFarland et al., 2020], and recent
resources have harmonized these measurements for broader comparison [Peidli et al., 2024, Wei
et al., 2025]. These atlases provide the empirical basis for virtual cell models that anticipate held-out
perturbation conditions, guide functional genomics, and narrow the experimental search space in
drug discovery [Ji et al., 2021, Bunne et al., 2024a, Rood et al., 2024].
The dominant computational abstraction remains conditional response regression. scGen introduced
latent arithmetic for out-of-sample perturbation prediction [Lotfollahi et al., 2019]; CPA and chemCPA
extended conditional autoencoding to compositional treatments, dose response, and unseen drugs
[Lotfollahi et al., 2023, Hetzel et al., 2022]; GEARS and TxPert used graph-structured biological
knowledge to predict transcriptional effects of genetic perturbations [Roohani et al., 2024, Wenkel
et al., 2026]; and representation models such as biolord, scGPT, and scFoundation have been used for
perturbation prediction or disentangled response analysis [Piran et al., 2024, Cui et al., 2024, Hao
et al., 2024]. These systems have expanded the modeling toolkit, but the object they are often asked
to predict is still a condition-level mean transcriptional effect.
The mean-shift view misses a defining feature of single-cell perturbation biology. A drug or gene
perturbation induces a response distribution, in which resistant subpopulations, rare transcriptional
states, and cell-state-dependent effect sizes carry biologically meaningful signal [Replogle et al., 2022,
Srivatsan et al., 2020, Aissa et al., 2021]. A second limitation is exposed by recent benchmarks: simple
baselines, systematic variation, and drug-response evaluation context can all change the apparent
ranking of perturbation predictors [Ahlmann-Eltze et al., 2025, Wong et al., 2025, Viñas Torné et al.,
2025, Wei et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2026]. These results suggest that additional capacity alone is
an insufficient answer. A virtual cell model needs a hypothesis class that can generate response
distributions while controlling how perturbation dynamics extrapolate to new conditions.
Flow matching and optimal transport provide a mathematical framework for this distributional
problem. Flow matching trains continuous normalizing flows by regressing vector fields along
probability paths, and OT-guided variants reduce transport cost and path crossing through minibatch
couplings [Lipman et al., 2023, Liu et al., 2023, Tong et al., 2024]. In single-cell biology, optimal
transport has been used for developmental alignment, data integration, cellular dynamics, and
perturbation response modeling [Schiebinger et al., 2019, Cao et al., 2022, Tong et al., 2020, Bunne
et al., 2023, 2024b]. Standard flow models, however, do not by themselves address the unpaired
nature of perturbation data or the need for structured extrapolation across conditions.
MRFlow instantiates this view by learning perturbation-conditioned transport dynamics in a varia-
tional latent cell-state space, as outlined in Figure 1. The model maps a control-cell distribution to a
perturbed-cell distribution through OT-guided flow matching. Its vector field is not an unconstrained
neural network: the dominant component is a mechanism-structured affine dictionary, in which a
condition-specific matrix is assembled from shared response operators plus a low-rank correction. A
spectrally regularized residual field captures nonlinear cell-level variation that remains after the affine
component. Genetic and chemical interventions enter through modality-specific encoders, but both
parameterize the same latent dynamics. A negative binomial decoder maps transported latent states
back to count-aware expression distributions.
The empirical study spans eight human single-cell perturbation datasets covering CRISPR activation,
CRISPR interference, CRISPR knockout, multiplexed drug screens, multi-dose drug response,
and combination treatments. Across these datasets, MRFlow obtains the highest composite score
against statistical baselines, conditional autoencoders, graph models, chemical-response models,
gene-embedding linear models, and a single-cell foundation model. Ablations attribute performance
to the mechanism-structured affine field, residual field, low-rank correction, OT-guided pairing,
and count-aware training. Operator-level analyses further show response patterns consistent with
pathway-level perturbation groups.

Related Work
2.1
Single-cell perturbation prediction
Single-cell perturbation prediction has developed alongside experimental platforms that pair pooled
interventions with scRNA-seq readouts. Perturb-seq enabled scalable CRISPR-linked molecular
profiling [Dixit et al., 2016, Adamson et al., 2016, Norman et al., 2019, Replogle et al., 2022], while
sci-Plex, MIX-Seq, scPerturb, and PerturBase extended chemical screens and reusable perturbation
resources [Srivatsan et al., 2020, McFarland et al., 2020, Peidli et al., 2024, Wei et al., 2025].
Computational models built on these data include scGen [Lotfollahi et al., 2019], CPA [Lotfollahi
et al., 2023], chemCPA [Hetzel et al., 2022], PRnet [Qi et al., 2024], GEARS [Roohani et al.,
2024], TxPert [Wenkel et al., 2026], GenePert [Chen and Zou, 2024], biolord [Piran et al., 2024],
and PerturbNet [Yu et al., 2025]. Recent benchmark studies stress that split design and systematic
variation materially affect conclusions about generalization [Viñas Torné et al., 2025, Wei et al.,
2026]. MRFlow takes a different route: it models the target as a conditional response distribution and
constrains the latent vector field through a shared affine dictionary.
2.2
Single-cell foundation models
Foundation models trained on large transcriptomic corpora are a natural reference point for virtual
cell modeling. Geneformer demonstrated transfer learning for gene network biology [Theodoris et al.,
2023], scGPT introduced generative pretraining across tens of millions of cells [Cui et al., 2024],
and scFoundation scaled masked expression modeling with an asymmetric transformer architecture
[Hao et al., 2024]. These models provide broad representations, while task-specific perturbation
benchmarks show that representation scale does not automatically translate into stronger held-out
perturbation prediction [Ahlmann-Eltze et al., 2025, Wong et al., 2025, Wei et al., 2026, Wang et al.,
2026]. MRFlow is complementary to this line of work: it models the perturbation response operator
directly and constrains the conditional dynamics instead of relying on pretraining scale.
2.3
Flow matching and optimal transport
Continuous normalizing flows define generative models through neural ODEs [Chen et al., 2018].
Flow matching, rectified flow, and stochastic interpolants train these dynamics without repeatedly
solving likelihood-based ODE objectives during training [Lipman et al., 2023, Liu et al., 2023,
Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden, 2023]. Minibatch OT improves flow paths by coupling source and
target samples through low-cost assignments [Tong et al., 2024]. Optimal transport has also become
a core tool in single-cell analysis, including developmental trajectory reconstruction, integration,
dynamics, and perturbation response [Schiebinger et al., 2019, Cao et al., 2022, Tong et al., 2020,
Bunne et al., 2023, 2024b, Klein et al., 2025]. MRFlow adapts these ideas to latent virtual cell
prediction, where the main obstacles are unpaired perturbation measurements and generalization
across held-out conditions.
2.4
Generative count models and perturbation encoders
Deep generative models for scRNA-seq commonly use negative binomial likelihoods to account for
library size and overdispersion [Lopez et al., 2018, Xu et al., 2021, Gayoso et al., 2021]. MRFlow
adopts this count-aware view but inserts a conditional transport model between encoding and decoding.
For perturbation conditioning, prior work has used gene regulatory or ontology graphs for genetic
interventions [Roohani et al., 2024], curated transcription-factor networks such as CollecTRI [Müller-
Dott et al., 2023], and molecular fingerprints for chemical structure [Rogers and Hahn, 2010, Hetzel
et al., 2022]. MRFlow uses separate gene and drug encoders only up to the condition embedding;
after that point, both modalities control the same mechanism-structured residual vector field.

Mechanism-structured residual vector field
The central architectural choice in MRFlow is the decomposition of the total velocity field into a
structured affine component and a neural residual component, as shown in Figure 2:

residual field absorbs nonlinear population variation that a single affine operator cannot express.

4
Experiments
4.1
Datasets, baselines, and metrics
The evaluation covers four genetic perturbation datasets and four drug perturbation datasets. The
genetic benchmarks are Norman CRISPRa in K562 cells, Replogle CRISPRi in RPE1 cells, Adamson
CRISPRi in K562 cells, and Dixit CRISPR knockout in K562 cells [Norman et al., 2019, Replogle
et al., 2022, Adamson et al., 2016, Dixit et al., 2016]. The drug benchmarks are SciPlex3 across
MCF7, A549, and K562 cells, McFarland MIX-Seq across 169 cancer cell lines, Aissa in PC9 cells,
and the Lotfollahi A549 drug-combination dataset [Srivatsan et al., 2020, McFarland et al., 2020,
Aissa et al., 2021, Lotfollahi et al., 2023]. Together, these datasets contain 1.50 million human
single-cell profiles and 2,121 perturbation conditions.
MRFlow is compared with deterministic baselines controlMean, trainMean, and baseReg [Ahlmann-
Eltze et al., 2025, Wong et al., 2025]; conditional autoencoders CPA [Lotfollahi et al., 2023] and
chemCPA [Hetzel et al., 2022]; PRnet [Qi et al., 2024]; graph-based GEARS [Roohani et al.,
2024]; GenePert [Chen and Zou, 2024]; and scGPT [Cui et al., 2024]. Learned baselines use
official open-source implementations, while controlMean, trainMean, and baseReg are deterministic
benchmark rules. Within each dataset, every method uses the same condition-level train and test split,
preprocessed expression matrix, benchmark vocabulary, and evaluation script. Metrics include DE20
Pearson, DE20 MSE, median Wasserstein distance, and E-distance. The composite score applies
per-dataset min-max normalization followed by an equal-weight average over the four normalized
metrics. Appendix C and Appendix F give dataset, split, preprocessing, and baseline-alignment
details.
MRFlow provides a unified architecture and condition interface for genetic and chemical perturbations.
In these benchmarks, models are trained and evaluated per dataset or task using the same MRFlow
design, rather than as one jointly trained cross-dataset model.
4.2
Main results
MRFlow ranks first by composite score on all eight benchmarks, and the ranking is supported by
raw metrics rather than by normalization alone. On SciPlex3, MRFlow improves Pearson and MSE
over baseReg, CPA, chemCPA, PRnet, trainMean, and controlMean, while also achieving the lowest
E-distance. On Replogle, GenePert has slightly higher Pearson than MRFlow, but MRFlow has much
lower Wasserstein distance and E-distance, indicating better population-level agreement. Tables 1
and 2 report the full raw metrics; Appendices A, B, and H provide modality-level visualizations, the
composite-score definition, and rank-based diagnostics over raw metric cells.

Appendices I and J provide mechanism-structured diagnostics, provenance tables, and selected-
condition examples; the main claims remain anchored in raw metrics and the shared evaluation
protocol. These panels make distributional prediction tangible at the scale of individual response
genes. They complement Tables 1 and 2 rather than replacing them: the tables quantify held-out
performance across all evaluated conditions, while the panels ask whether selected signed gene shifts
retain interpretable direction and relative amplitude. This distinction matters because a model can
obtain a high mean-effect correlation while still placing the predicted population in an implausible
region of expression space.
The visual diagnostics also clarify the role of the mechanism-structured field. The affine dictionary
constrains the dominant response to reusable latent operators, and the residual branch accounts
for condition- and cell-state-specific variation. The HDAC examples therefore serve as a local
consistency check on the same design principle evaluated quantitatively in the ablations: structured
response modes should preserve broad perturbation direction, while residual dynamics should refine
heterogeneous expression shifts without turning the model into an unconstrained generator.
5
Conclusion
MRFlow casts virtual cell perturbation prediction as conditional latent distribution transport, com-
bining OT-guided flow matching with a mechanism-structured affine dictionary, residual correction,
and count-aware decoding. Across eight benchmarks, raw metrics and ablations support evaluating
virtual cells as population generators rather than only mean-response regressors. MRFlow remains a
task-level architecture trained per benchmark, but it offers a concrete route toward predictors judged
by differential-expression accuracy, population distance, and response-gene consistency.

Raw Metrics Across Benchmarks
The raw metrics across drug and genetic perturbation benchmarks are reported in Tables 1 and 2
in the main text. Figures A.1 and A.2 provide companion visualizations of the same benchmark
comparisons, separating drug and genetic perturbation tasks. The composite score is an aggregate
summary computed from these four raw metrics, with the normalization rule specified in Appendix B.
This section makes the evidence behind the main-text ranking inspectable without relying on the
composite score alone. The visualizations present the comparison from a metric-level perspective,
separating mean-effect accuracy, measured by DE20 Pearson and DE20 MSE, from population-level
agreement, measured by Wasserstein distance and E-distance. This distinction is central to MRFlow:
the model is designed as a distributional transport method, and its advantage should appear not only
in average expression shifts but also in the geometry of predicted cell populations.
The four metrics are non-redundant by design. DE20 Pearson asks whether the strongest differentially
expressed genes move in the correct relative direction, while DE20 MSE penalizes the absolute
size of those shifts. Wasserstein distance and E-distance instead compare empirical distributions of
predicted and observed cell populations. A method can therefore score well on the mean response
while producing a poor population distribution, or it can improve distributional agreement at the cost
of a small loss in mean-effect correlation. The appendix figures make these trade-offs visible because
a single composite number would hide which biological question each method answers well.
This metric separation is also needed for comparing drug and genetic perturbation tasks. Drug
screens contain dose and cell-context variation, so strong average-response prediction can coexist
with heterogeneous single-cell effects. Genetic screens often include sharper transcriptional shifts, but
the source and target populations remain unpaired. MRFlow addresses both regimes through the same
conditional transport architecture, and the raw-metric view directly tests whether the architecture
behaves consistently across modalities.

and MSE summarize differential-expression recovery, while Wasserstein distance and E-distance
summarize population-level agreement. The composite therefore assigns half of its weight to mean-
effect behavior and half to distributional behavior. This choice matches the paper’s formulation of
virtual cell prediction as population transport, while retaining the familiar DE20 metrics used in
perturbation-prediction benchmarks.
The composite score is defined as an aggregate summary of the raw metrics. It provides a compact
ranking because each benchmark contains multiple methods and four non-commensurate metrics, but
it is not a substitute for the underlying measurements. For this reason, the main text reports Tables 1
and 2, Appendix A visualizes the raw comparisons, and Appendix H summarizes raw-metric ranks
without reusing the composite normalization.
C
Dataset and Split Details
Table C.1 summarizes the datasets used in the benchmark. Each benchmark uses a condition-level
held-out protocol with test ratio 0.2 for datasets evaluated under random condition splits. A condition
is defined by the perturbation identity together with the dose and cell context whenever those
annotations are present. The split is therefore a held-out perturbation-condition split rather than an
unseen-drug-identity or unseen-gene-identity split.
The eight benchmarks stress different parts of the virtual-cell prediction problem. Norman, Adamson,
Dixit, and Replogle evaluate genetic perturbations across CRISPRa, CRISPRi, and CRISPR knockout
designs. SciPlex3, McFarland, Aissa, and Lotfollahi evaluate chemical perturbations across multi-
cell-line screens, dose variation, resistance-associated drug response, and drug-combination settings.
This mixture tests whether a model can maintain the same response-distribution principle across
perturbation technologies and cell contexts.
The held-out unit is a perturbation condition rather than an individual cell. This matters because
cells sampled from the same condition are exchangeable observations of one experimental response
distribution. Randomly splitting cells within a condition would test interpolation among replicate
cells, which is easier and less relevant for AIVC use cases. The condition-level split instead asks
whether a model can predict the response distribution for conditions that were not observed during
training under the benchmark split.
Several dataset-specific details affect interpretation. Replogle uses non-targeting sgRNA as an internal
control rather than a separate control-cell group, so control information is defined by the screen
design. Aissa has only three non-control drug conditions in the processed benchmark, which makes
any aggregate statistic more sensitive to individual condition behavior. SciPlex3 and McFarland
contain explicit cell-context variation, so the condition includes context in addition to perturbation
identity. These details are not exceptions to the evaluation protocol; they define what a held-out
condition means for each dataset.

condition, reducing pressure to model perturbation-induced shifts. The weighting does not change the
evaluation target and does not insert test labels into inference; it only adjusts the training emphasis
for genes selected within the training procedure for each condition.
Condition-balanced sampling addresses another practical issue in perturbation atlases. Some pertur-
bation conditions contain many more cells than others. Sampling conditions more evenly prevents
large conditions from dominating the velocity field and decoder updates. For distributional prediction,
the model must learn response geometry across many conditions rather than optimizing primarily for
the most frequently sampled ones.
F
Baseline and Preprocessing Details
All methods are evaluated on the same held-out perturbation conditions for each dataset, and metrics
are computed using one shared evaluation script. Preprocessing follows the dataset-specific HVG
selections summarized in Appendix C; perturbation genes are kept in the modeling vocabulary under
the benchmark vocabulary construction. Expression metrics are computed in the same expression
space for all methods within a dataset, while the negative binomial decoder is trained against count-
scale targets for datasets with count matrices.
Table F.1 specifies the role and implementation source for each baseline. CPA, chemCPA, PRnet,
GEARS, GenePert, and scGPT are run from official open-source implementations. controlMean,
trainMean, and baseReg are deterministic benchmark baselines evaluated from the same preprocessed
matrices. Within each dataset, every method receives the same train and test perturbation-condition
split, the same processed expression matrix, the same HVG and benchmark gene vocabulary, and the
same metric computation pipeline.
The baselines cover three levels of difficulty. controlMean and trainMean test whether a method
beats simple population summaries. baseReg tests a deliberately simple supervised regression rule,
motivated by recent perturbation-prediction studies showing that simple baselines can be highly
competitive. CPA, chemCPA, PRnet, GEARS, GenePert, and scGPT represent learned perturbation
models that differ in condition encoding, graph use, chemical structure handling, and pretraining.
Comparing against all three groups reduces the chance that MRFlow is only outperforming weak
baselines or competing within a single model family.
The fairness principle is input alignment. A baseline does not receive a different held-out split, a
different expression matrix, or a different metric definition. The shared setup is especially important
for distributional metrics because Wasserstein distance and E-distance can change with preprocessing
choices, gene vocabulary, and cell subsampling. Keeping these inputs fixed makes the raw-metric
comparisons in Tables 1 and 2 attributable to the predictive models.
All datasets and learned baseline implementations used in this study originate from public papers,
public data portals, or official open-source releases associated with the cited work. The final repro-
duction package will preserve attribution to the original creators and will follow the corresponding
licenses, access requirements, and redistribution terms for each dataset and software component.

G
Compute Resources and Broader Impact
All MRFlow experiments were run on a workstation with one NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU with 32 GB
memory and 16 vCPUs from an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Platinum 8352V CPU at 2.10 GHz. The software
environment used Ubuntu 22.04, Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.8.0, and CUDA 12.8. Training time varied
with dataset size and condition complexity, ranging from approximately 3 to 8 hours per dataset.
The largest screens require more time because condition-balanced sampling, latent pairing, endpoint
alignment, and count reconstruction are computed over many perturbation conditions.
MRFlow supports perturbation prioritization, drug-response analysis, and virtual-cell modeling by
predicting response distributions before costly wet-lab validation. This can reduce experimental search
space and help identify perturbation conditions that merit closer biological inspection. The main risk is
over-interpretation: in-silico predictions may reflect biases in the training atlas, preprocessing choices,
or benchmark splits, and they are not clinical or causal evidence without experimental validation.
The paper therefore reports raw metrics, dataset details, baseline alignment, and qualitative response-
gene panels to make model behavior inspectable, without presenting MRFlow as a replacement for
biological experiments.

Additional Quantitative Evidence
Because composite scores can obscure metric-specific trade-offs, Figures H.1 and H.2 summarize
MRFlow ranks directly over raw metric cells. These summaries use the same values reported in
Tables 1 and 2; they are rank diagnostics rather than statistical significance tests. Together with
Appendix A, they address a common concern for multi-metric perturbation prediction: whether a
method wins because a normalization scheme favors one metric or because it remains competitive
across the raw measurements that define the task.
Raw-metric ranks are a stricter sanity check than the composite score in one respect: they ignore
the magnitude of each advantage and ask only how often MRFlow is near the best method for each
dataset-metric cell. A method that wins the composite through one very strong metric and several
weak metrics would be exposed by this view. Conversely, a model with consistently high ranks across
Pearson, MSE, Wasserstein distance, and E-distance has evidence for balanced behavior even when
another method wins an isolated metric.

The dictionary capacity sweep clarifies why the shared affine dictionary is a structural choice rather
than a generic capacity increase. Moving from K = 1 to K = 16 improves Pearson, MSE, and

conditions. Increasing to K = 32 does not improve the result, which suggests that too many bases
can weaken the regularizing effect of the dictionary. This supports the main-text interpretation that
MRFlow benefits from a controlled response basis with condition-specific recombination, not from
an unconstrained velocity field.
The K-sweep is a capacity sensitivity analysis for the shared mechanism-structured affine dictionary.
K = 1 corresponds to a single-basis dictionary and is too restrictive for the diversity of SciPlex3
perturbation responses. Intermediate values allow the model to recombine several latent response
operators, and K = 16 gives the best reported balance in this experiment. The lack of improvement
at K = 32 weakens a simple parameter-count explanation for the result. The evidence supports
structured expressivity with regularization, not the claim that more bases always give a better virtual
cell model.

Supplementary Tables for Mechanism-Structured Diagnostics and Example
Provenance
The supplementary tables in this section make the internal evidence behind the mechanism-structured
design auditable. They address a different question from the benchmark tables: rather than asking
whether MRFlow predicts held-out perturbation responses better than baselines, they ask whether
the learned condition-dependent operators organize perturbations in a way that is consistent with
external pathway annotations, whether the macro and residual fields have separable quantitative roles,
and whether the selected response-gene panels are traceable to an explicit candidate pool. These
analyses support a mechanism-informed inductive bias while keeping the interpretation at the level of
pathway-consistent operator organization.
The alpha-space diagnostics test the central structural hypothesis of MRFlow. For a condition c,
the shared affine dictionary is activated by weights α(c), so the geometry of these weights reflects
how the model recombines latent response operators across perturbations. If the dictionary only
acted as unconstrained extra capacity, pathway labels would not be expected to concentrate in α(c)-
space. Tables I.1, I.2, and I.3 show the opposite pattern at the diagnostic level: annotated same-class
perturbations have higher alpha similarity than different-class perturbations, and nearest-neighbor
enrichment exceeds permutation nulls. The level-2 pathway result is stronger than the level-1 result,
which is consistent with the idea that operator reuse is more visible when broad pathway labels are
refined into more specific MoA classes.

Supplementary Matched Response-Gene Shift Examples
To complement the aggregate metrics in the main text, we provide matched response-gene shift visu-
alizations across all eight benchmark datasets. Each panel corresponds to one held-out perturbation
condition and displays 20 response genes, with 10 upregulated genes and 10 downregulated genes.
For each gene, the observed signed expression shift is compared with the MRFlow-predicted signed
shift, where both shifts are computed relative to the corresponding control or source population.
These panels are generated from processed AnnData files and MRFlow checkpoint inference; no
simulated values are used. The examples are selected as high-matching qualitative cases using a
balanced criterion that favors direction agreement, visible response magnitude, and low absolute error.
They should therefore be interpreted as qualitative model-inspection panels rather than unbiased
estimates of global performance. The aggregate performance claims remain based on the raw metrics
reported in Tables 1 and 2.
These examples provide interpretability at the level that a biologist or drug-response analyst would
inspect after reading the aggregate tables. A distributional model may score well on global metrics
but still produce response-gene shifts that are difficult to interpret condition by condition. These
panels ask a local question: when a held-out condition has a clear signed response signature, does the
predicted signature preserve the direction and relative size of the response genes? The examples do
not replace quantitative evaluation, but they make the model output more auditable.
The selected genes are displayed as signed shifts relative to control rather than as raw expression
values. This presentation aligns the panels with the DE20 metrics in the main text and with the
biological question of perturbation response. It also avoids conflating baseline expression level with
induced change. For AIVC use, the induced change is often the actionable object: it indicates which
genes are predicted to rise or fall under an intervention and whether the predicted response agrees
with the observed perturbation signature.
