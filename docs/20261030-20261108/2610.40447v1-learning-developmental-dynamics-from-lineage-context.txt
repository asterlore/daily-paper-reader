Abstract 

A central goal of biology is to predict the dynamics of multicellular systems. Lineage context (the states of a cell’s sisters, cousins, etc.) could inform these predictions, but how best to leverage it remains unclear. Here we introduce lineage inpainting , a generative modeling approach that uses lineage context with tree-structured transformers and stochastic interpolants to model cell state dynamics across lineage trees. Applied to a lineage-traced transcriptional atlas of mouse embryogenesis, it recovers dynamics not resolved by gene expression alone, including multiple transcriptional convergence events where distinct lineages are drawn to a common state. Despite being fit on unperturbed development, lineage inpainting can generate hypotheses about perturbation responses by simulating counterfactuals. These simulations agree in many cases with observed developmental phenotypes, including a single-cell measurement of the Tbx5 knockout response. Our results show that lineage information provides a rich source of context for learning multicellular dynamics. 

# 1. Introduction 

Biologists have long sought to understand how a pluripotent cell gives rise to the many cell types in a fully developed organism [1–4]. Technologies such as single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) have enabled atlases which enumerate the cell states that appear during development [5–9]. While these atlases have mapped out cell

A large body of work has focused on the problem of inferring developmental dynamics from molecular snap-shots alone [10–17]. A fundamental challenge of these efforts is that the problem is underconstrained: multiple possible dynamics can give rise to the same snapshots [18]. Many approaches, including optimal transport meth-ods, resolve this ambiguity through a prescribed transport cost or dynamical regularization that favors short paths supporting a putative least-action principle driving cell dynamics. These assumptions are useful but can obscure biological transitions when molecular similarity is an unreliable proxy for shared ancestry or fate [3, 7, 19]. Lineage tracing provides complementary information about cell fate dynamics. Rather than inferring lineage relationships from molecular similarity, these techniques directly measure shared ancestry. In small, transparent embryos, such as C. elegans and the early embryo of D. rerio , cell divisions can be tracked by microscopy [20– 22]. Mammalian embryos are larger and less accessible, so lineage relationships are instead reconstructed from heritable marks that cells pass to their progeny [23–25]. These marks can be naturally occurring DNA mutations, static barcodes introduced at a given time point, or DNA edits that are continuously introduced over time by an engineered lineage tracing system [26–32]. In particular, recent methods including DNA Typewriter and PEtracer use prime editing to continuously and irreversibly modify synthetic edit sites in genomes [33–35]. With PEtracer, we recently reconstructed lineage trees that resolve ∼ 75% of cell divisions across early embryogenesis [36]. However, again due to the destructive nature of transcriptomics, the internal node states (and the state dynamics) cannot be observed directly – regardless of whether trees are measured by imaging or molecular recording. The first step to shed light on the full progression of states is to make measurements across developmental times. If we observe both the lineage tree and transcriptional states at two or more timepoints, we can try to integrate them by matching the leaves of an earlier tree to the internal nodes of a later one. In C. elegans ,every individual organism follows the same sequence of cell divisions, producing the same tree [7, 20]. Trees from different organisms can be directly overlaid, and the exact correspondence between cells at different timepoints is known. In mammals, however, development is non-deterministic, so lineage relationships do not specify a fixed cor-respondence between cells in different embryos [37, 38]. Lineage nevertheless provides a context in which to interpret each cell’s measured state. This context can be informative both because development is regulative, with cell fates shaped by interactions with other cells [39], and because developmental history can bias fate in ways not captured by cell state measurements [19, 40]. Considering a cell together with its relatives could there-fore help distinguish dynamics that are ambiguous without lineage context. This motivates a shift from pairing cells to instead modeling distributions over states on a tree, considering the cell’s state and its recorded lineage context simultaneously. Recent methods have begun to use lineage information for dynamic inference. LineageOT [41] and moslin [42] refine optimal transport couplings between timepoints using lineage information, returning a coupling over observed cells rather than a generative model. Consequently, one cannot directly use these methods to sample trajectories, forecast states, or predict the effects of perturbations. GENOT and related methods [43, 44] provide approaches to lift such couplings into generative models, but operate at the single-cell level, predicting a cell’s future or past from its current state alone. Here we show that lineage context enables more faithful modeling of developmental trajectories beyond single-cell transcriptional states. We introduce lineage inpainting , a generative approach that jointly samples unobserved cell states across a reconstructed lineage tree using the states of related cells observed at a single time point. The transformer architecture provides a natural foundation for this task: their attention mechanisms can integrate information across variable numbers of cells [45, 46]. We develop a tree-structured attention mechanism to incorporate lineage relationships and use it to build a transformer-based stochastic interpolant model [47–49]. We fit the model using a consistency objective: generated states must match observations from other trees collected at the corresponding developmental times in distribution . This objective allows lineage inpainting to integrate measurements across embryos without requiring exact cell-to-cell correspondences or a 

> 2

predefined transport cost between cell states. We apply lineage inpainting to PEtracer data spanning mouse embryogenesis from E7.5 to E10.0 [36]. The model yields a data-driven map of state transitions and recovers transcriptional convergence events missed by alternatives using gene expression alone, revealing how distinct progenitors can reach a common transcriptional state. We further show that a model trained on unperturbed development can be used to make predictions about the developmental effects of genetic perturbations at single-cell resolution. Together, these results demonstrate that lineage context can improve the reconstruction and prediction of developmental trajectories. 

# 2. Results 

The development of an embryo can be represented as a lineage tree branching through time as cells divide [2]. Retrospective lineage tracing methods such as PEtracer [33, 36] allow us to reconstruct these trees. For each sequenced cell, these methods provide both its transcriptional state and its position among the leaves of a reconstructed lineage tree. The internal nodes of that tree represent ancestral cells, each with an inferred time from molecular clock-based branch length estimation [50, 51]. Because sequencing is destructive each tree is observed only at a single time. We see the “snapshot” of cells alive at the time of capture (the leaves of the reconstructed tree) while every earlier ancestor and later descendant is unobserved ( Fig. 1a ). Sampling multiple embryos across development yields many trees, observed across different times. Our goal is to turn these partial observations into a model of developmental dynamics which can condition on any observed snapshot and sample from the distribution of possible ancestors and descendants of each cell in the snapshot ( Fig. 1b ). We split this into two subproblems ( Fig. 1c ). First, we need to somehow fill in the unobserved states at internal nodes of measured trees, providing us with samples of complete trees. Second, we need to fit a generative model capable of sampling from a distribution of states forward or backward in time, conditional on a single snapshot. We refer to these subproblems as imputation and sampling respectively. Below, we show how to address these problems. We first introduce a method for filling in internal node states which learns an imputation function rather than imposing any explicit transport cost, a second generative model for sampling states which operates over entire trees rather than individual cells and a tree-aware attention mechanism which underlies both models ( Fig. 1d ). 

2.1. Lineage inpainting 2.1.1. Learning to impute ancestral states 

Imputing states at the internal nodes of a tree is a classic problem in phylogenetics. In ancestral state recon-struction, one has access to trait measurements (e.g., tail length) at the leaves of a phylogenetic tree (i.e., extant species), and the goal is to reconstruct the traits of ancestral species represented by internal nodes of the phy-logenetic tree [52–55]. This problem has one key distinction from our lineage tracing setting, which is that in phylogenetics only a single evolutionary tree is available, whereas in our developmental system we are able to observe multiple trees which have different observation times. A common approach to ancestral state reconstruction is to assume that evolutionary dynamics are Brownian. Under this assumption, Felsenstein’s independent contrasts summarizes descendant states by averages weighted by their distance in the tree and their uncertainty [52, 56]. The assumption of Brownian dynamics is sensible and perhaps necessary when one only has access to a single snapshot of a single tree. But with access to observations across multiple trees at different times, we can hope to learn a more specific ancestral reconstruction operation which is consistent with our observations. The central challenge of fitting such an ancestral reconstruction model is that no internal node is ever mea-sured, so there are no ground-truth ancestral states to regress against. Instead, we optimize our model using a loss function which encourages imputed node states to be consistent across our trees. Imputing the ancestors of a tree captured late in development yields a prediction for the populations at earlier timepoints, and we can

A good ancestral reconstruction will match the distribution of the states at each time from the trees we observed at each of those times. Because each tree anchors this comparison to real data at a different time, requiring all trees to agree constrains the inferred dynamics to be consistent with our snapshot observations. We implement this idea as an alignment loss that minimizes the distributional distance, across trees, between what we call tree features . The simplest and most essential tree feature is the state of a single cell and its corresponding time. Matching the distribution of these across trees makes the cell population at each time point consistent from one tree to the next. This alone is not sufficient, because a model can reproduce the correct distribution of states at every time while still completely distorting the tree structure. So we also align higher-order tree features, including pairs of sister-cell states and trajectories of states across time (see Section 4.1.3 for full details). Our alignment loss is the sum of the distributional distance of several tree features, between each pair of trees ( Supplemental Figs. S14– S15 ). We compute these imputations with a transformer that takes the nodes of a tree as input, representing each observed node by its measured state and time, and each unobserved node starts as noise, with the model learning to turn this noise into consistent imputations. Tree structure is encoded in the model through a modified attention layer, where we bias each attention score by the tree distance between the two nodes it relates ( Box 1 ). This imputation model provides predictions backward in time, filling in internal node states given the leaf states as input. However, it cannot sample from the distribution of descendants of a cell. To make predictions both forward and backward in time, we fit a separate sampling model.

Box 1: A tree-structured transformer 

A cell’s fate and history are not fully determined by its current transcriptional state alone. Cells sharing a recent ancestor inherit common factors that indicate their origin and shape how they develop [19, 40]. A single-cell level predictor is not able to leverage this shared information. Instead, we would ideally like a model that can process an entire tree — each cell alongside all of its relatives — at once. Here, we show how a simple extension of the transformer architecture allows us to do this. The transformer is best known as the architecture at the heart of modern language modeling [57, 58], but it is also a general purpose tool for processing sets of “tokens”. In language, each “token” is a few characters in a sentence, and the transformer uses a mechanism called attention to allow each token to aggregate information from all others. Here, instead each token represents a cell state. 2.1.2. Learning to sample ancestor and descendant states 

Given trees with filled in internal states, our goal is to be able to condition on all the cells alive at any given time and sample the states for all the descendant and ancestral nodes. This problem resembles settings in computer vision called “inpainting” where we observe one part of an image and try to sample the rest, matching the natural distribution over images [60, 61]. Building on these approaches, we introduce a tree-structured generative model that enables conditionally sampling ancestors and descendants based on the observed snapshot (Section 4.1.4). Denoising diffusion models (and related flow matching and stochastic interpolant constructions) are widely used generative models that start from random noise and take denoising steps, with each step leading to sharper generated samples. We use a training recipe similar to the standard diffusion approach [47]. We pick a snapshot of the filled in tree to condition on, which we treat as the observed states. We then corrupt the remaining nodes – treated as unobserved – with noise, and train the model to predict the unnoised clean states. Similar to the imputation model, we use a tree-based transformer architecture for the sampling model, so that the model can learn to integrate information over the entire lineage tree. When we want to sample the ancestors or descendants given an observed snapshot we can then initialize all the unobserved nodes as random noise and iteratively

de-noise them, conditioning on the observed nodes throughout ( Fig. 1d ). 

2.1.3. Jointly learning to impute and sample 

Although in principle the imputation model and sampling model can be completely separate objects, we optimize the two models jointly (that is, end-to-end and simultaneously). At each training step, the imputation model first completes a partially observed tree, and that completed tree is then used to train the sampling model. Because the internal node states used to train the sampler are imperfect synthetic states produced by the imputation model, training directly on these outputs could lead the sampler to reproduce low-quality outputs from the imputer. To avoid this, we instead snap the imputer outputs to their nearest observed real cell at the corresponding or adjacent developmental snapshot, thereby providing sharp (realistic) training targets. We interleave these targets with training steps that retain the imputed states themselves, allowing the sampling loss to remain differentiable through the imputer. In this way, the sampler learns to generate realistic cell states while preserving the developmental correspondence learned by the imputation model ( Supplemental Fig. S12 ). Full training details are provided in Section 4.1.5. This decomposition into imputation and sampling is more general than the particular models we use here. The imputation step can be any procedure that supplies a correspondence between states at different times, and the sampling step can be any conditional generative model trained on the resulting completed trees. GENOT, for example, fits into this framework: discrete optimal transport supplies the imputation, and conditional flow match-ing learns to sample from the resulting coupling. Among the combinations of imputation and sampling methods we evaluated, jointly optimizing tree-structured transformer models for imputation and sampling performs best (Supplemental Text S2 , Supplemental Figs. S8–S13 ). 

2.2. Evaluating lineage inpainting with synthetic developmental processes 

Cells do not always follow minimal displacement paths in gene expression [7], and their trajectories are not always independent or Markovian with respect to transcriptomic state of a single cell [19, 62]. We test the extent to which lineage inpainting is able to (1) learn non-least-action dynamics, and (2) learn context-dependent dynamics. Neither capability can be directly tested on real data, since no state of an internal node is ever measured in real lineage-traced scRNA-seq data. As such, we turn to synthetic and semi-synthetic datasets. 

2.2.1. Lineage inpainting can learn non-least-action dynamics 

We first ask whether lineage inpainting can learn developmental dynamics that violate least-action optimal transport assumptions. Using what we refer to as branch-modified SDEs (Section 4.2.1), we construct two synthetic developmental processes that have similar marginal distributions, but distinct dynamics: one follows the least-action principle, while the other has trajectories that cross – violating optimal transport assumptions. We take snapshots of this process, yielding lineage trees with states at the leaves, at various times, and ask if the true dynamics can be recovered from these snapshots. We compare lineage inpainting against three baselines: multimarginal flow matching [48] (a deterministic cell-state flow baseline that does not use tree structure), GENOT with Sinkhorn couplings [43], which fits couplings between consecutive time points via entropic optimal transport (also ignoring tree structure), and GENOT with fused Gromov–Wasserstein (FGW) couplings [42, 43], which incorporates tree structure into the coupling cost. Full implementations and training details are given in Section 4.2.3. Across every dimensionality tested, lineage inpainting recovers the true dynamics with high accuracy ( Fig. 2a,b ,

Supplemental Fig. S1 ). The GENOT baselines perform well on the first process, where the dynamics are optimal-transport-like, but degrade on the second, where the crossing structure cannot be recovered by a minimal-displacement coupling alone. The gap persists even for the FGW-based baseline, which does have access to the tree: its coupling still includes a state-space displacement cost, which favors the wrong assignment in the crossing 

> 7

process (Section 4.2.3). Multimarginal flow matching is far from the truth in both processes at every dimension, as expected for a deterministic method that cannot represent branching. To understand if lineage inpainting can perform with more complicated data distributions, we next construct semi-synthetic datasets where real observed cells are wired together through artificial rules such that the true dynamics are known. Our approach is to construct large lineage trees out of small cell type motifs. A cell type motif is simply a repeated pattern of cell types on a lineage tree. For example, a size three motif could be a cell of type A with daughters of type B and type C [63]. As visually depicted in Fig. 2c , we construct cell type motifs by picking two real E9.0 cell types as progenitors, and four E9.5 cell types as descendants. Given such a motif, we can then construct a full lineage tree by repeatedly patterning the same motif, and randomly sampling different cells of the same type across different instances of the motif. We test the ability of models to reconstruct the true dynamics from snapshots across 32 random choices of 6-node cell type motifs ( Fig. 2c ). Since these cell types are randomly chosen, they can be optimal-transport-like (where child cell types closely resemble their parent cell types) by chance, but they need not be. We find that GENOT models generally perform well when dynamics are OT-like but fail otherwise, as we might expect. Lineage inpainting similarly performs well on OT-like motifs, but maintains strong performance across a broader range of dynamics. We can characterize the dynamics where lineage inpainting succeeds. We call a motif Felsenstein-like if assigning the two sibling-group means to their imposed progenitors is cheaper than swapping the groups – this echoes the inductive bias built into our architecture. We find that lineage inpainting recovers essentially every Felsenstein-like motif and fails only outside that set. In some cases, the randomly chosen cell type motif is neither OT-like or Felsenstein-like. Such cases can be pathological – such as when two very distinct cell types switch fates ( Fig. 2c ). In the sampled motifs, every motif classified as optimal-transport-like is also Felsenstein-like. Here lineage inpainting captures a broader range of motifs than the OT-based generative models. 

2.2.2. Lineage inpainting can learn context-dependent dynamics 

We next ask if lineage inpainting models are able to learn context-dependent dynamics. That is, if they are able to extract information from a cell’s lineage context to improve predictions of its past and future. Again, we first test this using fully synthetic data generated by branch-modified SDEs. We construct a dataset where a progenitor commits stochastically to one of two fates at an early time. For several subsequent divisions the committed fate biases each descendant’s state only weakly, such that it can be obscured by noise (with scale σ). Several generations later the two fates diverge clearly. When σ is large, a method that processes cells one at a time has access only to the very noisy information about early commitment steps. In contrast, a method that aggregates evidence across a clone should be able to make a more accurate prediction by averaging over it. Because the lineage inpainting model attends over cells in a subtree simultaneously, it can in principle perform this aggregation. Indeed, we find that lineage inpainting is able to extract this early fate bias from lineage context ( Fig. 2d,e ). At low noise ( σ = 0 .2), where the fate bias is clear from a single cell, all methods perform well, and increasing the context adds little. However, as noise increases, lineage inpainting performance improves with context size and further outperforms alternatives. Having shown that lineage inpainting benefits from lineage context in synthetic datasets, we now move to a semi-synthetic setting with real cells. We again use the same kind of cell type motif, but with a different motif structure, which resembles a transcriptional convergence event. We build trees using a mixture of two motifs that share a child type B (Fig. 2f ) – in other words, there are two paths to making the same cell type. In both motifs, the parent of the shared child type is determined by the type of its cousin. To infer the ancestor of a type B cell, a model must therefore copy its cousin’s cell type. Models which operate at the single-cell level cannot perform this context copying operation, since they take only a single cell as input. Lineage inpainting, however, has access to the cousin cell type at test time. Across 10 random instances of these motifs, lineage inpainting is able to correctly assign the cell type of the ancestral cells reliably ( Fig. 2f ). Together, these benchmarks show that lineage inpainting recovers couplings that minimal displacement can-

not, and resolves dynamics that no single cell’s state determines, in both simulated and real cell states. 

2.3. Modeling mouse embryogenesis using a lineage-traced atlas 

Having established the model’s performance on synthetic data, we next apply lineage inpainting to our primary setting: mouse embryogenesis. We assemble a ∼4-million-cell atlas spanning E7.5 to E10.0, combining 1.4 million lineage-traced cells from [36] with non-lineage-traced cells from the same dataset and from Qiu et al. [9]. The cells from these two datasets are integrated in a single scVI model [64]. We fit a single lineage inpainting model to this combined dataset, operating on the scVI latent space (see Sections 4.3 and 4.1.3). To visualize the ancestor–descendant coupling learned by the model, we construct a cell-type-level transition map spanning E7.5 to E10.0 ( Fig. 3a ). For each observed time point, we take the trees with leaves at that time point, sample their internal node states near the previous observed time point. We then label these imputed states by their nearest neighbors and aggregate into a cell type transition map (Section 4.4.1). Despite being completely blind to labels during training time, lineage inpainting recovers a cell-type transition map which largely recapitulates known transitions. Compared with the manually curated list of cell state transitions presented in Qiu et al. [9], 93% of inferred single-cell transitions are consistent, with the remainder detailed in Supplemental Fig. S4 . Notably, this map captures the multiple mesodermal origins of endothelial cells [65–68]. We next ask if the individual state transitions are faithful at single-cell resolution. We evaluate the model at the single-cell level using six metrics which evaluate the distribution of states, individual trajectories, and the structure of the tree as a whole (Section 4.2.2). Two metrics are purely data-driven and do not rely on cell type labels or biological prior knowledge: clonal distortion , which measures whether pushing a clone back to an ancestral state and then forward recovers the clone’s original distribution of states, and marginal distortion , which measures the disagreement between the model’s imputed distributions and the empirical cell-state distribution at each observed time point. Three metrics assess individual trajectories and draw on cell type annotations: how often inferred transitions cross germ layers ( germ layer crossing rate ) or oscillate between cell-type labels ( lineage oscillation rate ), and how well ancestor and descendant expression is correlated for Hox genes known to remain stable within neural ectoderm and neural crest cell types ( Hox code distortion ) [69]. Finally, linkage distortion compares the ancestral-linkage matrix [36, 70] – a summary of how recently pairs of cell types shared a common progenitor – between real and model-imputed trees, quantifying whether the model preserves tree structure rather than individual trajectories or state distributions alone. Across all six metrics, lineage inpainting outperforms two optimal transport baselines fit to the same atlas, GENOT-Sinkhorn and GENOT-cbFGW [43] ( Fig. 3b , Supplemental Fig. S3 ). We next ask how much lineage inpainting uses lineage context to refine its predictions, separately for ancestor and descendant prediction (Section 4.4.2). Because neither direction has ground-truth states, we score both by cycle consistency: whether states pushed to another time and back recover the starting distribution. For ancestral inference, we fix a target internal node and vary the amount of descendant context provided as input. For descendant inference, we infer a fixed target leaf set from ancestral snapshots with varying numbers of cousins and conditioning times. In both directions, increasing context improves performance ( Fig. 3c,e ). At the smallest context sizes, lineage inpainting is comparable to or worse than the GENOT baselines. However, with additional context, it continues to improve well beyond them. The benefit of added context is not uniform across cell types ( Fig. 3d,f ). For ancestral inference, the largest gains appear in epidermal progenitors, while the cervical spinal cord benefits least. For descendant inference, the endothelial cells gain the most and the cervical spinal cord the least. Taken together, these results indicate that lineage inpainting learns biologically sensible cell-state transitions from the mouse embryogenesis atlas. Its performance increases as a result of its ability to make use of lineage context.

2.4. Lineage inpainting resolves transcriptional convergence dynamics 

Having fit a lineage inpainting model of mouse development, we ask how its inferred dynamics differ from those of optimal transport in the gene expression latent space. We compare against GENOT-Sinkhorn [43]. To make the comparison, we sample a discrete coupling between cells from each model (Section 4.2.3). Across all adjacent time points, we measured the disagreement between the models as the L2 distance between their two predicted progenitor states ( Fig. 4a ). The disagreement depends on cell type. For some cell types it is small, and OT and lineage inpainting recovered similar ancestors. For others – endothelial cells and several classes of differentiated neurons – the difference is large ( Fig. 4b ). We ask what distinguishes these cell types. From our synthetic and semi-synthetic experiments, we know that cell-level models struggle to capture con-vergence events, while lineage inpainting is able to resolve them using lineage context (recall Fig. 2f ). To test if this is the source of the disagreement between the models in the mouse development setting, we need a way to score convergence. Convergence is a statement about origins rather than present states: if cells that look alike today descend from a single progenitor population, their inferred ancestors should also look alike, whereas if the same state was reached independently from diverse origins, those ancestors will be scattered across the expression space. We turn this intuition into a per-cell score by taking the 100 nearest neighbors of each cell in the scVI latent space and measuring the transcriptional variance among their inferred progenitors, relative to the local spacing of cells (Section 4.4.3). Because the neighbors are by construction similar in their current state, any remaining variance among their ancestors reflects divergence in origin rather than in present identity. Across cell types, the convergence score aligned with the OT-vs-lineage inpainting disagreement (Spearman ρ = 0 .78), and the same cell types were high on both ( Fig. 4b , Supplemental Fig. S5 ). Endothelial cells are the clearest example of transcriptional convergence. Their inferred progenitors fall largely into five cell types: somatic mesoderm, dermomyotome, splanchnic mesoderm, head paraxial mesoderm and allantois ( Fig. 4c ), suggesting that endothelium is generated independently by paraxial, lateral plate and extraembryonic mesoderm. This model prediction is consistent with prior literature establishing that endothelium arises from multiple distinct lineage origins [65–68]. The model reaches this conclusion from lineage context rather than expression alone. At E9.5 endothelial cells sit in clades composed predominantly of leaves of a single other type, with different endothelial cells embedded among different types ( Fig. 4c ), and the model assigns ancestors accordingly. Cell-level models such as GENOT have no access to this clonal information at test time, and consequently struggle to distinguish endothelial cells

2.5. Quantifying transcriptional memory and erasure in convergent differentiation 

When distinct lineages converge on a common cell type, what happens to the differences between them? In 1976, Lewis and Wolpert [71] proposed that cells could have the same functional identity while retaining distinct signatures associated with their spatial location (or positional information), an idea they called non-equivalence. In transcriptional convergence events where multiple cell types are produced independently in different regions of the embryo, non-equivalence suggests that convergent cell types may retain signatures of their origin. Targeted experimental studies have tested non-equivalence in individual cell types. Some cell types appear molecularly equivalent across lineages: macrophages arising from distinct origins placed in the same niche become near indistinguishable [72]. In contrast, fibroblasts epigenetically maintain their embryonic Hox code expression [73]. These individual case studies show that origin-specific differences can persist to varying extents in different cell types. Here, we use lineage inpainting to quantify origin-specific transcriptional memory across multiple convergence events in the embryo. We focus on three cell types with high convergence scores: endothelial cells, spinal cord interneurons and somatic motor neurons ( Fig. 4b ). For endothelium, we compare origins in paraxial mesoderm, a tissue along the sides of the developing spine, and lateral plate mesoderm, which lies further to the sides. For the neurons, we compare origins along the anterior-posterior axis, which we separate using spinal cord and hindbrain annotations [74]. We defined transcriptional programs distinguishing these sources, together with cell type identity programs, by linear discriminant analysis between labeled cells (Section 4.4.3, Fig. 4d ). Binning the inferred trajectories by their E8.5 source and following them along both programs shows that across all three convergence events, cells first diversify along the source-associated program, then establish cell type identity with similar dynamics across distinct sources ( Fig. 4e ). Notably, the dynamics of diversification are not the same across types: endothelial progenitors are already separated along the paraxial versus lateral plate program at E7.5, before the endothelial identity program is established, whereas the neuron progenitors remain similar on the anterior-posterior program until after E7.5. The stability of the positional transcriptional program is also distinct between ECs and neurons. Endothelial cells begin to lose their spatial distinctness as the identity program establishes: the separation peaks near E8.5 and then narrows through E9.5. In contrast, the neurons hold theirs stably after E8.5. These results suggest that neurons retain more memory of their origin than endothelial cells do. We next quantify this memory at the level of individual genes. For each convergence event, we measure expression differences between source populations at E8.5, for instance between paraxial-derived and lateral plate-derived endothelial progenitors, and compare them with differences between their differentiated descendants at E9.5 (Fig. 4f ). We call genes with origin-associated differences at both times memory genes , and those whose 

> 13

differences are no longer detected at E9.5 erased genes .All convergence events have some memory genes and some erased genes, but the proportions vary across cell types ( Fig. 4g ). Endothelium retains a number of memory genes but erases a far larger share of its origin-specific differences than any of the neuronal types, which retain considerably more. This is consistent with the regional specialization of neurons [75–77]. We find that spatial markers – Hox genes in particular – are retained across every event we examined, including Hoxa7 , Hoxa9 and Hoxb9 in endothelium and spinal cord interneurons and 

Hoxc6 and Hoxc9 in somatic motor neurons. Across these convergence events, cells acquire a shared identity while retaining some transcriptional differences between their origins, consistent with the idea of non-equivalence. The extent and dynamics of transcriptional memory retention and erasure vary between cell types. 

2.6. Reasoning about developmental effects of genetic perturbations 

Lineage inpainting provides a model of developmental dynamics in unperturbed embryos. We next ask if this purely observational model can be used to reason about the effects of genetic perturbations. Intuitively, a model of developmental lineage trees is a good source for reasonable hypotheses about genetic perturbation effects. For any gene, the model shows which progenitor cells express it and what those progenitors give rise to. It also shows the developmental output of similar progenitors that do not express the gene. Together, these can be used to hypothesize an effect of genetic knockout. To test this, we treat our observational model as causal: perturbing a gene alters the states of individual cells, but leaves the state transition rules unchanged, such that the same forward model can be applied to cells that a perturbation has displaced in state space. Under this assumption, we simulate a knockout by projecting perturbed cells to their nearest neighbors that do not express target gene [79], and propagate them forward through the tree, exactly as we do in the unperturbed case. Cells lacking nearby non-expressing observations are considered off-manifold and are not propagated further. We simulate the knockout of each of the 3 ,956 highly variable genes on which the model is fit and categorize perturbations based on which coarse cell type they most severely deplete. Within each category, the most specific depleting perturbations are usually its well-known regulators. In particular, Sox10 is the most specific neural crest-depleting perturbation [80], Foxc1/2 for the paraxial mesoderm [81], Foxa1/2 for the endoderm [82], and so on. These textbook regulators were not chosen manually, but rather emerge from an untargeted screen over all highly variable genes, ordered by cell type depletion specificity. To more broadly test lineage inpainting predictions for genetic knockout effects, we compare the predicted cell type abundances to knockout phenotypes annotated in the Mouse Genome Database (MGD) [83]. We filtered to MGD phenotype annotations indicating abnormal development of a structure for which we have corresponding cell type annotations. This yields 1 ,539 gene to cell type pairs, each of which represents a cell type known to be affected by a defect in the paired gene. We then measure, for each pair, the extent to which genetic knockout is predicted to perturb the paired cell type ( Fig. 5b , Supplemental Fig. S6 ). We find that annotated pairs have a larger fraction of perturbed cells (median 82%) compared with a background of randomly paired cell types and genes (median 40%). Agreement with the MGD annotations varies by tissue ( Fig. 5b ). Median perturbed fraction is high for vascular (91%) and cardiac (85%) cell types but lower for neural cell types (73%). This likely reflects the developmental window of the model: neuronal differentiation is just beginning around E9.0, and many MGD neural phenotypes may arise later. We next ask if these predictions are consistent with human loss-of-function effects, using gnomAD gene constraint scores [84, 85] as a measure of the consequences of genetic perturbation. If the model captures developmental effects that generalize across species, genes more strongly constrained in humans should have more severe predicted effects. We identify the 100 most and least constrained of the 3 ,591 screened genes with human orthologs (LOEUF ≤ 0.22 and ≥ 1.75, respectively), and evaluate their predicted perturbation

2.7. Lineage inpainting predicts the developmental dynamics of Tbx5 -perturbed embryos 

We next ask how the predicted effects of Tbx5 knockout unfold across developmental trajectories. We first ask when the model predicts that trajectories will shift in the Tbx5 knockout, resolved by cell subtype. We find that heart trajectories are affected quite early, many prior to E8.0, while forelimb field trajectories shift much later ( Fig. 5f ). This suggests the cardiac and forelimb effects of Tbx5 arise in separate progenitor populations, rather than through a shared progenitor [86]. This can be appreciated by looking at inferred Tbx5 expression along the tree, where the gene turns on only after clades have become biased to only forelimb or only heart (Fig. 5g ). To test this across all trees, we compute for each heart and forelimb trajectory when it becomes fate-biased (generating only heart or only forelimb descendants) and when it begins to express Tbx5 . Tbx5 turns on after restriction to the heart or forelimb fate, rather than within earlier progenitors that produce both cell types ( Fig. 5h ). Within the heart, the predicted shifts are not uniform. The inflow tract and the atria are strongly shifted, while the outflow tract is much less so ( Fig. 5f ), so posterior cell types are more affected than anterior ones, consistent with prior work [87–89]. We next ask if this pattern persists at a more fine-grained level than cell type labels. We measure shift magnitude as nearest-neighbor distortion , defined for each reference cell as its distance to the nearest perturbed cell, normalized by its distance to the nearest reference cell (Section 4.4.4). We find that the pattern persists at the single-cell level: a higher posterior program score corresponds to a higher predicted nearest-neighbor distortion ( Fig. 5i ).

# 3. Discussion 

Many biological processes unfold over time across communities of cells. However, directly observing changes in transcriptional state over time is typically not possible, as comprehensive measurements of gene expression are destructive. Instead we must reconstruct dynamics from snapshots. Yet the past and future state of a cell are not in general predictable from its current state alone, since cell fate also depends on a cell’s history and the external signals it encounters. In this work, we have shown that lineage information provides a rich source of context for the reconstruction of multicellular dynamics. 

> 18

We established a framework for modeling cell state dynamics across entire developmental trees using lineage context, rather than modeling individual cell trajectories independently. We introduced lineage inpainting, and showed that it can recover non-optimal-transport and context-dependent cell state dynamics. By applying lineage inpainting to mouse embryogenesis data, we resolved transcriptional convergence dynamics inaccessible to alternative approaches. Finally, we showed that despite being fit on observational data alone, lineage inpainting can be used to generate reasonable hypotheses about perturbation effects. In sum, this work takes a step toward what one might call a virtual embryo – a model which can forecast the fates and origins of cells across the embryo and simulate counterfactuals. While we have initially applied lineage inpainting to lineage-traced scRNA-seq, in principle the same modeling approach can extend naturally to include other types of cellular context. With lineage-traced spatial transcrip-tomic readouts [33, 90], one can simply add an additional bias term to the tree-structured transformer ( Box 1 ), using a function of spatial distance rather than lineage distance. Our work raises several threads for future study, in regard to both computational methodology as well as biology. While lineage inpainting is generative in the sense that it samples from a distribution over cell states on a lineage tree, it does not generate the structure of the tree itself and instead takes it as an input. Is it possible to simultaneously generate the lineage tree structure alongside the transcriptional states? Recent work on generative modeling for phylogenetic inference provides early evidence that such a model may be feasible [91, 92]. While lineage inpainting infers cell states on lineage trees, it does not model mechanisms underlying state transitions directly. It does not have any explicit notion of gene regulatory networks, cell signaling, or cell-cell interactions. Is it possible to build mechanistic information into the model, as recent work has explored for cell-level models [93]? This may require extending models to integrate with other measurement modalities, such as ATAC-seq [94], spatial transcriptomics [95], or readouts of signaling pathway activation [96, 97]. Mechanistic models may lead to stronger out-of-distribution generalization for testing counterfactuals. Overall, we believe this work demonstrates the importance of models which move beyond individual cell trajectories to considering multicellular systems across multiple scales. The ideas developed here are more general than embryogenesis alone, and may be useful in the study of cancer, inflammation, infection, regeneration and other multicellular dynamical phenomena.

# 4. Methods 

4.1. Implementing lineage inpainting 

In this section, we outline in more detail the precise model fitting, model architecture, and sampling procedures we implement in lineage inpainting models. We first introduce the general impute-and-sample framework, then the details of our particular implementations of the imputation and sampling step. We describe three technical contributions: (1) a tree-aware attention mechanism inspired by phylogenetic comparative methods [52], (2) a divergence between distributions over complete lineage trees which we use as a loss function for imputation models [98], and (3) a stochastic interpolant model for sampling from distributions over lineage trees [49]. 

4.1.1. The impute-and-sample framework 

Our goal is to fit a model which, given a lineage tree with a single snapshot of states observed, can sample from the distribution of possible complete trees where all states, past and future, are known (recall Fig. 1a ). The data available are incomplete, in the sense that we only ever observe the states at the leaves of lineage trees (Fig. 1b ). Many approaches to fitting such a model can be broken into two components which we refer to as imputation and sampling ( Fig. 1c ). The central idea is that the sampler is a generative model which must be fit using complete lineage trees where all node states are observed. Because our direct observations have states only at the leaves, the sampler must instead be fit on trees where the internal node states are somehow imputed by a separate procedure. Existing methods can be thought of under the umbrella of this impute-and-sample framework. In GENOT [43], for example, the imputer is an entropic optimal-transport coupling between marginals at different timepoints, which defines a distribution over possible ancestor–descendant pairings; the sampler is a cell-level conditional flow-matching model trained to reproduce the cell–cell relationships encoded by this coupling. The impute-and-sample framework is useful for laying out the design space of models. One can imagine many different imputation methods: discrete optimal transport methods, deterministic ancestral reconstructions (such as Felsenstein’s method [52]) or a learnable approach as we will describe below in Section 4.1.3. Similarly, one can imagine many different samplers: a large family of generative models for conditional sampling have been 

> 30

explored in recent years [15, 47, 49, 99]. One could apply these directly and sample states independently, or one could try to sample states across an entire tree jointly, as we will describe below in Sec. 4.1.4. In Supp. S2 we explore many possible pairings of imputers and samplers, and choose our implementation accordingly. 

4.1.2. A tree-aware transformer architecture for lineage trees 

Both the imputation and sampling models we introduce below are built from a shared architectural backbone for processing lineage trees simultaneously. It is an extension of the transformer architecture [57, 58], which maps a set of input tokens to a set of output tokens. Here, each node of a lineage tree corresponds to a token. Beyond this redefinition of the token, we make two core modifications: (1) a biased attention layer which encodes the structure of the lineage tree and (2) a tree-dependent additive displacement for each output token, which provides an inductive bias inspired by classical ideas in phylogenetics about modeling the evolution of states on a tree [52]. 

Encoding tree structure in a biased attention mechanism Transformers are built around the attention mechanism, which, without an added positional embedding, is permutation equivariant. If we were to directly use a transformer (without a positional embedding) to operate on lineage trees, with each cell corresponding to a token, the structure of the lineage tree would be ignored. Instead, the cells would be processed as a “bag of cells” with no internal relational structure. To encode the structure of the lineage tree in the transformer architecture, we instead use a biased attention layer ( Box 1 ) where attention weights receive an additive bias corresponding to a scaled version of the patristic distance. 

An additive displacement for output tokens Rather than using the transformer to directly predict raw output tokens, we instead recursively average child states for ancestral inference, or copy the value of the observed ancestor for descendant inference. This allows the transformer to predict a residual on top of this displacement state for each token, encoding an inductive bias into the architecture. Our choice of this displacement draws inspiration from classical approaches in phylogenetics. Felsenstein’s phylogenetic comparative method [52] as-sumes that traits evolve along phylogenetic trees following a Brownian motion. That is, across every edge of the tree, the change in the trait is sampled from a Gaussian distribution with variance proportional to the length of the edge (the amount of time elapsed). Given the observed states at the leaves of the tree, the expected state of any unobserved node under the Brownian model is a weighted average of its observed relatives. Conditioning on its descendant subtree, the posterior mean of an ancestral node is a variance-weighted mean of its descendant states. This is Felsenstein’s ancestral reconstruction, computed by a bottom-up recursion from the leaves of the tree [52]. We compute a simpler, unweighted mean at every node from the anchor states and the topology and take it as a per-node displacement bv , a first guess at each cell before any learning. The network then predicts only each node’s deviation from bv , a residual far easier to learn than the state outright, and we add bv back at the output. Section S4 gives the exact recursion. One forward pass runs as follows. Each node’s current state is linearly projected to a hidden token, then we add learned embeddings of the node’s lineage time tv and a marker distinguishing a clamped observed anchor from a node to be filled in. The tokens pass through a stack of attention layers, where the patristic-distance bias enters. As in graph transformers that encode pairwise or structural information inside attention [100], we add the patristic distance to each pair’s attention logit before the softmax following ALiBi [59], scaled by a weight learned separately for each layer and head (Box 1). In practice, the model develops large negative weights in early layers that concentrate attention on close relatives, and progressively less negative weights in later layers with positive weights only in the last layers. A final head maps each node back to state space, where we add bv

back to the output.

Matching only the temporal marginals (the population of cell states at each time) is not enough to learn developmental dynamics [18]. In practice, we use an imputation loss which aims to match three types of distributions: (1) marginals at individual developmental times, (2) lineage paths across multiple times, and (3) common-ancestor triples relating descendants of a shared ancestor. Our second loss component enforces that the distributions of individual cell trajectories agree across the two collections. For each completed tree, we sample a lineage path, recording the sequence of cell states encountered at the shared comparison times. We concatenate these states into a single vector—for example, ( xt0 , x t1 , x t2 ). An imputer that samples ancestors independently of their descendants can have the correct marginals while failing to preserve dependence along the lineage. With more than two observation times, the same construction matches the joint distribution of states along the lineage. Path matching constrains the “depth” relationship between states along a trajectory, but it omits the “breadth” relationship between descendants. This “breadth” dimension can also carry dependence. For ex-ample, a cell’s descendants may be conditionally dependent given the ancestral state, so that knowing both the ancestor and one descendant is informative about other descendants (e.g., due to interaction between sis-ter cells). 4.1.4. Learning a generative sampling model 

The imputation model produces completed trees by filling in ancestral states in a single forward pass. We want a generative model that, given a tree observed at a single snapshot, can sample the unobserved states of each cell’s ancestors and descendants. This requires a second sampling model, Sϕ, which we train by taking the complete trees produced by the imputation step and randomly choosing a developmental time to condition on, then masking out all the other nodes and learning to fill in the states conditional on this selected snapshot. The sampling model uses the same tree-aware transformer architecture but generates the masked states through a multi-step denoising procedure, conditioning on the retained snapshot throughout. This builds on diffusion, flow, and stochastic interpolant based generative modeling techniques which have seen great success in computer vision [47–49, 60, 99, 103]. For a completed tree ̂ Xi = ( Ti,̂x i), we choose a random developmental time and treat the cells alive at that time as the observable snapshot. 4.1.5. Jointly training the imputation and sampling models 

The imputation and sampling models are trained simultaneously rather than in separate stages. Each training step draws two observed trees whose capture times differ by half a day, d, d + 0 .5 so that the imputation loss can compare their completions over the times they share. We take up to 2 ,048 subtrees from each, rooted at a common time chosen uniformly from [ d−0.5, d ]. Subtrees with more than 128 nodes are subsampled by randomly dropping leaves. At every training step, we impute a batch of observed trees and compute the imputation loss of Algorithm 1, and the same completed trees are then used to train the sampler. The two losses are added and a single optimizer step updates both parameters θ and ϕ at once. Training the sampler on completed trees creates a tension. If we train directly on imputed states, the sampler may learn to reproduce imperfect samples from the imputation model. But using the imputed completion directly has an important advantage: the sampling loss can propagate back through the imputer. This gives the two models an autoencoder-like relationship, encouraging the imputer to produce completions from which the sampler can reconstruct the measured cells from a single snapshot. In practice, this additional signal makes the completions sharper and more lineage-coherent than the imputation loss alone (Section S2). We balance these objectives by alternating between two training steps. On one step, we leave the imputed completion unchanged, condition the sampler on an internal snapshot, and compute the loss over the resulting tree. The imputed internal states still enter the sampler as context, so gradients can flow through them into the imputer, while the sampler loss is only computed on the leaves. On the other step, we select the conditioning snapshot and leave those states at their imputed values, then replace the other imputed targets with their nearest measured cells at nearby developmental times. These replacements give the sampler real-cell targets at internal times. Although the nearest-neighbor targets are detached from the imputer, retaining the imputed conditioning snapshot preserves a gradient path back into the imputer. Alternating the two steps lets the sampler train on measured-cell targets throughout development while retaining the gradient flowing from sampler through the imputer. The imputation loss weights its marginal, path and common-ancestor components 1, 0 .25 and 0 .25, and the marginal component is compared against up to 4 ,096 measured cells randomly sampled at each comparison time. We train for 20 ,000 steps with Adam and keep an exponential moving average. 

4.2. Model evaluation 4.2.1. Synthetic benchmarks 

Synthetic benchmarks provide a setting where the ground-truth dynamics are known. Every synthetic dataset described here is a population of trees, with each tree observed at a single time, matching the structure of the real data. Two of the four benchmark settings are fully synthetic and are built from what we call branch-modified stochastic differential equations (described below). The other two are semi-synthetic, built by wiring real mouse-embryo cell states into prescribed lineage motifs [63]. 

Branch-modified SDEs. We define branch-modified stochastic differential equation as SDEs whose parame-ters vary across the branches of a lineage tree. In our synthetic datasets, a cell’s latent state is one-dimensional and follows an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process dx = θ(μ − x) dt + σ dW , reducing to a Brownian motion at θ = 0. As in a conventional branching process, cells divide by branching into two daughters, each of which starts from 

> 35

its parent’s final state, so state is inherited continuously across divisions. At certain divisions the daughter’s attractor μ is set to a value determined by its position in the lineage tree – a branch modification of the SDE parameters. Through the branch modified SDEs, we are able to construct processes where a cell’s future then depends not only on its current state, but also on its position in the lineage tree. Trajectories are integrated by Euler-Maruyama: xi+1 = xi + θ(μ − xi) ∆ t + σ√∆t ξ i with ξi ∼ N (0 , 1) and ∆t is the integration timestep. The diffusion is additive and state-independent. To vary the ambient dimension we lift the one-dimensional latent to Rd by y = xw + ε, with w a fixed random unit vector shared across trees and ε ∼ N (0, (0 .1/√d)2Id

).

Synthetic topology benchmark (crossing versus non-crossing). Our first benchmark tests whether a model uses lineage structure. It involves a pair of processes – referred to as crossing and coherent in Fig. 2 –with similar marginals but distinct joint distributions. The trees have nine generations. The root starts at zero, and cells are Brownian ( θ = 0, μ = 0) until the split generation 5, where the two daughters of each cell switch to Ornstein–Uhlenbeck dynamics with θ = 2 .0, σ = 0 .1, and attractors −1 and +1 respectively. At generation 7 each family splits again. In the non-crossing process, the −1 family is mapped to ( −1.5, −0.5) and the +1 family is mapped to (0 .5, 1.5), while the crossing process maps them to ( −1.5, 0.5) and ( −0.5, 1.5) respectively. Each branching interval (i.e., time between cell division) is 1 .0 with 100 integration steps. The 40 training trees are binary to depth 9 with 512 leaves each, observed at one of the times 5 to 9. We test various dimensionalities across d ∈ { 1, 4, 16 , 64 } with a separately trained model at each dimension, and held-out evaluation uses a single further realization of the process observed at every time. 

Synthetic context benchmark. This benchmark tests whether a model can leverage the states of related cells across a subtree. A progenitor commits stochastically to one of two fates early in the tree. For several generations afterwards the committed fate biases each descendant’s state only weakly, by an amount that becomes small relative to the noise as σ grows, so a single cell’s state barely reveals its fate; only later do the two fates separate sharply. The bias is shared across a clone, so averaging over related cells accumulates it and exposes the latent fate. The trees have ten generations. The root starts at zero, and cells are Brownian until the split generation 3, where each cell draws one of two fates independently, with probability 0 .75 for the positive fate, and acquires the corresponding weak primed attractor μ ∈ { +0 .2, −0.2} under Ornstein–Uhlenbeck dynamics with θ = 2 .0. At generation 9, the attractor of each primed family switches to μ ∈ { +1 .0, −1.0}. Each branch interval is 1 .0with 200 integration steps, the 200 training trees are binary to depth 10 with 1 ,024 leaves each, observed at one of the times 5 to 10. The evaluated noise scales scan across σ ∈ { 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8}.The context-scaling evaluation predicts the transition from time 9 to time 10 given k ∈ { 1, 2, 4, 8} related cells as input. A clone of size k shares a common ancestor at time 9 − log 2 k, so larger k means deeper shared ancestry. Results are averaged over five held-out process realizations.

We evaluate models fit to the lineage-traced atlas with six metrics, shown in Fig. 3b . Lower is better throughout. We will next describe these metrics and their concrete implementation. Three metrics involve making some assumptions about developmental dynamics. Germ-layer crossing rate

Clonal distortion measures the cycle-consistency of a model: given a clade, the extent to which it can be recovered by simulating reverse in time and then forward in time. Linkage distortion measures the extent to which lineage relationships between cell types is consistent in simulated data and real measurements. We define all six of these metrics in detail below. Marginal distortion. 4.2.3. Baselines 

We compare against generative models of developmental dynamics that span how much lineage structure they use. Our multimarginal flow-matching baseline [48] learns from independent samples of consecutive time marginals and ignores the tree entirely. Its deterministic flow maps each input state to a single output. GENOT with Sinkhorn couplings [43] learns the same kind of transition but couples consecutive snapshots by entropic optimal transport in state space, again without reference to the tree. GENOT with fused Gromov–Wasserstein couplings [42, 43] fuses the state-space cost with a tree-distance cost, so the coupling it fits does use lineage structure, though only through this pairwise cost rather than as context the model attends over. To make this the fairest test of the optimal-transport approach, we scale the fused coupling to the full lineage-traced atlas—which a dense solve cannot achieve—by solving it clone by clone, in minibatches drawn from one source lineage and one target lineage, with a constant-time tree-distance oracle (GENOT-cbFGW, detailed below). On the synthetic benchmarks, where the trees are small, the fused coupling is solved on randomly paired trees (Section 4.2.1). All three are trained on the same atlas and time grid as the lineage inpainting model, with optimal-transport couplings fit between adjacent grid times in both directions. 

The optimal-transport couplings. Here we describe our implementations of two optimal transport-based baselines. First is a gene-expression based model which gives a generative model consistent with entropic OT couplings, (GENOT-Sinkhorn) and a similar method using a fused Gromov–Wasserstein coupling (GENOT-FGW) [42, 43], which additionally encodes lineage tree structure through the Gromov term. Both choose the cross-time correspondence by a transport cost instead of learning it. A standard implementation of GENOT-FGW does not scale to massive datasets, so below, we provide a modified implementation for the lineage-traced atlas. We refer to this method as being clonally batched (GENOT-cbFGW). 

Scaling GENOT to million-cell atlases. GENOT-Sinkhorn uses entropic optimal transport to first couple cell states in the scVI embedding, then fits a flow-matching model to sample transitions with the coupling [43]. This approach can be minibatched, and easily scales to very large datasets. However, it does not consider the lineage tree information. The fused Gromov–Wasserstein coupling instead determines a coupling using two cost terms solved together as one entropic fused-GW problem: a displacement term (similar to the Sinkhorn described above), and a structure term that compares two intra-domain matrices of pairwise tree distances, one among the source cells and one among the target cells. We weight the two terms equally. Naively running the fused-GW solver on the lineage traced atlas is not feasible. A dense fused-GW solver forms the full intra-lineage distance matrices, quadratic in the number of cells, which caps it at tens of thousands of cells. Minibatching is the natural fix, but this is not straightforward. The intra-domain cost compares the tree distance within a pair of source cells against the tree distance within a pair of target cells, and a tree distance exists only within one lineage tree, so a minibatch drawn at random across the atlas leaves the cross-clone entries undefined. We instead minibatch by lineage tree: each iteration pairs one source lineage with one target lineage, solves the fused-GW problem on cells sampled from each, so that both intra-domain matrices hold within-lineage distances, and feeds source–target pairs sampled from the coupling to one flow-matching step. The per-iteration cost is then fixed by the minibatch size rather than by the size of the atlas. Even with this adjustment, another scalability issue remains: the minibatch still needs the pairwise tree distances among its sampled cells, and recomputing paths for every minibatch would reintroduce the dependence on lineage tree size. We instead precompute, once per lineage of N cells, with a constant-time tree-distance oracle 

> 40

using an O(N log N ) preprocessing by a standard Euler-tour and sparse-table range-minimum construction. Each minibatch’s distance matrix is then filled by a fixed number of constant-time queries, the number of cells enters only the one-time oracle building step, and the coupling can run at the scale of the atlas, over a million cells. 

GENOT-Sinkhorn. Each training iteration samples a random adjacent transition between consecutive obser-vation times (i.e., every 0.5 days) in either direction and draws a minibatch of 512 source and 512 target cells, then solves an entropic optimal transport problem between them in the scVI embedding with a squared-Euclidean cost and regularization ε = 5 × 10 −2. We normalize the coupling to a distribution over pairs and sample 512 source-to-target pairs from it. We then take a single flow-matching step, conditioning the velocity field on the source state and the two endpoint times. The velocity field model uses a multilayer perceptron with three hidden layers of width 1024. We train for 5 ,000 iterations with Adam at learning rate 10 −4, and at inference transport each source cell with 5 noise draws. 

GENOT-cbFGW. The fused coupling uses an inner Sinkhorn solver at the same regularization ε = 5 × 10 −2,with the displacement and structure terms weighted equally. Following the lineage-minibatching construction above, each iteration pairs one source lineage with one target lineage and solves the fused-GW problem on 512 cells sampled from each, with the within-lineage distance matrices filled by the constant-time oracle. The network and the remaining training hyperparameters match the Sinkhorn variant. On the synthetic benchmarks, where every tree is small, the fused coupling is solved once on a random pairing of trees instead (Section 4.2.1). 

4.3. Data and preprocessing 4.3.1. The lineage-traced atlas 

We model a mouse-embryo atlas of roughly four million cells spanning embryonic day E7.5 to E10.0. About 1 .4million of these are lineage-traced cells from Colgan et al. [36]. The remainder are non-lineage-traced cells, both host cells from chimeric embryos in the same study and cells from a separate scRNA-seq atlas [9]. The trees are reconstructed with branch length (i.e. node time) estimates in [36] and are considered here as observed: each lineage record is a rooted tree T in which every node already carries a continuous developmental time, derived from molecular clock-based branch length estimation [36]. Because sequencing is destructive, a tree is measured at a single capture time, so its observed cells are exactly its leaves, the only nodes with a measured state xv . The earlier ancestors are the unobserved nodes of the tree the model completes; the cells that would have followed lie beyond the capture time, and every analysis that samples descendants does so on the topology of a tree observed at the later time (Section 4.1.4). We summarize a cell’s state xv ∈ R50 by the 50-dimensional scVI embedding of its transcriptome [64]. We fit one scVI model to raw counts across the whole atlas with the data source (lineage-tracing study versus reference atlas) as a categorical covariate, so the embedding is shared and batch-corrected across the two sources; gene selection is described below and the model configuration is given in Section S4.1. Cells are annotated hierarchically at four nested levels, from finest to coarsest: cell subtype, cell type, lineage, and germ layer. Observed leaves keep their atlas annotations; an imputed ancestor carries the annotation of the observed cell it is coupled to (Section 4.4.1), and cells of the untraced atlas are annotated by nearest-neighbor transfer from the traced cells (below). This hierarchy supplies the germ-layer and cell-type labels used by the evaluation metrics (Section 4.2.2). 

Gene selection. We start from the genes called highly variable in the lineage-traced data that are present in both data sources. To remove batch-related features that merely separate the two data sources, we normalize each cell to 10 4 counts, take logarithms, and remove the top 250 differentially expressed genes per source (Wilcoxon test between sources), leaving 3 ,956 genes.

Annotation transfer. Cells of the untraced atlas are labeled by an exact L2 nearest-neighbor search in the 50-dimensional embedding against the annotated traced cells, using the k = 15 nearest neighbors. At each of the four label levels (cell subtype, cell type, lineage, and germ layer), we take a majority vote and call the level confident when the top label holds at least a 0 .7 fraction of the neighbors; the transferred annotation is the finest confident level, or “ambiguous” when no level is confident. 4.4. Computational analysis methods 4.4.1. Sankey transition maps 

We summarize the cell-type level transitions learned by a model using a Sankey map. For each time window, we first fully inpaint the lineage records using the sampler of Section 4.1.4. Each sampled internal state is then snapped to its nearest observed cell, allowing us to assign a cell type label. This can equivalently be thought of as a nearest-neighbor annotation of cell types. Cell type assignments whose nearest-neighbor distance exceeds a cutoff—the 80th percentile of all internal-node nearest-neighbor distances pooled across trees—are filtered out. We then convert these completed trees into adjacent-time transition pairs. Walking from root to leaf, if a retained representative p at time t and a retained representative c at time t + ∆ t are both available, the path contributes one pair ( p, c, t, t + ∆ t). In the mouse transition maps, ∆ t = 0 .5 embryo days is the natural time interval since that is how the data are sampled. The pairs are counted by time-resolved labels (label , t ), counted at germ-layer resolution at E7.5, lineage resolution at E8.0, and cell-type resolution from E8.5 on. An edge is retained if it has at least five supporting pairs and its shares of the target node’s incoming pairs and of the source node’s outgoing pairs sum to at least 0.2.

4.4.2. Context scaling 

The context-scaling evaluations of Fig. 3c–f ask how the accuracy of an inferred ancestor or descendant changes with the context, or size of the input clade. Both are cycle-consistency tests on subtrees of the atlas whose root falls between E7.0 and E9.0 and which carry between 3 and 20 leaves (at least 6 for descendant inference), with at most 100 focal nodes drawn per terminal cell type. For ancestral inference we fix a focal leaf and reveal successively larger clades around it, moving up its ancestors as far as one and a half embryonic days above the leaf. At each level the clade’s leaves are clamped and the model infers the subtree’s root. That root alone is then clamped and every leaf of the subtree is regenerated below it, conditioned only on the root. The reconstruction quality metric is the energy distance between the regenerated and the measured leaves of the subtree. We measure context size by the time from the focal leaf back to the most recent common ancestor of the revealed leaves (grouped into quarter day bins). For descendant inference the focal node is an internal node with at least three descendant leaves and at least one cousin. We fill every internal state of the subtree with the sampler from its clamped leaves and take the distinct sets of cells alive at 60 times swept through the subtree, each holding an ancestor of the focal node, at most a day above it, with some of its cousins. Clamping the imputed states of such a set and regenerating the leaves below asks how much the cousin information improves reconstruction of the focal node’s descendants: the score is the energy distance between the regenerated and the measured descendants of the focal node, and 

> 43

context is the number of cousins in the set (up to 8). The GENOT baselines take no context, and as such are represented with flat lines. For 400 focal cells per panel, one leaf is taken back to the root’s or the focal node’s time with a single draw and forward again with five draws, and the regenerated cloud is scored against the same measured leaves. The per-type panels ( Fig. 3d,f ) show error by cell type, excluding blood and extraembryonic types, for cell types with at least 10 ,000 leaves and at least three evaluations at every context size. 4.4.4. Perturbation analysis 

We simulate a knockout by redistributing cells in the scVI state space, and running forward-in-time inference using the sampler. The core assumption is that removing a gene displaces individual cells but leaves the transition rules intact, so the model fit on unperturbed embryos can be applied to displaced cells. Below we describe the details of how we repurpose our models to simulate genetic perturbations. To knock out a gene g we replace a cell by the nearest observed cell that does not express g, and then let the model propagate that cell’s descendants as it would in an unperturbed record. Every number reported is a difference between two rollouts of the same lineage records under the same model, one with the cell states redistributed and one without. It describes how the learned dynamics respond to the shifts in cell state, but does not address scenarios where perturbations shift cells to unnatural states (i.e., those not observed in the reference embryos). Each perturbation prediction begins from trees observed at a later timepoint T (e.g. E9.5). We first impute the internal nodes of the tree using the sampler, giving a reference state at every internal node. Then, starting from the internal nodes at a simulation start time (e.g., E7.5), we run forward-in-time inference with the model, restricting the model to parts of the state space where g is not expressed. At the current time of the simulation, we first identify all cells which express g. We then project these g-on cells to their nearest g-off neighbors in the scVI state space. Then, with this remodeled snapshot, we run the lineage inpainting model forward. We then repeat this process at every successive time step of a pre-selected grid. We use timesteps of 0.5 days across all our simulations. The knockout is enforced at every time the simulation crosses, not only at the start: a lineage that the model pushes back into expressing g is knocked out again. A node whose representative cell is moved is marked perturbed, and so is everything below it. Because the topology is held fixed, the analysis can only rearrange cells among the states and lineages seen in normal development: it says nothing about effects on proliferation, cell death, or tree topology. The nearest-neighbor projection step can carry a node far from any cell that resembles it. In these cases, we take the view that model predictions will be unreliable. We therefore record, at every node that moved, how far it moved, relative to the local spacing of the observed cells around it. That is, the distance from its assigned cell to its own nearest neighbor. We consider a node off-manifold when it moved farther than a fixed multiple of that spacing, and a readout node is off-manifold when any node on its path is. In practice we use an off-manifold ratio of 1.1. When a node drifts off manifold, we treat the trajectory as “frozen” in its prior location in the state space. The genome-wide screen knocks out each gene in turn in the E9.0 records of one replicate embryo, starting from E7.5, with 16 denoising steps. The heart screen of Fig. 5d,e takes the 21 genes of a curated congenital heart disease panel [78] that are among the highly variable genes and runs a full knockout on the same records, with 64 denoising steps. Both are read out at E9.0.

S1.1. Additional results on synthetic and semi-synthetic benchmarks Decomposing error across transitions. In Fig. 2a,b , we show that lineage inpainting models are able to leverage tree topology to correctly infer transitions. The metrics we show are only for the transition where differences between models are very distinct (7 → 8). Here we show the rest of the transitions ( Supplemental Fig. S1a ). Decomposing performance across transitions points toward the source of multimarginal FM’s poor perfor-mance. Because this multimarginal FM baseline fits a deterministic cell-state ODE (akin to [12, 13]), it is unable to model bifurcations. So at transitions where the output is unimodal from a certain state (6 → 7, 8 → 9), multimarginal FM performs well. But in the other transitions where there is a bifurcation (5 → 6, 7 → 8) it performs poorly. 

Additional baselines (including non-generative approaches). Throughout the main text, we benchmark only against generative baselines: multimarginal FM, GENOT-Sinkhorn and GENOT-FGW. Here, we also com-pare to two established non-generative baselines. In order to compare these to generative baselines, we use a nearest neighbor projection. To get the ancestor (or similarly, descendant) of an input cell, we identify the closest cell in the training dataset, and output its discretely coupled ancestor. In a similar spirit, we also compare to lineage inpainting implemented with two “non-parametric” imputation models. The first is mean pooling, or in other words the “vanilla” ancestral reconstruction which is used as the additive baseline in the tree-structured transformer. The second is what we refer to as NN-snapped imputer, which replaces the mean-pooled ancestral reconstruction with its nearest observed cell. Including these baselines in the semi-synthetic experiments, we find that all four baselines perform more poorly than lineage inpainting with a learnable imputation function across both semi-synthetic benchmarks. WaddingtonOT [11] and moslin [42] exhibit stronger performance for recovering the correct cell type coupling over their generative counterparts. This suggests that the poor performance of the GENOT models is not always attributable to the optimal transport coupling itself. This has a clear explanation in the context-copying benchmark. GENOT models are fundamentally incapable of recovering this, as they never receive the cousin context (which determines the ancestral state) as input. Even provided with the ground truth coupling, a flow matching model fit to generate ancestral states conditional on a single descendant state will be unable to correctly match the coupling, since the true state depends on the state of the cell’s cousins. 

Consistency across performance metrics. Throughout our synthetic evaluations, we use performance met-rics which evaluate the extent to which generated states match the discrete clusters of the true states. In the semi-synthetic experiments, these correspond to cell types, and in the synthetic experiments, these correspond to the attractors of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equations. Here, in Supplemental Fig. S1d , we compare the fate accuracy to distributional divergences which do not discretize the state space. We find that broadly the two metrics are in agreement ( ρ ≈ − 0.63 across multiple divergences). The non-parametric imputation models (mean pooling and nearest-neighbor snapping) are outliers: they can achieve strong fate accuracy without closely matching the correct distribution in state space. This is understandable: the hierarchical means can be close to the correct cell type, but far from any realistic cell.

# S3. Supplementary results: theoretical analysis of alignment loss 

To learn an imputation model, we compare distributions of cell states and lineage features pooled across collections of completed trees. In particular, we use a distributional divergence to compare the marginal distributions of states, the distribution of states along ancestral paths, and the distribution of states along ancestor-descendant-descendant triples. The goal of this section is to illustrate the principles underlying this procedure. We start by considering how we can compare two state-decorated trees when we do not know the corre-spondences between nodes, showing that this reduces to a distributional comparison for any pair of trees. We then show how to lift that construction over pairs of trees to pairs of distributions over trees. This gives us a principled whole-tree discrepancy and connects the energy distance we focus on with analogous divergences, like the Wasserstein-1 distance. The final section here concretely traces the trade-offs between this whole-tree loss and our three component loss. The central idea is that, because we have few trees and because the whole-tree loss is very high-dimensional, we can actually do a better job at measuring the discrepancy between two collections of trees by restricting ourselves to these lower dimensional components. This statistical efficiency comes at a price: two whole-tree distributions can agree on all the features but disagree on “long-range” dependencies that the features do not measure. In the binary setting, we make this tradeoff exact by characterizing the set of distributions over trees left unresolved by the features and comparing the bias and variance of the resulting estimate of the whole-tree energy. We find regimes in which the projected feature statistic has lower MSE for the whole-tree energy despite this; as tree depth grows, the computational advantage of the feature representation increases. 

S3.1. Comparing unordered trees

S3.3. From whole-tree laws to low-order features 

To make this tradeoff exact, we now restrict our attention to complete binary trees with states encoded as −1and +1. This is essentially a generalized Ising model on trees, where we allow the interactions to vary over arbitrarily long ranges on the tree. The binary setting is useful since it allows us to write all of our analysis as functions of the full probability mass table, which is finite and discrete. 

> 67

Our three feature families break up each tree in complementary directions. The generation marginal keeps the state distribution at each time, the path triple captures a “depth” summary along each ancestral lineage, and the common-ancestor triple is a “breadth” summary across branches below every shared ancestor. We pool these feature samples at each time, replacing the whole-tree comparison with repeated comparisons of these low-dimensional feature distributions. These features let us break the large tree into smaller pieces, effectively reducing the variance of our divergence. But the central issue is that the feature laws cannot distinguish all whole-tree laws. The goal of this section is not to show that these features identify the whole-tree law (they almost certainly do not) but to quantify how much whole-tree information they resolve relative to their variance gains. Let P0 be seven independent fair signs, with pP0 (x) = 2 −7. The nice thing about the binary setting is that we can exactly describe the compatible set. After child-order symmetry, a whole-tree law is a point in a 41-dimensional simplex. In moment coordinates, the feature tables (marginal, path, and common-ancestor) fix 13 combinations and leave the remaining 28 free. For any law P , the compatible set CF (P ) is the intersection of the simplex with the 28-dimensional affine space through P spanned by the omitted combinations. We can make this a bit more interpretable by focusing on just the four grandchildren. The features observe only one- and two-node marginals on these grandchildren, so they tell us that the four signs are pairwise independent and fair. Any distribution over the grandchildren is a point in the fifteen-dimensional probability simplex, but the distributions compatible with the observed trees occupy one slice of the simplex. These features fix the one-and two-node moments, so only the only freedom is in the two grandchild combinations, the third-order moment (which, somewhat counterintuitively, is the same for every triple due to child-order symmetry), and the four-node parity, so the compatible grandchild laws form a two-parameter family in which the mass of an assignment depends only on its number of positive signs. This gives us a smaller simplex that we can use to visualize the compatible set. S4.1. Transcriptional state embedding 

We fit scVI to raw counts with a negative-binomial likelihood [64]. Data source is a categorical decoder covariate, and gene-specific dispersion is shared across sources. We use the posterior mean of the Gaussian latent state as 

xv ; training does not use early stopping. 

S4.2. Inputs and embeddings 

We treat each node as one token in the transformer. Observed nodes retain their measured states, while hidden nodes receive independent standard Gaussian noise at hidden nodes in Iθ , and the noisy residual xsv − bv at hidden nodes in Sϕ. The projected inputs are combined with embeddings of developmental time and a token representing whether the node is masked or observed; for the sampler Sϕ we add an interpolant-time embedding as well. Developmental times and patristic distances are in embryo days, without further normalization. 

S4.3. Transformer layers 

Each layer applies attention and a feed-forward sublayer, with layer normalization before each and a residual connection around each. Patristic-distance bias has a learned scale for each layer and head, initialized to zero. Time embeddings, feed-forward sublayers and output layers use GELU (Gaussian error linear unit) between their linear layers. 

S4.4. Residual output 

After final layer normalization, the output layers predict a clean-state residual, which we add to bv . At an observed anchor, bv equals the observed state. Below an anchor, it copies the nearest observed ancestor’s state; above the anchors, it recursively averages the child displacements with equal weight per child. Observed states remain fixed throughout. 

S4.5. Numerical sampling 

We use forward Euler updates for CFM and Euler–Maruyama updates for VP-SDE, keeping the observed states fixed. For N sampling steps on the uniform grid sj = j/N , the model is evaluated at j = 0 , . . . , N − 1, and the
