## ABSTRACT 

Single-cell RNA foundation models (scFMs) are increasingly used for in-silico bi-ological discovery, yet predictive performance alone does not reveal whether their decisions rely on biologically meaningful signals or model-specific shortcuts. We systematically investigate post-hoc attribution of downstream predictions across two tasks: cell-type annotation using pretrained Cell2Sentence and scGPT mod-els, and perturbation prediction using scGPT+STATE in a controlled setting. For cell annotation, we use attribution to assess whether predictions rely on estab-lished marker genes and how their relevance varies with model architecture, ex-pression rank, and the surrounding cellular context. For perturbation prediction, we compare attribution patterns with known regulatory ground-truth in a synthetic setting to assess what they reveal about the model’s learned computations. Across these tasks, attribution reveals biological feature use, contextual interactions, con-tributions from different input pathways, learning progression, associations with prediction quality, and potential pitfalls that are not fully captured by standard performance metrics. Experiments with complete and limited regulatory knowl-edge further demonstrate that conclusions drawn from attribution depend strongly on the choice of biological reference, baseline, and null model. Together, these findings highlight the value of attribution as a flexible post-hoc diagnostic for understanding model behavior, identifying potential issues, and informing the de-velopment of scFMs. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

The development of single-cell foundation models (scFMs) is transforming biological research, with the promise of accelerating discovery through in silico experimentation (Jiang et al., 2026). How-ever, extrapolation remains challenging. Generalizing beyond the training distribution requires mod-els to capture biological mechanisms that remain stable across changing conditions rather than re-lying on spurious correlations (Tejada-Lapuerta et al., 2025). This motivates methods for assessing whether the internal computations underlying scFM predictions are biologically plausible, both to better understand model failures and to evaluate whether successful predictions arise for biologically meaningful reasons (Tejada-Lapuerta et al., 2025; Dimitrov et al., 2026). Interpretability provides a natural framework for such analysis. Beyond supporting model debug-ging and trust (R¨ auker et al., 2023), inspecting learned representations and computations can provide evidence for learned internal models of the underlying domain (Li et al., 2025) and may itself serve as a tool for scientific discovery (Freiesleben et al., 2024). Existing interpretability studies of scFMs have primarily analyzed intermediate representation spaces, identifying biological information en-coded in cell or gene embeddings (see Section 2). However, the presence of biological information in a representation does not reveal which input features support that information or how it is used by 1arXiv:2611.02335v2 [cs.LG] 3 Nov 2026 downstream predictors built on top of the representation. A more direct analysis therefore requires linking downstream predictions back to biologically meaningful input features. In this work, we use attribution-based explainability to assess whether downstream predictors built on scFM representations rely on biologically meaningful signals consistent with established biologi-cal knowledge, as illustrated in Fig. 1. We study two common applications of increasing complexity. For cell type annotation, we assess whether predictions rely on established cell-type marker genes beyond confounding factors such as expression rank, and use symbolic explanations to examine how the relevance of marker genes shared by closely related cell subtypes changes in different contex-tual settings. For perturbation prediction, we assess how closely attribution patterns align with the underlying gene regulatory network, providing a diagnostic of what the model has learned. Because reliable regulatory ground-truth is generally unavailable in real perturbation datasets, we construct a synthetic setting with a fully specified causal regulatory system, enabling direct comparison between model explanations and the mechanisms used to generate the data. Our contributions are threefold: (1) We provide the first systematic study of post-hoc scFM explainability that focuses on dis-secting downstream task predictions rather than only analyzing information encoded in intermediate representations or attention patterns. (2) We develop attribution-based analyses that connect downstream predictions to established biological knowledge at both the level of individual genes and higher-order contextual in-teractions (Section 3). (3) We demonstrate that attribution-based explainability can serve as a practical tool for ana-lyzing gene regulatory structure learned by scFMs, enabling direct evaluation of whether model attributions recover known regulatory relationships in a controlled perturbation set-ting (Section 4). 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Post-hoc interpretability of biological foundation models. Interpretability has been increasingly used to investigate the biological information learned by foundation models. In protein language models, supervised probing of learned embeddings has been used to evaluate the decodability of biological attributes at both residue and protein levels (Li et al., 2024; Whitfield et al., 2026), while intervention-based analyses have revealed three-dimensional contacts even in models trained only on protein sequences (Nainani et al., 2025; Candido et al., 2026). Unsupervised sparse autoen-coders have further been used to decompose protein-model representations into more interpretable features (Rives et al., 2021; Adams et al., 2025; Simon & Zou, 2025; Gujral et al., 2025; Nainani et al., 2025; Candido et al., 2026), revealing features associated with structural, physicochemical, and functional properties. Beyond proteins, interpretability analyses of DNA foundation-model em-beddings have also uncovered phylogenetic structure (Pearce et al., 2025). 

Post-hoc interpretability of single-cell foundation models. Within transcriptomics, recent work has largely examined the representations learned by scFMs. Sparse autoencoders have uncovered cell- and gene-level features associated with known pathways and marker genes (Claye et al., 2025; Pedrocchi et al., 2025), while zero-shot analyses have studied how embeddings organize biological properties such as cell identity relative to technical factors (Kedzierska et al., 2025). Attribution methods have further estimated the influence of individual genes on learned representations (Zhao et al., 2026; Gold et al., 2026) or interpretable features derived from them (Claye et al., 2025). Attention weights have also been used to rank genes for annotated cell types (Yang et al., 2022) and to investigate recovery of regulatory interactions (Kendiukhov, 2026). However, attention is output-agnostic and captures only one step of the computation, so it need not reflect what drives a prediction (Jain & Wallace, 2019; Wiegreffe & Pinter, 2019). These studies show that scFM representations encode biologically meaningful information, but largely focus on intermediate representations. In contrast, we explain downstream task predictions 

by propagating a chosen output through the full network with LRP (Bach et al., 2015), assigning each input feature a signed relevance for that prediction. While Keyl et al. ## 3 CELL TYPE CLASSIFICATION 

Cell typing is a fundamental task in single-cell analysis, where each cell is assigned to a biolog-ically meaningful cell type based on its gene-expression profile. Although single-cell foundation models can achieve strong cell-type classification performance, predictive accuracy alone does not reveal what biological information the models use to distinguish cell populations. We therefore use cell typing as our first interpretability setting, asking whether model predictions are driven by biologically meaningful signals. 3.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Datasets. We curate two benchmarks from the CZ CELLxGENE Census (CZI Cell Science Pro-gram et al., 2025) with well-characterized molecular signatures, comprising peripheral blood cells and adult human brain nuclei. Each sample is represented by its gene expression profile. The blood dataset has a two-level label hierarchy, with four coarse cell types (T cells, B cells, natural killer cells, and monocytes), further divided into 22 subtypes. The brain dataset contains seven broad cell types, including excitatory and inhibitory neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, oligodendro-cyte precursor cells, microglia/macrophages, and vascular/perivascular cells. Both datasets contain 3∼61K samples, and are split into train, validation, and test sets using a 70/15/15 ratio. Further dataset details are provided in Appendix A. 

Models and context settings. We evaluate two families of pretrained single-cell foundation models throughout this study, Cell2Sentence (C2S; Levine et al., 2024; Rizvi et al., 2026) and scGPT (Cui et al., 2024). C2S models are adapted from pretrained autoregressive language models (here, Pythia-410M, Pythia-1B, Gemma2-2B). They represent cells as sequences of top-K genes sorted by expression value, tokenized with the original text vocabulary. We use K = 200 for C2S (Pythia-410M) and both K = 200 and K = 1000 for the larger C2S-Scale models, Pythia-1B and Gemma2-2B, allowing us to examine the effects of model scale and input context. scGPT models are encoder-only Transformers that represent gene names and their expression values as combined token embeddings and were trained with a maximum context length of K = 1200 . We consider scGPT models pretrained on blood, brain, and whole-human cells. 

Cell-type classification with linear probes. To obtain task-aligned predictions without modifying the pretrained scFMs, we freeze each model and train linear probes on final-layer representations to predict blood cell types and subtypes, and brain cell types. This is necessary even for C2S models that can generate cell-type labels directly, since their generated labels may not match the annotation granularity of our datasets. For C2S, we compare mean pooling over non-padding tokens with the final-token representation, and for scGPT mean pooling with the global cell representation ([CLS] ), selecting the better-performing option per model configuration and task based on validation performance. The probes achieve high accuracy overall, with near-perfect performance on coarse cell types. Hyperparameters and pooling/layer sweep results are detailed in Appendix B. 3.2 MARKER RECOVERY BEYOND EXPRESSION VALUE 

To assess whether linear-probe predictions are supported by biologically meaningful evidence, we examine whether the genes most relevant to each prediction align with established cell-type markers rather than merely reflecting expression magnitude. We use Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP; Bach et al., 2015) to assign each input gene a signed relevance score indicating how strongly it supports or opposes the probe prediction. LRP obtains these scores by propagating the model output backward through the computational graph. 1 Please refer to Appendix C for details. We evaluate marker recovery using average precision (AP), which measures how strongly marker genes are concentrated near the top of a gene ranking. For each cell, genes in the marker set M are treated as positives and all remaining genes as negatives. ## 4 PERTURBATION PREDICTION 

Having first examined cell typing as a relatively simple setting for assessing whether predictions rely on biologically meaningful signals, we extend our analysis to genetic perturbation prediction, a more complex task with important applications in areas such as drug discovery. This progression allows us to move from cell-identity signals to the regulatory dependencies that govern cellular responses to intervention. Recent benchmarks suggest that scFMs do not consistently outperform simpler baselines on perturbation prediction (Ahlmann-Eltze et al., 2025; Kernfeld et al., 2025; Bendidi et al., 2024), motivating a closer examination of what biological structure these models capture beyond predictive performance. We therefore use attribution-based analysis to assess whether the learned dependencies reflect the underlying gene regulatory network. 4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Synthetic dataset. Ground-truth regulatory relationships are often unavailable in real single-cell perturbation datasets, making direct evaluation of whether model predictions reflect the underlying gene regulatory network (GRN) difficult. We therefore use a synthetic setting with fully known reg-6ulatory structure. Following Qi & Chapfuwa (2026); Lorch et al. (2026), cells are generated from context-specific linear stochastic differential equations followed by a noisy emission process, yield-ing two cellular contexts over 128 genes. We generate 8M unperturbed control cells for pretraining and, for fine-tuning, an additional 5M controls and 1M perturbed cells across both contexts. The perturbed set contains 8,192 independently simulated cells for each of 128 randomly signed single-gene perturbations, covering all genes. Controls and perturbed cells are sampled independently from their respective stationary populations and thus have no one-to-one correspondence. Refer to Appendix A.3 for full details. 

Model. For perturbation analysis, we use scGPT as the backbone in a two-stage training proce-dure. We first pretrain scGPT from scratch on control cells using masked gene-expression recon-struction (Cui et al., 2024). We then feed its cell embeddings to the State Transition architecture of STATE (Adduri et al., 2025) and fine-tune the joint model on perturbation data. STATE processes sets of control cells and mixes their intermediate representations through self-attention to predict distributions of perturbed cell states. Details are provided in Appendix E. To distinguish learned be-havior from architectural inductive biases, we also evaluate two random-weight baselines with five replicates each, fully random initialization and pretrained scGPT with an untrained STATE head. The model preserves perturbation identity (centroid accuracy ( 0.962 )) and captures dominant re-sponse directions (Pearson ∆20 = 0 .704 ), despite worse all-gene RMSE than the perturbed-mean baseline ( 0.855 vs. 0.606 ). Full results and metric definitions are provided in Appendices F and H. 4.2 RECOVERING REGULATORY RELATIONSHIPS WITH LRP We use LRP to test whether scGPT+STATE has learned the direct and indirect gene–gene relation-ships encoded in the synthetic GRN. Learning progression. Regulatory alignment, as measured in Section 4.3, would be useful to evaluate while training models, to track how alignment with known regulatory relationships evolves during training. However, it is unrealistic to assume full regulatory knowledge in practical applica-tions. Therefore, we simulate a scenario wherein an analyst would ascertain regulatory interactions for only the most differentially expressed genes (DEGs). For each perturbation, we select the top-six genes (excluding the perturbed gene) with the highest training-dataset empirical DE. The only revealed knowledge is which of the selected DEGs are directly regulated by the perturbed gene, with no interaction sign or magnitude. Because this is a positive-vs-unknown recovery problem and AP requires labelled negatives, we adopt recall@20 as the primary metric. Figure 6A shows a consistent pattern across the sparse- and full-knowledge settings: recall in-creases with training for all signatures and is higher for perturbation-LRP than for gene-LRP. Fur-ther, perturbation-LRP displays a learned alignment signal by epoch 5, at which point predicted DE alignment was still not distinguishable from the random-model controls. This suggests that perturbation-specific information emerged in the perturbation pathway sooner than it was expressed in model outputs. 

Limits of sparse auditing. Though we have seen that attribution signals can serve practical diag-nostic uses even with no or limited ground-truth knowledge, care must be taken not to over-interpret the results as true regulatory discovery. To illustrate this, we consider two typically well-motivated randomised controls (100 replicates each): random targets and perturbation permutation, respec-tively measuring the effect of DEG-panel selection and perturbation-specificity. Indeed, sparse reg-ulator recovery results clearly surpass these controls for all evaluated signatures in Fig. 6B. On the other hand, none of the signatures align with the sparse ground-truth significantly better than with the degree-preserving GRNs from Section 4.3. This implies that the signatures broadly priori-tised the DEGs but did not distinguish which were direct targets of the perturbed gene, underscoring the need for careful experimental design and controls that preserve relevant graph structure. 

## 5 CONCLUSION 

Across cell-type classification and perturbation prediction, attribution analyses provided information complementary to predictive metrics. They revealed which marker genes, contextual interactions, input pathways, and potential shortcuts supported predictions, while also exposing learning progres-sion and prediction reliability beyond output statistics. However, biological conclusions depended on appropriate references, informative baselines, and controls matched to the data-generating pro-cess. Together, these findings establish attribution-based explainability as a powerful post-hoc tool 9for debugging scFMs and guiding model development by revealing the biological signals, shortcuts, and computational pathways behind their successes and failures. Although our study shows a range of questions attribution can address, it covers selected pretrained models, linear-probe tasks, and a single perturbation model. More fundamentally, scFM inter-pretability is constrained by scarce biological ground-truth. Marker panels provide only partial references, while comprehensive GRNs are generally unavailable for real perturbation data, mo-tivating our synthetic setting with fully known regulatory structure despite its limited biological complexity. Future work should test transfer across models, tasks, and datasets using complemen-tary biological evidence, controlled interventions, and task-specific nulls. A.1 BLOOD PBMC I MMUNE -C ELL BENCHMARK 

The blood benchmark contains 61,350 cells and 24,071 genes from 17 source datasets and 106 donors. Each cell has two labels, a coarse immune cell type and a fine-grained cell subtype. The final hierarchy contains 4 cell types and 22 subtypes. 

Extraction and pre-processing. We constructed the blood benchmark from CZ CELLxGENE Census (CZI Cell Science Program et al., 2025), release 2025-11-08 . The goal was to obtain a clean PBMC-like immune-cell dataset in which models could be evaluated at both cell type and subtype resolution. To reduce technical and biological heterogeneity, we restricted the source pool to human primary cells from normal blood generated with the same 10x Genomics 3 ′ v3 assay. We also required each candidate cell to have at least 500 detected genes in the Census metadata. From this source pool, we retained only cells with informative immune-cell annotations. Broad labels that did not specify a subtype were removed, and the remaining labels were mapped into a two-level hierarchy: 4 major immune cell types and 22 nested subtypes. We then applied standard cell-level quality control to raw counts, removing cells with high mitochondrial fraction, predicted doublet status, or unusually low or high numbers of detected genes. Finally, we sub-sampled the filtered cells to make the subtype distribution as balanced as possible, targeting approximately 3,000 cells per subtype. A.3 SYNTHETIC DATASET 

For the perturbation prediction experiment, we generate a synthetic dataset based on Qi & Chapfuwa (2026); Lorch et al. (2026). Each cell in this dataset is represented by the expression of G = 128 

genes. The data is generated from a stochastic linear dynamical system whose interactions are defined by an explicit gene regulatory network (GRN). For a cellular context c, the latent expression state x ∈ RG evolves as 

dx(t) = ( Acx(t) + bc + γq ) d t + σ dw(t), (2) where Ac denotes the context-specific GRN, bc the context-specific baseline, and w(t) is a standard Wiener process. Perturbation q is encoded as a sparse vector γq , with non-zero entries reflecting the sign and strength of the intervention on the target genes. Also following Qi & Chapfuwa (2026), the observed expression value for each gene g is sampled from a negative binomial likelihood, with mean softplus( xg ) and dispersion estimated from the Norman et al. (2019) dataset. We generate two cellular contexts over the same 128 genes. The first context 0 is defined by a sparse signed regulatory network, where each gene selects 10 regulators uniformly (without replacement, excluding self), and assigns each edge a random sign and magnitude uniformly between 1 and 3. The second is obtained from the first one by rewiring 50% of the regulatory edges in each row and modifying the context-specific baseline. The two contexts therefore share part of their regulatory structure while differing in the causal mechanisms governing gene expression. For control cells, 

γq = 0. For perturbed cells, the intervention directly targets one of the 128 genes and its effect propagates through the corresponding GRN. The raw expression values are subsequently normalized to a library size of 10 ,000 and log-transformed. 17 We generate 8M unperturbed control cells for pretraining and, for fine-tuning, an additional 5M con-trols and 1M perturbed cells across both contexts. The perturbed set contains 8,192 independently simulated cells for each of 128 randomly signed single-gene perturbations, covering all genes. Con-trols and perturbed cells are sampled independently from their respective stationary populations and thus have no one-to-one correspondence. Perturbations are instantiated with random sign and ran-dom magnitude in [2 log G, 6 log G] = [9 .7, 29 .1] , bounded away from zero to prevent undetectable effects. Both datasets are divided into 80%, 10%, and 10% training, validation, and test splits, respectively, stratified by context and perturbation.

## C LAYER -WISE RELEVANCE PROPAGATION (LRP) D ETAILS 

Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP; Bach et al., 2015) is a framework for interpreting neural network predictions by attributing the model’s output, or a quantity derived from it, to the input features. LRP assigns a relevance score R to each neuron that reflects its contribution to the quantity of interest and propagates these scores backward through the network using layer-specific rules. E.1 PRE -TRAINING 

We pretrain the scGPT backbone from scratch on the synthetic control-cell dataset described in Appendix A.3. 

Architecture. We use the original scGPT backbone. Each input cell contains expression values for 128 genes together with a [CLS] token, resulting in a sequence length of 129. Gene-expression values are represented as continuous normalized-log1p values, and genes with zero expression are retained explicitly. The model consists of 12 Transformer layers with an embedding dimension of 512, 8 attention heads, a feed-forward dimension of 512, and a dropout rate of 0.1. The complete pretraining model contains approximately 20.3M trainable parameters. 

Masked expression prediction. Pretraining follows the masked expression-prediction setup of scGPT (Cui et al., 2024). For each batch, we sample a masking ratio uniformly from 

{0.25 , 0.50 , 0.75 }, corresponding to 32, 64, or 96 masked genes, respectively. Masked genes are sampled uniformly without replacement, and the model is trained to reconstruct their continuous normalized-log1p expression values from the remaining genes.

For perturbation prediction, we augment the pretrained scGPT backbone with the State Transition architecture of STATE (Adduri et al., 2025) and jointly train the resulting model on the synthetic control–perturbed data. 

Architecture. Each control-cell expression profile is processed by the scGPT gene encoder, con-tinuous value encoder, and 12-layer Transformer backbone to obtain a [CLS] cell representation. This representation is projected through a two-layer basal encoder and combined with a separately projected one-hot perturbation embedding. The resulting cell representations are processed jointly by a bidirectional, non-causal STATE set Transformer. We omit the batch encoding, as the dataset contains homogeneous cell populations for each condition. The STATE module has hidden dimension 64, two Transformer layers, four attention heads, and dropout of 0.1. Both the basal-state and perturbation encoders contain two layers, followed by a two-layer output decoder. We additionally use a learned context token together with an auxiliary context classifier. The decoder predicts post-perturbation expression values for the same 128 genes, with a ReLU activation applied to the output. 

Fine-tuning objective. Since predicted and observed perturbed cells do not have a one-to-one correspondence, we follow STATE Adduri et al. (2025) and optimize the model at the population level using an Energy distance between the predicted and observed perturbed cell sets. The objec-tive additionally regularizes the predicted change relative to the control population and includes an auxiliary context-classification loss: 

Lpost = LEnergy + λ∆L∆ + λcontext Lcontext . (13) Here, Lcontext denotes the cross-entropy loss of the auxiliary context classifier. We set 

λ∆ = 10 −5 and λcontext = 0 .1.

The Energy distance is evaluated over four randomized gene chunks with a blur parameter of 0.05 .

Optimization. Fine-tuning proceeds in two stages. During the first five epochs, the pretrained scGPT backbone is frozen and only the STATE parameters are optimized. We then unfreeze the com-plete scGPT gene encoder, value encoder, and Transformer backbone and jointly optimize scGPT and STATE for up to 15 additional epochs. STATE parameters are optimized with a learning rate of 10 −4, while the pretrained scGPT param-eters use a smaller learning rate of 10 −6. We use the Adam optimizer. Gradients are clipped to a maximum norm of 1.0. Each training example comprises a set of 64 cells, and each training step processes 2 sets. After unfreezing the scGPT backbone, early stopping with a patience of five val-idation evaluations is applied, and the model with the best validation performance is used for final evaluation.
