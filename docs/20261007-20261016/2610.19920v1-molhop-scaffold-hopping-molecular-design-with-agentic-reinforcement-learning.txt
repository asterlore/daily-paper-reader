ABSTRACT 

Molecular design is often formulated as an optimization problem over existing molecular spaces. However, molecular optimization and the discovery of structurally distinct replacement molecules represent two fundamentally different search problems. LLM-based molecular design agents typi-cally perform the former, conducting local refinement within the neighborhood of existing structures to improve target properties through structural modifications. In contrast, the latter requires explo-ration of a broader chemical space to discover molecules with entirely different scaffolds while preserving the functional characteristics of reference molecules. We propose MolHop, an agentic framework for cross-scaffold molecular discovery that enables models to navigate and edit within a pre-organized chemical space. MolHop organizes approximately 1.6 million molecular scaffolds from commercial molecules into a coarse-to-fine hierarchical action space. The agent selects new exploration regions through scaffold navigation actions and adjusts candidate properties through molecular editing. The framework maintains historical trajectory memory to support long-horizon search processes. On drug molecule and commercial compound tasks, connecting Grok-4.6 and DeepSeek-Flash-V4.1 to the MolHop framework substantially improves their cross-scaffold joint success rates. We develop MolHop-9B through agentic reinforcement learning, and MolHop-9B outperforms generative baselines including GPT-5.6-sol, Claude-5-Opus, and Gemini-3.1-Pro, while generating candidate molecules with larger scaffold differences from the reference molecules. These results demonstrate that effective molecular discovery beyond the traditional optimization paradigm requires structured chemical and action spaces, enabling agents to explore new structural regions rather than merely refine existing molecules. 

1 Introduction 

Molecular discovery often starts from a reference compound that already possesses the target function. However, within the molecular discovery process, optimizing an existing molecule and finding a structurally distinct alternative molecule represent two fundamentally different search problems. Molecular optimization typically performs local editing within the neighborhood of the reference structure and optimizes specific properties. In contrast, many prac-tical molecular discovery tasks require candidate molecules to leave the original scaffold family while maintaining multiple target properties. For example, applications including patent circumvention, restricted substance substitu-tion, and toxicity mitigation require alternative molecules with different structures but matched multiple properties. Since a new scaffold starting point may simultaneously alter multiple physicochemical properties, the search process needs to continuously select new structural regions during long-horizon exploration and progressively recover property matching through multiple rounds of scaffold hopping and molecular editing. In recent years, language model-based molecular design agents have introduced a new paradigm for molecular op-timization through tool use. Existing methods (Yang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026a;b; Zhou et al., 2026) primarily rely on local editing operations to iteratively optimize candidate molecules. Scaffold similarity constraints are further introduced to encourage the search process to preserve the core structure of the reference molecule. We extract approximately 1.6 million molecular scaffolds from a commercial molecular library and organize them into a multi-scale action space using a coarse-to-fine hierarchical representation. 2 Related Work 

2.1 Goal-directed molecular search and LLM-based molecular agents 

Goal-directed molecular design has traditionally been formulated as an optimization problem under property feedback. Early approaches optimize molecular generation policies through reinforcement learning or evolutionary search, where candidate molecules are iteratively generated and evaluated according to predefined objectives (Olivecrona et al., 2017; Jensen, 2019). Subsequent benchmarks and methods improve optimization efficiency through better molecular representations, sampling strategies, and generation policies (Brown et al., 2019; Gao et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2025). Recent advances in large language models have introduced agentic workflows for molecular design, enabling iterative interaction with molecular editing tools and property evaluation environments. Existing molecular agents typically refine candidate structures through sequential editing actions, where the search process is guided by property feedback (Yang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026a; Zhou et al., 2026). Despite these advances, most existing approaches focus on local structural refinement around an initial molecule. The search trajectory is primarily composed of molecular edits, while transitions between distant scaffold regions are not explicitly modeled as search decisions. However, many practical discovery scenarios, including scaffold replacement and alternative molecule identification, require deliberate exploration of chemically distinct regions while maintaining target properties. MolHop addresses this limitation by introducing scaffold navigation as an explicit action, allowing agents to perform long-range chemical space exploration before local molecular optimization. 

3 Methods 

The MolHop harness comprises three core components, hierarchical scaffold space, a structured molecular action interface, and trajectory memory. As shown in Figure 1, the agent selects new structural regions in a hierarchical chemical space, adjusts candidate properties through editing actions, and uses feedback from historical trajectories for long-horizon search. 

Hierarchical Scaffold Space. MolHop formulates scaffold hopping as navigation in a hierarchical chemical space. This space organizes scaffolds at multiple levels of structural abstraction, allowing the agent to select structural regions at different granularities according to the search state. We construct the scaffold space from the Enamine Screening Collection 1 and obtain approximately 1.6M valid Bemis–Murcko scaffolds. MolHop organizes these scaffolds using the H1-H7 hierarchical scaffold representation, progressively restoring connectivity topology, atom types, and bond types from ring-level structures until reaching concrete editable scaffolds, as shown in Figure 1(b). This hierarchy provides multiscale scaffold navigation actions, with higher-level nodes supporting exploration across regions and lower-level nodes supporting fine-grained scaffold selection. In cross-scaffold tasks, MolHop records the H1 region of the reference molecule and checks the region of the candidate structure at the end of the episode to ensure that the final candidate completes the specified scaffold transition. Details of hierarchy construction and the scaffold query process are provided in Figure A1 and Appendix A.1. 

Scaffold Navigation and Molecular Editing Actions. MolHop connects scaffold navigation and molecular optimiza-tion through a structured action interface. This interface includes four action categories, scaffold navigation, molecular inspection, local editing, and search control, as shown in Table A2. The agent selects subsequent actions based on the scaffolds, editable sites, and action constraints returned by the environment, without directly generating molecular

Trajectory Memory. Cross-scaffold search requires tracking both explored structural regions and changes in candi-date properties across editing steps. Memory records the reference targets, current candi-date state, property feedback, scaffold visit trajectory, editing history, and search budget, and is returned to the agent together with tool feedback in Figure 1(e). Because all records originate from actual environment states, property eval-uations remain consistent with their corresponding molecular states, and the agent does not need to manually maintain historical structure and property information. This design allows the agent to compare historical candidates, identify explored regions, and adjust subsequent navigation strategies during long-horizon search. Memory supports trajectory decisions within a single episode. Its update rules are controlled by the environment, which produces an agent-facing state according to the context budget. The detailed design is provided in Appendix A.3. 

4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup 4.1.1 Dataset 

We evaluate the cross-scaffold molecular design ability of MolHop on two tasks involving drug molecules and com-mercial screening compounds. Given a reference molecule, the model must generate a structurally valid candidate molecule that leaves the reference scaffold family while maintaining five physicochemical properties close to their reference values and lowering the SA score. All methods use the same reference sets and evaluation protocol. Each test set contains 200 reference molecules, drawn respectively from approved synthetic small molecules in ChEMBL (Gaulton et al., 2012) and the Enamine Screening Collection. Details of dataset construction and splitting are provided in Appendix B.1.

4.1.3 SFT and Agentic RL Training 

The MolHop agent uses a two-stage training procedure. We train the 2B, 4B, and 9B models from Qwen3.5 (Team, 2026) to obtain MolHop-2B, MolHop-4B, and MolHop-9B, respectively. First, supervised fine-tuning through SFT teaches the MolHop harness tool-use protocol. The agents are then trained with GRPO in the MolHop environment to learn multi-turn scaffold exploration and molecular optimization policies. 4.1.4 Baselines and Inference Budget 

We compare three categories of methods, generation baselines, general LLM agents, and MolHop agents trained through agentic RL. Generation baselines receive a reference molecule and a target description, directly generate candidate SMILES, and are then evaluated using a unified offline scoring function. Grok-4.6 and DeepSeek-Flash-V4.1 are connected to the MolHop harness as general LLM agents and perform multi-turn search through tool use and environment feedback without additional training. MolHop agents obtain tool-use policies through the SFT and GRPO procedure described above. We also include the method of Lee et al. (2025) as a representative oracle-driven molecular optimization method and optimize it with a budget of 10,000 oracle calls under its original settings. For agent-based methods, the main experiments use a uniform interaction budget of at most 50 assistant turns. Because generation baselines and tool-interaction methods use different search forms, this comparison reflects their final discovery ability under the complete design workflow. To evaluate the effect of the MolHop harness on the molecular design ability of existing language models, we compare frozen Grok-4.6 and DeepSeek-Flash-V4.1 in one-shot generation mode and with access to the MolHop harness. This comparison changes only the search environment, tool interface, and multi-turn feedback mechanism accessible to the models. Grok-4.6 improves from 41.0% and 36.5% to 64.0% and 69.0%, while DeepSeek-Flash-V4.1 improves from 15.5% and 17.5% to 52.5% and 60.5%. Meanwhile, both scaffold Tanimoto similarity and MCS ratio decrease among successful candidates, indicating that the success rate improvements brought by the harness are accompanied by more substantial structural transitions. A further decomposition by condition is provided in Appendix C.1 . The analysis shows that the harness improvements do not come solely from improving one constraint, but instead simultaneously increase the proportions satisfying structural validity, property matching, and scaffold transition. This result indicates that a structured search environment can activate the tool-planning ability of frozen language models in cross-scaffold design.

4.5.2 Ablation of MolHop Components 

Under the MolHop-4B setting, we separately remove molecular editing, trajectory memory, and the hierarchical scaf-fold library and retrain the corresponding agents to analyze the contributions of these components to cross-scaffold search. 

Figure 4(a-b) shows the effects of different components on training. The full MolHop maintains favorable optimiza-tion trends in both reward and validation joint@H1. Although the variant without the scaffold library obtains higher training reward, its validation joint@H1 decreases substantially. After removing the hierarchical scaffold library, the agent lacks an explicit scaffold navigation path and tends to optimize properties around the current structure. This change affects both scaffold transition and synthetic accessibility optimization. Molecular editing alone makes it dif-ficult to consistently complete the target scaffold transition and to ensure that candidates satisfy the SA improvement condition during property adjustment. The continuous reward can increase through reductions in property error, but optimizing the surrogate objective does not necessarily translate into a higher joint success rate. Detailed analysis is provided in Appendix C.3. As shown in Figure 4(c,d) , performance decreases after removing editing, memory, or the scaffold library. Removing the scaffold library and memory leads to more substantial performance decreases, indicating that hierarchical scaffold space and trajectory memory play key roles in long-horizon cross-scaffold search. The improvement from the editing component is relatively small and primarily comes from further adjusting candidate properties after scaffold selection.

5 Conclusion 

This work studies cross-scaffold molecular discovery as a search problem that requires both structural exploration and property optimization. Unlike conventional molecular optimization methods that mainly perform local modifi-cations around existing structures, scaffold hopping requires agents to identify and navigate new structural regions while recovering target properties. To address this challenge, we propose MolHop, an agentic framework that orga-nizes molecular scaffolds into a hierarchical search space and integrates scaffold navigation, molecular editing, and trajectory memory into a unified interaction environment. Our experiments demonstrate that the MolHop harness substantially improves the cross-scaffold discovery ability of frozen LLM agents, showing that structured chemical search spaces and action interfaces can effectively support molecular exploration. Furthermore, agentic reinforcement learning enables models at different scales to learn search policies that coordinate scaffold selection and molecular editing under multi-objective constraints. These results high-light the importance of designing structured environments for molecular agents, where exploration decisions are ex-plicitly represented rather than emerging only from local molecular transformations. We believe MolHop provides a step toward more capable molecular discovery agents that can navigate broader chemical spaces through learned interaction with structured chemical environments.

Appendix A Detailed Design 

A.1 Hierarchical Scaffold Space 

The scaffold space of MolHop is constructed from the Enamine Screening Collection, which contains 4,805,572 commercial molecular records. We first standardize the molecules using RDKit, including molecular parsing, SaltType filtering, and retention of the largest organic fragment in multicomponent molecules. We then extract Bemis–Murcko scaffolds, convert the results to canonical SMILES, and deduplicate them, yielding 1,811,153 unique scaffold records. To ensure consistency between the scaffold space and the subsequent editing environment, we further filter the struc-tural types. The search space retains single-component connected scaffolds and removes records containing bridgehead or spiro structures. There are 87,329 scaffolds containing only spiro structures, 55,729 containing only bridgehead structures, and 2,480 containing both types, resulting in a total of 145,538 excluded records. The final set contains 1,665,615 valid scaffold leaf nodes. For each H7 exact scaffold, MolHop generates signatures for the H1-H7 hierarchical scaffold representation according to deterministic graph abstraction rules and establishes parent–child hierarchical relationships based on shared signa-tures. As shown in Figure A1(a), this hierarchy progressively restores connectivity, topological details, and chemical type information from coarse-grained ring structures, ultimately yielding a complete typed Murcko scaffold. The hierarchical representation is generated by predefined graph abstraction rules and does not rely on clustering with continuous similarity thresholds. The definitions and node counts at each level are shown in Table A1. After the hierarchy is constructed, each H7 scaffold serves as a selectable leaf node. During search, the agent performs scaffold queries and scaffold selection based on hierarchical nodes and loads the selected H7 scaffold into the editing environment, as shown in Figure A1(b). A.2 Scaffold Navigation and Molecular Editing Actions 

The MolHop harness provides four groups of structured actions, including scaffold navigation, molecular inspection, local editing, and search control, with a total of 9 external actions, as shown in Table A2. The agent selects subsequent actions based on structured information returned by the environment, including candidate categories, scaffold identi-fiers, editable sites, and action constraints. All actions are executed by the environment, which returns validated state feedback. The agent does not directly generate free-form SMILES to modify molecules. Scaffold navigation actions query and select candidate structures in the hierarchical scaffold space. nav_where re-turns the position of the current molecule in the H1-H7 hierarchical space and the regions available for exploration. 

nav_list enumerates categories, subregions, and specific scaffolds at a specified level. nav_filter applies struc-tural constraints to H7 leaf nodes, including molecular weight, heavy-atom count, ring count, rotatable-bond count, heteroatom count, and elemental constraints. All filtering conditions apply to the same scaffold record and are eval-uated jointly. The filtering results return a candidate set with stable identifiers for subsequent selection and further filtering. nav_pick loads the target scaffold using a candidate identifier and uses it as the input structure for subse-quent edit actions. Molecular inspection actions identify editable positions in the current structure. mol_show returns the correspond-ing site information for a specified action category, and its all mode returns counts of sites in different categories. 

mol_site takes a target site identifier and returns the edit types supported at that position and information on available fragments. A.3 Trajectory Memory 

MolHop memory is constructed deterministically by the harness from environment interaction to record the search state within a single episode. Memory updates are triggered by actual execution events, including scaffold loading, molecular editing, state restoration, and property evaluation. Whenever a tool response is generated, the harness reconstructs memory from the current environment state and returns it to the agent together with the tool result. As shown in Figure A2, memory organizes the search process into a set of interrelated state representations, including the current target state, scaffold retrieval trail, revision ledger, and context for deciding the next action. This structure records changes in candidate molecules across different scaffolds and revisions and associates historical actions with the corresponding property gaps. 15 MolHop 

The information recorded in memory includes the reference target, current candidate state, property feedback, eval-uation status, best historical record, scaffold visitation trajectory, editing trajectory, candidate-set state, and search budget, as shown in Table A4. Property feedback is directly bound to the corresponding molecular state, and structural identifiers distinguish different revisions to ensure consistency between historical evaluations and the current candidate state. To accommodate a limited context window, the harness generates memory summaries according to predefined rules. By default, a state summary displays at most 10 segments of scaffold visitation records, retains the most recent 12 steps in each segment, and explicitly records the number of omitted entries. During inference, memory content in earlier tool responses can be replaced with summary placeholders, while the complete score log remains in the environment state. RL rollouts use a separate state compression process that updates candidate lists and historical entries before new observations enter the context. All memory update and summarization rules are determined by the environment and do not depend on the LLM autonomously generating historical summaries. This design ensures that the same environment state corresponds to the same memory representation and makes memory a structured episode state that can be directly used for subsequent action selection. B Experimental Setup 

B.1 Dataset Construction 

The experimental data are constructed from ChEMBL and the Enamine Screening Collection. All experiments use a unified offline scoring function to recalculate target properties and SA scores. The drug molecule data are obtained from records of approved synthetic small molecules in the ChEMBL database. We select molecules with max_phase=4 and exclude entries marked as withdrawn. The Approved label in ChEMBL denotes the drug status recorded in the database and does not indicate that this study revalidates marketing status in different regions. The commercial compound data are obtained from the August 2026 version of the Enamine Screening Collection. All molecules are first standardized using RDKit, including removal of records with nonempty SaltType labels and retention of the largest organic fragment in multicomponent molecules. Molecular parsing, connectivity checks, and metal-element filtering are then performed, followed by deduplication based on canonical SMILES. For reference molecules from both data sources, we apply the same structural filtering criteria. The heavy-atom count is between 10 and 50, the ring count is between 1 and 6, the absolute value of Crippen logP is at least 0.5, and TPSA is at least 10 Å². The logP and TPSA conditions ensure stability in relative-error calculations and do not define a general range of drug-likeness. The five target properties and SA scores are all recalculated using the unified implementation within the project, rather than directly using property labels provided by the database or vendor. The test sets are first deduplicated by Bemis–Murcko scaffold and then sampled in strata based on ring count. Within each stratum, representative molecules are selected using MaxMin diversity selection with Morgan fingerprints of radius 2 and length 2048 bits. All sampling procedures use the fixed random seed 42. For ChEMBL data, candidates 17 MolHop 

sharing the same scaffold are selected by prioritizing first-in-class molecules, followed by records with earlier approval dates. Duplicate molecules between the two test sets are excluded using InChIKey. Both SFT and RL training data exclude InChIKeys present in the test sets. SFT starting points additionally exclude the RL starting-point set. SFT and RL data sampling use random seeds 123 and 456, respectively, and the training– validation split uses random seed 42. Different molecules in the training sets may share a Murcko scaffold, but no starting molecules are identical to those in the test sets. C.3 Scaffold Library Ablation Analysis 

To analyze why reward and joint@H1 are inconsistent after removing the scaffold library, we diagnose the terminal candidates generated in the later stage of training by the model without the scaffold library. Specifically, we examine the final output states of 400 evaluation samples at step 150, including satisfaction of the property matching, SA improvement, and scaffold transition conditions. The results show that this variant can effectively optimize the property matching objective, with 96.1% of candidates satisfying MD<1. However, terminal SA scores do not improve correspondingly, with a median SA score change of +0.393 relative to the starting point. Further analysis shows that 67.7% of candidates already satisfy the three conditions of structural validity, property matching, and H1 transition but are excluded from joint@H1 because their SA scores do not meet the requirement for strict improvement. The final joint success rate is 19.4%. These results indicate that, after removing the scaffold library, the search process relies more on molecular editing of the current structure for optimization. This strategy has two limitations. The absence of explicit scaffold navigation reduces the model’s ability to enter distant structural regions, and some candidates cannot consistently complete the required scaffold transition. In addition, although local editing can reduce property distance, it struggles to ensure simultaneous SA improvement. Consequently, the model tends to obtain candidates with better property matching but cannot consistently coordinate the three objectives of structural transition, property matching, and synthetic accessi-bility.

D OOD Property Optimization beyond the Restricted Visible Scaffold Library 

We further examine a stricter scenario in which the property distribution of target molecules lies outside the central region of the visible scaffold library, assessing whether an LLM integrated with the MolHop harness can still obtain candidates satisfying the constraints through editing. This experiment distinguishes the ability to directly retrieve suitable scaffolds from the scaffold library from the ability to continue property optimization within the harness. 22 MolHop 

To construct this scenario, we define a property visibility mask, clip_mask , based on the complete scaffold library. Specifically, we calculate the 25th and 75th percentiles of each of the five properties logP, TPSA, MW, LabuteASA, and MolMR for molecules in the scaffold library and retain only H7 leaf nodes whose five properties all fall within the corresponding [p25,p75] intervals. The resulting thresholds are shown in Table D1. After applying this mask, 287,888 leaf nodes remain in the scaffold library, accounting for 17.3% of all leaf nodes. We then select a set of OOD targets from the 400 reference molecules in the ChEMBL-200 and Enamine-200 test sets. All five properties of each reference molecule fall within the above [p25,p75] box, but its nearest-neighbor match distance in the restricted scaffold library satisfies md ≥ 1. This yields 83 targets, including 58 from ChEMBL and 25 from Enamine. For each target, we retrieve the leaf node with the smallest match distance in the restricted scaffold library and treat this result as the scaffold library upper bound. Because the target definition excludes close neighbors within the library, the md values of all 83 upper-bound candidates are at least 1.009, their mean md is 2.376, and their joint@H1 values are all 0. We then integrate DeepSeek-Flash-V4.1 with the MolHop harness to search the same restricted scaffold library, with a maximum interaction budget of 100 turns. The results are shown in Table D2. Compared with scaffold library retrieval alone, DeepSeek + harness achieves a joint@H1 of 43.4%, or 36/83, on the 83 OOD targets. Of the candidates, 90.4%, or 75/83, satisfy md<1, and the mean match distance decreases from 2.376 to 0.542. This result indicates that, when the visible scaffold library itself cannot provide candidates close to the targets, the agent can use the editing actions provided by the harness to further optimize properties on the basis of selected scaffolds and recover some joint successes. We show the complete tool-use sequence and selected molecu-lar states for each episode. A successful MolHop-4B case highlights a local property trade-off and a subsequent return to a candidate retained in memory (Figure E1). A comparison from the same starting molecule shows why closer property matching alone does not ensure joint success (Figure E2). A MolHop-4B failure shows an unmet SA condi-tion despite accurate matching of all five target properties (Figure E3). Two further baseline examples show successful submission and budget exhaustion on different tasks (Figure E4). Throughout, D denotes the sum of five normalized absolute property deviations, and SA/ref is the candidate SA score divided by the reference SA score.
