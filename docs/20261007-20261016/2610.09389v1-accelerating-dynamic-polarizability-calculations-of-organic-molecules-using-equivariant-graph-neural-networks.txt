Abstract 

Predicting the dynamic polarizability tensor of organic molecules is essential for simu-lating light–matter interactions in optoelectronic devices, yet conventional quantum-chemical methods such as time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) are computationally expensive and limit large-scale screening. We present an equivariant graph neural network architecture that predicts frequency-dependent, complex-valued dynamic polarizability ten-sors directly from readily available molecular information, such as 3D geometry and UV–vis spectra. The model learns to approximate the shape and magnitude of the dynamic polar-izability tensor, capturing both dispersive and absorptive features that define the molecular optical response. We evaluate the model on molecules from both the QM9 dataset and the Harvard organic photovoltaic dataset using various metrics, including the earth mover’s dis-tance, which is suitable for comparing spectral quantities. For both datasets, the model successfully reproduces the main features of the dynamic polarizability tensor and generalizes across diverse molecules. To assess the applicability, we further validated the model using a downstream workflow to calculate the optical properties of organic photovoltaic devices. We used the model-predicted polarizabilities to perform device-level optical simulations, yielding charge carrier generation rates in agreement with TD-DFT-based results ( R2 = 0 .94 ). Our approach offers a promising complement, or even an alternative, to conventional methods such as TD-DFT, enabling faster and more efficient screening of photoactive organic molecules for optoelectronic applications. 

# 1 Introduction 

Organic optoelectronic materials, e.g. , materials used for organic photovoltaics (OPV) are a promis-ing class of materials due to their mechanical flexibility and lightweight fabrication [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Their performance in optoelectronic devices is fundamentally determined by how they interact with light. Optimizing the optical properties of organic semiconductors is therefore central to advancing or-ganic optoelectronic technologies. However, this is challenging and computationally demanding. For each candidate molecule, one must determine how it interacts with electromagnetic fields across relevant excitation energies, a property encoded in the dynamic polarizability tensor. This frequency-dependent tensor quantifies how the molecular electron cloud deforms in response to incident light and thus serves as the fundamental link between molecular structure and optical behavior. 1arXiv:2610.09389v1 [physics.chem-ph] 7 Oct 2026 The dynamic polarizability tensor forms the core input to the transition matrix (T-matrix) for-malism [9, 10, 11], which reformulates molecular optical responses into a representation directly compatible with Maxwell-based electromagnetic simulations. This pipeline propagates quantum-chemical information from the molecular scale to device-level optical models, enabling accurate prediction and optimization of OPV device performance (Figure 1). Recent work by some of the present authors has begun to realize fully differentiable digital twins of complete OPV device stacks, unifying material-, device-, and system-level simulations to enable end-to-end, gradient-based optimization of solar cell performance [12]. Extending this differentiability down to the molecular property calculation step itself, where conventional quantum-chemical methods such as time-dependent density functional theory (TD-DFT) are used, remains desirable and motivates machine-learning (ML) approaches capable of predicting these properties directly and differen-tiably. Predictions of dynamic polarizability tensors typically rely on TD-DFT. Their computation re-mains a major bottleneck in molecular screening workflows. For example, calculating dynamic polarizability tensors for a subset of roughly 5 000 molecules from the Quantum Machine 9 (QM9) dataset [13, 14] requires approximately 13 600 CPU hours. For the Harvard Organic Photovoltaic dataset (HOPV15) [15], which contains 347 larger and more structurally complex organic photoac-tive molecules relevant to OPV applications, the cost is around 60 000 CPU hours. In contrast, computing only UV–vis spectra is far more efficient, requiring only about 500 CPU hours for the same QM9 subset and 5 300 CPU hours for the HOPV15 molecules. UV–vis spectra are directly related to the imaginary part of the dynamic polarizability tensor, but can be obtained without resolving its full frequency-dependent, complex-valued structure. However, calculating dynamic polarizabilities is much more computationally complex than obtaining the discrete transitions that define the absorption spectrum. These numbers highlight that dynamic polarizability calculations dominate the computational budget in high-throughput OPV screening pipelines, accounting for roughly 80 to 90% of the total cost. 

Figure 1: A scheme depicting the overall multiscale multiscattering simulations of the OPV stack. A neural network (NN) replaces computationally demanding calculations of dynamic polarizability tensors needed for the T-matrix construction step of the photoactive molecules. Graph neural networks (GNNs) offer a promising alternative for predicting dynamic polarizabil-ity tensors at a fraction of the computational cost. Message passing neural networks (MPNNs), introduced by Gilmer et al. [16], enabled end-to-end learning of molecular representations. Early architectures such as SchNet and DimeNet started incorporating geometric information to im-prove quantum-chemical predictions [17, 18]. Equivariant architectures such as Tensor Field Networks [19], SE(3)-Transformers [20], EGNNs [21], and PaiNN [22], introduced rotation- and translation-equivariant message passing, enabling the learning of vector and tensor properties di-rectly from atomic coordinates. More recent and widely used models for molecular regression tasks include NequIP [23] and Alle-gro [24], which scale equivariant learning to large systems with high accuracy and data efficiency. Although these models internally propagate geometric information that can be viewed as low-order tensors, they primarily target scalar quantities such as energies (and, by extension, forces, which are the gradients of the energies). Recent developments push beyond this, aiming to learn higher-order tensorial observables. De-taNet [25] introduces explicit higher-order tensor message passing to predict static polarizabilities and hyperpolarizabilities with quantum-chemical fidelity. HotPP [26] follows a similar approach by passing Cartesian tensor embeddings to model vibrational and electronic response properties. Most recently, Falletta et al. proposed a unified equivariant framework in which response proper-ties, including forces, polarization, and static polarizability, emerge as exact derivatives of a learned 2potential, guaranteeing physical symmetry preservation [27]. Similarly, a recent SO (3) -equivariant local coordinate frame GNN proposed by Filling et al. [28] enables direct tensor regression but remains confined to predicting static molecular polarizability tensors from geometric structures on small-molecule datasets [29, 30, 31, 32]. Equivariant GNNs have also been applied to frequency-dependent dielectric tensors of crystals [33]. To the best of our knowledge, no existing equivariant model directly predicts frequency-dependent, complex-valued dynamic polarizability tensors from a molecular graph. We address this gap by extending the DetaNet architecture to incorporate both atomic coordinates and a sampled UV–vis spectrum. Although the model can be trained using atomic coordinates alone, adding spectral information substantially improves performance, and UV–vis spectra are comparatively inexpensive to obtain. This enables accurate, direct prediction of the full dynamic polarizability tensor across a range of frequencies, providing a fast, ML-based complement to TD-DFT. While contemporary frameworks focus on static properties of simpler structures, our model successfully scales to the larger, structurally complex molecules of the HOPV15 dataset. Furthermore, we validate its practical utility by passing these predicted polarizabilities into a realistic device-level optical modeling workflow, ultimately calculating macroscopic charge carrier generation rates for full organic photovoltaic solar cells. We generate reference data using TD-DFT for two benchmark datasets: a sampled subset of the QM9 database, which we denote as QM9SPol, and the HOPV15 dataset for more application-relevant molecules. QM9SPol contains small organic molecules to benchmark and validate our methodology, while HOPV15 consists of larger and more structurally complex molecules closer to real-world applications. We assess the model’s performance on both QM9SPol and HOPV15 using various metrics, including the mean squared error (MSE), R2 scores, and the earth mover’s distance (EMD). To further evaluate the applicability of our model for realistic device-level simulations, we con-duct a comprehensive optical analysis for a held-out subset of 35 application-relevant molecules from the HOPV15 database. Using both TD-DFT polarizabilities and our model predictions, we compute the corresponding molecular T-matrices and perform device-level optical simulations to obtain charge-carrier generation rates. The optical responses obtained from model-predicted po-larizabilities show agreement ( R2 = 0 .94 ) with those based on TD-DFT, demonstrating that our model serves as a promising alternative to TD-DFT for device-level optical simulations. This con-firms that beyond accurately predicting dynamic polarizabilities, our approach also maintains the physical fidelity required for downstream modeling while reducing computational cost. 

# 2 Methods 

# 2.1 Datasets and data preparation 

In this study, we use two primary datasets. The QM9SPol dataset contains smaller organic molecules to benchmark and validate our methods, and the HOPV15 dataset contains larger or-ganic molecules to assess performance in more application-relevant cases. The quantity we aim to predict is the polarizability tensor α(ω). The tensor describes how a molecule’s electron cloud deforms in response to an external electric field oscillating at angular frequency ω. It is a complex 

3 × 3 tensor that describes the excitation of electric dipoles in a corresponding molecule. In the sections below, we explain how we calculate it for the different datasets and how we use the tensor’s properties to train our model. 

The QM9SPol dataset 

The QM9SPol dataset (QM9 with UV–vis spectra and polarizabilities) is a sampled subset of the original QM9 dataset, containing 5 160 randomly selected molecules from QM9 [13, 14]. We re-optimize all geometries, and we calculate static and dynamic polarizabilities, as well as the 15 lowest singlet excitation energies and the corresponding oscillator strengths. We compute dynamic polarizabilities across the 1.55–6.2 eV range (800 nm to 200 nm), at equally spaced 61 energies, including the full complex electric–electric ( ee ), electric–magnetic ( em ), and magnetic–magnetic (mm ) tensor components. The HOPV15 dataset 

The HOPV15 dataset consists of 347 conjugated molecules and small polymers representative of acceptor materials in organic photovoltaic systems [15]. Relative to the QM9SPol dataset, the molecular structures in HOPV15 exhibit greater chemical and structural complexity. For each molecule, we apply the same electronic structure protocol used for QM9SPol to compute the static polarizability at zero frequency and the frequency-dependent polarizability tensor components ( ee ,

em , and mm ), along with the 40 lowest singlet excitation energies and their associated oscilla-tor strengths. We use the same AAA interpolation procedure for both QM9SPol and HOPV15. HOPV15 is smaller than QM9SPol but more representative of realistic OPV molecules. Compre-hensive details of all quantum chemistry calculations and their respective computational costs are available in the Supporting Information.

# 2.3 Post-polarizability prediction optical simulation pipeline 

To compare model-predicted dynamic polarizabilities with their TD-DFT counterparts in a realistic device context, we integrate both into the same optical simulation workflow. For this purpose, we first convert the dynamic polarizabilities into molecular T-matrices [9, 10, 38], which describe light–matter interactions at the single-molecule level [11, 39]. We manually set predicted negative, diagonal imaginary dynamic polarizability values to zero prior to this step. These negative values occur only occasionally as prediction errors of the ML model; clipping them to zero corrects unphysical artifacts without affecting any TD-DFT-based results. We homogenize the resulting T-matrices using the treams framework to obtain the effective bulk T-matrix and corresponding permittivity of the photoactive layer [40]. Using these material properties, we carry out device-level optical simulations with a semi-analytical scattering-matrix (S-matrix) solver, assuming the solar cell is a stack of stratified isotropic me-dia [41]. The S-matrix is a linear operator connecting the incoming and outgoing waves of the stack [42]. It is constructed analytically for each layer from its permittivity ε and thickness d [43], and individual layers are successively combined via the Redheffer product [44, 45] to obtain the overall transmitted field E( x). From this field, we calculate the generation rate G of the stack as:

# 3 Results and discussion 

# 3.1 Evaluation metrics of the model 

Commonly used metrics for the prediction of molecular properties, such as the static polarizability, are the R2 score, MSE or root mean squared error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE) [22, 25, 26, 46]. These are all pointwise metrics, calculated via a direct element-by-element comparison between predicted and reference values, averaged over all tensor components and frequency points. However, since our goal is to predict the full frequency-dependent polarizability tensor, pointwise metrics are insufficient. Therefore, we adopt the EMD, or 1-Wasserstein distance, which quantifies discrepancies in the overall spectral shape of each tensor component across frequencies, provid-ing a more meaningful evaluation for spectrum-level predictions. The EMD conceptualizes each spectrum as an area distributed across frequency. The distance measures the minimal total cost required to redistribute the predicted spectrum to match the target spectrum, with cost defined as the product of the total area moved and the distance it is moved. Unlike pointwise metrics, the EMD captures differences in the overall shape of the spectrum and is less sensitive to small shifts of sharp peaks, which can otherwise dominate error measures like the RMSE. For each matrix element i ∈ { 1, . . . , 18 } from the real and imaginary parts of dynamic polarizability, the distributions of predicted and reference values, denoted as μ(i) and ν(i), are compared using the 1-Wasserstein distance W1(μ(i), ν (i)). # 3.2 Training results 

Prior to training, we randomly partition both datasets into training and test sets using a 90/10 split. For QM9SPol, this results in 4644 molecules for the training set and 516 for the test set. For HOPV15, the split yields 312 molecules for training and 35 for the test set. We decompose the dynamic polarizability tensor, sampled at F = 61 frequency points, into its real and imaginary components and concatenate it to form a tensor of size 122 × 3 × 3. For HOPV15, which consists of photoactive molecules relevant to the target application, we train three separate models: one predicting 61 polarizability values, one fine-tuned from a model pre-trained on QM9SPol (also 61 values), and one predicting 241 polarizability values, obtained by expanding the original 61 sampled frequencies with additional points generated via the AAA algorithm. For the UV–vis spectra input, we choose to sample 61 points to match the frequencies used in the polarizability tensors. Although higher sampling densities are tested, only a slight performance gain is observed. We also evaluate models trained solely on atomic coordinates. However, these baselines consistently show higher error across both QM9SPol and HOPV15, motivating the inclusion of UV–vis spectra, which are comparatively inexpensive to obtain. We optimize model performance through Bayesian hyperparameter tuning, and apply a dropout rate of 20% to all hidden layers to prevent overfitting. We summarize the performance of the models across different experiments in Table 1. Although the EMD is the more relevant metric, we also include the RMSE and R2 scores to make further comparisons. For the QM9SPol dataset, the model effectively captures the overall shape of the frequency-dependent dynamic polarizability tensor, although it occasionally misses minor peak structures. These discrepancies may originate from the relatively limited size of the dataset, which could lead to overfitting, especially considering that DetaNet was originally trained on over 130 000 molecules to achieve accurate UV–vis spectrum predictions. Predicting the dynamic polarizability tensor across the 1.55–6.2 eV frequency range is a more demanding task and likely requires a substantially larger and more diverse dataset for optimal performance. We illustrate the model’s performance on QM9SPol in Figure 4. Fine-tuning from QM9SPol to HOPV15 does not offer an advantage over training the model on HOPV15 from scratch. Molecules in the HOPV15 dataset are larger, structurally more complex, and differ substantially in their physical properties from those in QM9SPol, limiting the effective-ness of knowledge transfer between the two datasets. On the HOPV15 dataset, the model effectively learns the structure and variation of the dynamic polarizability tensor across the frequency range. The models trained on 61 and 241 polarizabilities produce comparable results, with the 241-point model performing slightly better on RMSE and EMD, though with a marginally lower R2 score. The finer sampling grid improves the model’s ability to capture smaller peaks in the polarizability tensor. We show an overview of the best-performing 241-point model in Figure 5.

# Theoretical background 

Time-dependent density functional theory 

Density Functional Theory (DFT) is a practical way to describe how atoms and molecules behave. An atom is a mostly empty space: almost all of its mass sits in the nucleus, yet its electrons decide how the atom reacts with its surroundings. In principle, one could predict the motions of the electrons by solving Schrödinger’s equation, but that task becomes impossibly hard as soon as many atoms are involved because every electron interacts with every other one [1]. DFT sidesteps this problem by focusing on the electron density, the probability of finding an electron at any point in space, instead of tracking each electron separately. Working with this density turns the many-body problem into a set of much simpler equations that computers can solve approximately, even for molecules containing hundreds or thousands of atoms, well beyond the reach of more accurate but far computationally expensive wavefunction-based methods such as coupled-cluster (CC) or configuration-interaction (CI) [1]. When a molecule is placed in a time-varying electric or magnetic field, its electron cloud adjusts accordingly. Time-Dependent Density Functional Theory provides a systematic way to predict that adjustment: it treats the external field as a small perturbation of the ground state calculated by DFT and, from the resulting linear response, extracts molecular properties such as the dynamic polarizability.

# Integration of the model into organic photovoltaic device simulations 

In this section, we provide a detailed description of the computational setup for all steps, starting with the molecular description of the single molecules, over the T-matrix construction and the prediction with the machine learning model, and finally to the calculation of the generation rates of the whole stacked organic photovoltaic device using both calculated and predicted dynamic po-larizabilities. This quantum-chemistry-to-device pipeline forms the basis for the fully differentiable digital twin of the OPV stack described in previous work [4]. All produced quantum chemistry outputs from TURBOMOLE are deposited in the NOMAD database and can be accessed via the following DOI: https://doi.org/10.17172/NOMAD.RG5M-AQT6 .

Details of the quantum chemistry calculations of QM9SPol and HOPV15 sets 

All quantum chemistry calculations in this work have been performed with the development version of the electronic structure program TURBOMOLE [5, 6] that is developed at our university, the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology. For the ground state properties we used DFT, and for the excited state properties we employed its time-dependent linear response variant, TD-DFT. 

QM9SPol set A subset of 5160 molecules was randomly selected from the QM9 [7, 8] dataset containing over 134000 molecules with up to 9 heavy (not hydrogen) atoms. Atomic positions of all molecules were optimized using the gradient minimization technique to find stable ground state configurations using computationally cheap generalized-gradient approx-imation (GGA) PBE exchange-correlation (XC) energy functional [9, 10] with Karlsruhe atomic orbital (AO) split-valence basis set def2-SVP [11]. The minimum of the molecular structure on the potential energy surface was confirmed by performing vibrational frequency analysis. In order to further speed up all calculations, a resolution-of-identity (RI)[12], and multipole-accelerated RI-J[13] algorithms were employed. All subsequent calculations used previously found stable geometries and a single-point energy calculation (ridft) with a hybrid functional and a higher-quality basis set were used to obtain reliable results. For optical properties, a hybrid PBE0 XC functional [14, 15] in combination with the triple-ζ-quality AO basis set with added valence and polarization functions (def2-TZVP) [16, 17, 18, 19] was chosen. This combination of the XC functional and the basis set is known to produce an absorption spectrum of a large and diverse set of molecules with the smallest mean square error compared to many functionals, while still having acceptable computational complexity. To speed up the calculations and additionally reduce computational demand, a semi-numerical algorithm in ground (senex)[20] and excited (esenex)[21] state for the exchange part of the energy functional were also used alongside RI and RI-J options. Magnetic contributions were included to the calculation of dynamic polarizabilities with a step called "mpshift". Absorption spectra were obtained by calculating the discrete electronic transi-tions from the ground to the 15 lowest-lying excited states, and an overall spectrum was constructed by applying a Lorentzian line-shape with 0.05 eV as FWHM to each transition and summing up all broadened line-shapes. The broadened line-shapes are summed over the energy window from 

1.5 eV to 13 .5 eV , and the spectrum is sampled at the same energies as the dynamic polarizability tensors (61 points for QM9SPol, 241 points for HOPV15). Subsequently, electric-electric ( ee ), electric-magnetic ( em ), and magnetic-magnetic ( mm ) static polarizabilities were calculated for all molecules. Finally, a complex ee , em , and mm dynamic polarizability tensors were calculated for an equally spaced 61 frequencies from 1.55 eV to 6.2 eV 

(approximately 800 nm -200 nm in wavelength). The computational costs for the different calcula-tions are depicted in Table 1. We see that the most important quantum chemistry result for our 17 OPV simulations (the dynamic polarizability tensors) are computationally the most demanding part. Thus, to generate additional data for training the neural network, which can replace those expensive calculations, we applied the efficient AAA algorithm[22, 23, 24] allowing us to interpo-late the calculated 61 frequency to a total of 241 frequency spanning the same spectral window (1.55 eV - 6.2 eV ). This comes at virtually no additional computational cost, whilst improving the overall training of the neural network for dynamic polarizability predictions. 

HOPV15 set A total of 347 organic molecules with characteristic absorption in the visible (Vis) and/or ultraviolet (UV) part of the electromagnetic spectrum were extracted from the Harvard Organic Photovoltaic database (HOPV) for further studies in the context of the multi-scale multi-domain simulations of the complete OPV device stack. For all 347 molecules, the same procedure for quantum chemistry calculations was used as for the molecules contained in the QM9SPol dataset, which is described above in Subsection. The only difference is that for obtaining the absorption spectra we calculated the 40 lowest-lying discrete electronic transitions from the ground to the excited states.

Details of the quantum chemistry calculations of donor polymer PM6 

To obtain the electronic ground-state structure of the PM6 polymer donor material, we considered two monomers as the molecular model. Structural molecular optimization was performed with the same hybrid PBE0 XC functional [14, 15] and the triple-ζ-quality basis set with additional valence and polarization functions (def2-TZVP) [16, 17, 18, 19] as above. Subsequent calculations of all optical properties, like the dynamic polarizabilities, used a coulomb-attenuated long-range-corrected hybrid cam-B3LYP functional [26] with the def2-TZVP basis set. This choice ensures a correct description of long-range effects and charge-transfer states of the polymer material. For PM6, the dynamic polarizabilities were evaluated in a wavelength range from 300 nm to 

1200 nm , in increments of 2 nm . This is the equivalent of 1.033 eV to 4.133 eV . In addition, we 19 introduce a Lorentzian line-shape broadening factor of 0.1 eV . We construct the T-matrices from the dynamic polarizability tensors, as described below in the Dynamic polarizability to T-matrix 

section. 

Molecular model of PM6 and models of HOPV molecules 

To understand the optical response of the photoactive bulk material, the optical responses on the molecular level are investigated. Since the morphological complexity of the photoactive material is high, we assume a simplified model. We treat the donor and the absorber molecules separately to calculated their optical properties, rather than directly modeling them as a mixture on the molecular level. This means that for the PM6:HOPV system, we define the structural properties of the molecular models separately and use those as the basis for all our further calculation steps. In a later step, we then mix the materials on a macroscopic level. The donor polymer PM6 is short for Poly[(2,6-(4,8-bis(5-(2-ethylhexyl-3-fluoro)thiophen-2-yl)-benzo[1,2-b:4,5-b’]dithiophene))-alt-(5,5-(1’,3’-di-2-thienyl-5’,7’-bis(2-ethylhexyl)benzo[1’,2’-c:4’,5’-c’]dithiophene-4,8-dione)]. We build a molecular representation of the polymer, consisting of two identical monomer molecules. The second monomer is connected to the first with a covalent C-C bond and rotated by 180 ◦ around the polymer chain. Overall, this structure has 314 atoms and has lattice constants of 3.98 nm , 0.44 nm , and 2.28 nm . The final, geometry optimized structure is visualized in Figure 2(a). For clarity, Figure 2(b) shows the individual monomer units highlighted in different colors.

The homogenization approach, which we used here, is based on the effective T-matrix ( Teff ). The T-matrix Tmolecule of the individual molecular units is used to construct the collective response of the bulk materials. Stack architecture 

The considered solar cell stack is shown in Figure 5. Sunlight enters the stack through the glass substrate with a thickness of 1 μm. It is followed by: 150 nm ITO, 30 nm ZnO, 200 nm of photoactive material, 50 nm of PEDOT:PSS, 10 nm Ag and 140 nm PEDOT:PSS. All layers are assumed to be stratified isotropic media. The Ag layer is an approximation for a semi-transparent back contact of silver nanowires. The photoactive material is defined as a 1:1 blend ratio in volume of PM6 and one molecule of the HOPV test dataset. PM6 thereby acts as a donor polymer and the HOPV molecule as an acceptor polymer.
