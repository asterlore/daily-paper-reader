ABSTRACT
A central challenge in de novo protein design is generating proteins with desired
structural and functional properties. Existing design models typically require a
dedicated input representation for each property, limiting the design objectives
they support. We develop Prompteina, the first all-atom structure generation
model that supports new and broader design objectives specified in natural lan-
guage, analogous to text-to-image generation. To train Prompteina, we construct
Prompteina-Data, a paired text–protein structure dataset of 1.8M structures and
50M+ captions describing global and residue-specific structural and functional
properties. We adapt Proteina-Complexa, a pretrained structure generation model,
through text-conditioned pretraining and finetuning that aligns residues with their
corresponding text. Prompteina outperforms baselines on 15 of 18 structural prop-
erties and generates proteins that more closely match descriptions of their intended
functions. In binder design, it leads baselines on at least 13 of 20 targets and im-
proves interface confidence by 14% over the strongest baseline. With iterative in
silico feedback, Prompteina’s gain over non-iterative generation is 57% larger than
the next-best baseline on the hardest targets. Finally, we experimentally validate
Prompteina designs against GM2A, a target with no reported de novo binders. We
identify nine binders, with the strongest at an apparent KD of 107 nM. These re-
sults establish natural language as an effective and powerful conditioning interface
for protein structure generation. Code, data and model weights on

INTRODUCTION
A central challenge in de novo protein design is generating proteins with specified properties, from
their overall fold or function to properties of the individual residues that make up the protein. Ex-
isting protein structure generation models allow control over such properties, but a custom input
representation must typically be engineered for each property to condition generation (Watson et al.,
2023; Geffner et al., 2025; Stark et al., 2026). For instance, a protein’s desired fold may be rep-
resented by a single categorical label, and its desired secondary structure by a sequence that labels
each residue as part of a helix, strand, or loop. While this form of conditioning provides precise
control, the need to define a separate representation for each property limits the range of design
objectives that any one model can support.
An ideal conditioning approach would support a diverse set of properties without the need to en-
gineer a custom representation for each. Image generation provides a precedent: natural language
offers a unified conditioning interface for expressing diverse visual attributes that would otherwise
require task-specific labels or controls (Reed et al., 2016; Rombach et al., 2022).
We introduce Prompteina, the first text-conditioned all-atom protein structure generation model
that supports diverse design objectives through a single natural-language interface. Given an input
prompt, Prompteina jointly generates an all-atom protein structure and sequence conditioned on the
prompt’s text embeddings. By contrast, existing approaches either use text to guide structure gen-
eration only at inference time (Ingraham et al., 2023) or use it to generate sequences that must then
be folded into structures (Dai et al., 2025; Pei et al., 2026). This limits robust, fine-grained control
over both structure and sequence. By directly conditioning the generation of both modalities on text,
Prompteina enables steering of global properties such as fold class alongside residue-specific proper-
ties including specific disulfide bonds and binder–target contacts (Fig. 1; App. E.4). Prompteina also
complements work on agentic protein design by letting language-model agents pass specifications
directly to the structure generator in natural language rather than translate them into fixed, model-
specific inputs that constrain which design decisions they can express (Shanehsazzadeh, 2026).
Training Prompteina for direct, fine-grained control requires paired text–structure data describing
both global and residue-specific properties. Existing text–protein datasets, however, pair sequences
with only high-level functional descriptions (Dai et al., 2025; Song et al., 2026). We construct
Prompteina-Data, comprising 1.8M structures and 52.4M captions describing both functional and
structural properties. We transform annotations mined from databases and scientific literature or
computed directly from structures into diverse natural-language captions and annotate, where pos-
sible, correspondences between phrases in the captions and the residues they describe (§2.1). Using
this data, we extend Complexa (Didi et al., 2026), a pretrained all-atom protein structure gener-
ation model, with two text-conditioning pathways (§2.2). We adapt the model at scale through
text-conditioned continual pretraining on Prompteina-Data, then fine-tune it with direct supervision
of the correspondences between caption text and residues for finer residue-level control (§2.3).
Across five groups of structural properties, Prompteina leads evaluated baselines on 15 of 18 tasks,
supports more properties than any single baseline, and generates proteins that more closely match
descriptions of their intended functions (§§3.1.1–3.1.2). At finer structural scales, Prompteina steers
the number of residues at the binder–target interface across 20 targets (ρ > 0.99, §3.2.1). Beyond
steering individual interface properties, we evaluate Prompteina in agent-guided protein binder de-
sign. The same language-model agent writes specifications based on the properties supported by
each model’s conditioning interface. Prompteina outperforms every baseline on at least 13 of 20
targets and raises interface confidence by 14% over the strongest baseline (§3.2.2). When we al-
low the agent to iteratively refine designs, Prompteina shows larger gains over matched-budget i.i.d.
generation than either baseline, especially on the most difficult targets (§3.2.3). As a proof of con-
cept, we conducted a prospective, agent-guided design campaign against GM2 ganglioside activator
protein (GM2A), a therapeutic target implicated in GM2 gangliosidosis (Deschenes et al., 2023).
GM2A has no reported de novo binders and was one of three targets without a hit in a prior cam-
paign (Stark et al., 2026). In our campaign, nine of 89 assayed designs were classified as binders,
with the strongest yielding an overall apparent KD of 107 nM across three replicates (§3.3).
These results establish natural language as an effective conditioning interface, enabling structural
and functional control across scales and integrating language-based reasoning and iterative refine-
ment into protein design. We make our code, data, and model weights available on GitHub.

TEXT-GUIDED PROTEIN STRUCTURE GENERATION
A natural-language conditioning interface should let protein designers specify properties across
structural scales, from global properties of the whole protein, such as fold class and overall shape,
to residue-specific properties, such as disulfide bonds, catalytic sites, and binder–target contacts.
We therefore construct Prompteina-Data, a dataset pairing protein structures with natural-language
descriptions spanning these scales (§2.1), and use it to train Prompteina, an all-atom structure gen-
eration model conditioned on natural language for monomer and binder design (Fig. 2; §§2.2–2.3).
2.1
PROMPTEINA-DATA: TEXT–STRUCTURE DATA
We construct Prompteina-Data from experimental structures in the Protein Data Bank (PDB; Berman
et al., 2000), predicted structures in the AlphaFold Database (AFDB; Varadi et al., 2022), and Ted-
dymer synthetic dimers (Didi et al., 2026) (App. B.1). Prompteina generates one chain at a time,
so we treat each chain as a separate entry, giving about 1.8M entries. The dataset includes up to
59 annotations per entry, derived from database fields and atomic coordinates (Fig. 2a; App. B.2).
Annotations span global properties, including fold class and compactness, and residue-specific prop-
erties with residue positions, such as disulfide bonds, catalytic sites, and protein–protein contacts.
At inference, prompts may specify one property or combine several, so we vary caption content
and length in the training data (Wu et al., 2025) (App. B.3). For each entry, we sample several
subsets that differ in the number and choice of annotations, then prompt Qwen3-235B-A22B (Yang
et al., 2025) to write one caption from each subset (Fig. 2b; App. B.4). In total, we generate 52.4M
captions, averaging 29 captions per entry. We use an LLM judge to compare 80 generated captions
with their ground-truth annotations (App. B.5). Across this sample, the generated captions match
their ground-truth annotations closely (97.8% recall, 89.8% precision, and 95.7% accuracy).
2.2
PROMPTEINA: TEXT-CONDITIONED STRUCTURE GENERATION
Our model, Prompteina, builds on the pretrained Complexa model (Didi et al., 2026) (App. C.1),
adding two text-conditioning pathways and an auxiliary text–residue alignment objective.
Global and residue-level text conditioning.
Complexa’s denoiser generates backbone coordi-
nates and one side-chain latent per residue before a frozen variational autoencoder decodes both
into an all-atom structure and amino acid sequence. To give every residue the same conditioning
signal for global properties, we broadcast a pooled embedding from frozen Qwen3-Embedding-8B
(Zhang et al., 2025) through adaptive layer normalization. For residue-specific properties, each
residue attends to frozen Qwen3-8B token states (Yang et al., 2025) via cross-attention before pro-
tein self-attention in each denoiser layer (Fig. 2d; Apps. C.2–C.3). Two-layer MLPs map both

combination, which performs best across tasks (App. D).
Text–residue alignment.
In text-conditioned pretraining, we train the denoiser using a flow-
matching loss but do not directly supervise which residues correspond to each text span. Prompteina-
Data contains residue positions for many annotations, which we use to construct text–residue align-
ment annotations (Fig. 2c). For each Prompteina-Data annotation selected for a caption, we call its
associated residue positions the annotation’s residue span. A residue span contains, for example, the
helix residues for a mainly alpha fold annotation and the participating residues for a disulfide bond or
protein–protein contact. We attempt to locate the corresponding text span with regular expressions
and TF-IDF-weighted token matching. If either span is unavailable, we retain the caption–structure
pair but omit that alignment annotation. We validate span matching on held-out captions (App. B.6).
We train an auxiliary alignment head on these annotations, following work using segmenta-
tion masks for token-level supervision in text-to-image diffusion (Wang et al., 2024).

2.3
TRAINING AND INFERENCE
Training.
We first adapt the Complexa denoiser to natural-language conditioning using newly ini-
tialized parameters for pooled and token-level text conditioning (§2.2; App. C.5). These parameters
are first trained with the denoiser frozen before we unfreeze it for joint text-conditioned contin-
ual pretraining on Prompteina-Data, following staged training in text-to-image models (Chen et al.,
2023) (Fig. 2e; App. C.6). For alignment fine-tuning, we freeze the denoiser and update text projec-
tors, cross-attention layers, and alignment head. We describe other Complexa training modifications
that improve performance and their ablations (Apps. C.6, D).
Inference.
At inference, Prompteina takes a prompt and protein length and, for binder design, a
target structure and optional target contact residues. Prompteina samples backbone coordinates and
side-chain latents with the same stochastic differential equation as Complexa and uses classifier-
free guidance to adjust text-conditioning strength (Ho & Salimans, 2022) (App. C.7). The frozen
variational autoencoder decodes these into an all-atom structure and amino acid sequence.

EXPERIMENTS
We show that Prompteina’s natural-language conditioning interface controls structure generation
from prompts specifying structural and functional properties (§3.1). This control extends to pro-
tein binding, where Prompteina directly steers the binder–target interface (§3.2.1) and gives agent-
written designs higher interface confidence than state-of-the-art binder design models do (§3.2.2).
Iterative revision yields the largest interface-confidence gains with Prompteina, especially on the
hardest targets (§3.2.3). Finally, we prospectively design and experimentally test candidates against
GM2A, identifying nine binders among 89 designs assayed by surface plasmon resonance (§3.3).

TEXT-GUIDED STRUCTURE GENERATION
We first test Prompteina on prompts for structural properties (§3.1.1) or protein function (§3.1.2).

3.1.1
CONTROL OF STRUCTURAL PROPERTIES
Baselines.
For structural control, we benchmark text-guided models that accept free-form prompts
and property-specific models that take labels or structured inputs.
Text-guided models receive
the same free-form prompts as Prompteina: Chroma (text) uses a structure-captioning model for
inference-time text guidance (Ingraham et al., 2023), whereas Pinal (Dai et al., 2025) and BioMa-
trix (Pei et al., 2026) generate amino acid sequences from natural-language descriptions, which we
fold with ESMFold. Property-specific models, comprising Chroma (label) (Ingraham et al., 2023),
Proteina (Geffner et al., 2025), and RFdiffusion (Watson et al., 2023), receive their native labels or
structured inputs. For each baseline, we specify every property it supports and evaluate it only on
tasks expressible through its conditioning interface. All use paper-default settings (App. E).
Evaluation.
We compare Prompteina with six baselines across 18 tasks organized into five prop-
erty groups: fold and topology, secondary structure, tertiary structure, global shape and surface
properties, and combinations of fold class with secondary-structure or disulfide counts (Table 1a).
Complete task definitions and scoring procedures are included in App. E. For categorical properties,
we report accuracy, and for count-valued properties such as helix count, we report the Spearman
rank correlation (ρ) between requested and realized values measured in each generated structure.
Prompteina enables new and broader forms of structural control.
Across the benchmark,
Prompteina controls all five property groups and leads on 15 of 18 tasks (Table 1a and Table 11).

We next test whether Prompteina can generate structures for protein families, such as lysozymes and

3.2
TEXT-GUIDED PROTEIN BINDER DESIGN
Designing strong binders requires control over both global binder properties, such as fold class, and
the size and chemistry of the binder–target interface (Cao et al., 2022; Watson et al., 2023). We test
whether Prompteina can steer these properties through natural language (§3.2.1) and produce better
predicted binders than methods using predefined, property-specific inputs (§§3.2.2–3.2.3).
3.2.1
BINDER–TARGET INTERFACE STEERING
We first test control over binder–target interface size, which is associated with binding specificity
(Prasad Bahadur et al., 2004). We use interface residue count, defined as the number of binder
residues that contact the target, as a proxy (App. G.1). Across 20 targets from the Complexa binder-
design benchmark (Didi et al., 2026), we generate binders with Prompteina while requesting inter-
face residue counts from 8 to 30 and holding the target and binder-length distribution fixed. For each
target, we compare requested and realized counts using Spearman correlation (ρ) and a linear fit.
Prompteina reliably steers binder–target interface residue count.

3.2.2
AGENTIC PROTEIN BINDER DESIGN
Designing a binder against a new target begins with research into the shape and chemistry of its
surface, which epitopes known partners engage, and what scaffolds have succeeded against related
targets. Previous work shows that LLM agents can conduct this research and operate design tools end
to end (Ghafarollahi & Buehler, 2024; Shanehsazzadeh, 2026). However, an agent can communicate
design decisions only through the conditioning inputs a model accepts. Existing models require a
separate input representation for each property and therefore support a narrower range of properties,
whereas Prompteina’s single natural-language interface supports a broader range. We test whether
this broader range of supported properties allows the same agent to produce better binders.
Setup and baselines.
We compare Prompteina with Complexa (Didi et al., 2026), RFdiffusion3
(Butcher et al., 2025), and BoltzGen (Stark et al., 2026) on Complexa’s 20-target benchmark. For
each target, Claude Opus 4.6 compiles a research dossier and writes 200 binder specifications using
each model’s supported input properties (App. G.2). Each model samples one structure per specifi-
cation. We also run all three baselines in a non-agentic setting, where they receive only the target
structure, binder-length range, and fixed benchmark hotspots. We do not run Complexa with the
agent because its released checkpoint supports no additional input properties (App. G.3).
Evaluation.
We score each design under three sequence conditions: the generated sequence
(self), full ProteinMPNN redesign (MPNN), and fixed-interface redesign (MPNN-FI) (Dauparas
et al., 2022). ESMFold2-Fast ipSAEmin separates binders from non-binders more accurately than
the AlphaProteo AF2 filters used by Complexa (App. G.4), corroborating Shanehsazzadeh (2026), so
we use it to measure binder–target interface confidence throughout our binder-design experiments.

3.2.3
ITERATIVE BINDER PROMPT OPTIMIZATION
Recent design models perform inference-time search by scoring partial structures during generation
and retaining promising candidates (Didi et al., 2026; Rector-Brooks et al., 2026). Analogously,
when agents call design models as tools, they can use in-silico or experimental feedback on com-
pleted designs to identify promising design strategies and revise the next batch of specifications
(Shanehsazzadeh, 2026). We test whether this iterative revision yields larger gains when the agent
uses Prompteina’s broader conditioning interface than when it uses RFdiffusion3 or BoltzGen. For
each method and target, we run four rounds of 50 designs, matching the 200-design budget of §3.2.2.
Round 1 contains 50 i.i.d. designs generated as in §3.2.2. Before each later round, the agent reads
all scores so far and writes 50 new specifications (App. H.1). At budgets of 50, 100, 150, and 200
designs, we compare the accumulated iterative pool with matched-budget i.i.d. generation.

RELATED WORK
Protein structure generation.
Early diffusion and flow-matching models generated protein back-
bones (Yim et al., 2023; Watson et al., 2023; Geffner et al., 2025), while newer all-atom models
jointly generate structure and sequence (Geffner et al., 2026; Didi et al., 2026; Butcher et al., 2025;
Rector-Brooks et al., 2026; Stark et al., 2026). Existing protein structure generation models use
custom input representations to specify desired folds, motif coordinates, target hotspots, and in-
teractions, and some also require separate checkpoints or inference-time classifiers and rewards
(Watson et al., 2023; Didi et al., 2026; Rector-Brooks et al., 2026; Ingraham et al., 2023). RFdif-
fusion3 and BoltzGen combine many controls in one model, but each property has a distinct model
input and its own training annotations (Butcher et al., 2025; Stark et al., 2026). ESM3 jointly gener-
ates sequence, structure, and function through dedicated token tracks (Hayes et al., 2025). Chroma
provides the closest precedent for using natural language to control protein structure generation. It
guides sampling through a separate structure-to-caption model rather than conditioning its denoiser
on text (Ingraham et al., 2023). This approach requires gradients during sampling and does not use
supervised text–residue alignment. The authors report that its natural-language conditioning remains
sensitive to guidance strength. Prompteina instead directly conditions the denoising process on text,
enabling a single natural-language interface to specify global and residue-specific properties.
Protein–text multimodal models.
Protein–text multimodal models support alignment, caption-
ing, and text-guided generation. Alignment models learn shared representations for retrieval and
classification (Xu et al., 2023b; Su et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2023a), while captioning models describe
protein sequences or structures (Liu et al., 2024; Fei et al., 2025; Abdine et al., 2024). Neither
generates protein sequences or structures from text. Text-guided protein models such as ProteinDT,
Pinal, InstructPro, InstructBioMol, and BioMatrix produce amino-acid sequences rather than struc-
tures (Liu et al., 2025; Dai et al., 2025; Song et al., 2026; Zhuang et al., 2025; Pei et al., 2026).
Their prompts usually provide high-level functional descriptions, and the resulting sequences must
then be folded into structures. ProteinDT uses a text-aligned latent representation, while Pinal pre-
dicts Foldseek structure tokens before generating the sequence. HelixProtX emits backbone torsion
angles from text, but its pretrained parameters are unavailable for evaluation (Chen et al., 2024).
Prompteina instead directly conditions the generation of protein sequence and all-atom structure on
text, with supervised text–residue alignment connecting phrases to the residues they describe.
We provide an extended survey of related work in Appendix A, including conditioning interfaces
supported by protein structure generation models and their property-specific representations.
5
DISCUSSION
We introduce Prompteina, a text-conditioned all-atom protein structure generation model, and its
paired text–structure dataset, Prompteina-Data. Prompteina uses a single natural-language inter-
face to control global and residue-specific structural and functional properties. Prompteina matches
or exceeds existing approaches on shared properties while supporting a broader range. Its natural-
language interface lets an agent turn research directly into design prompts, yielding higher inter-
face confidence than the same agent achieves with existing models. Experimental validation then
identified Prompteina binders for GM2A, a target with no previously reported de novo binders.
As language-model agents improve, specialist generative models need interfaces that let agents
use scientific knowledge and language-based reasoning to guide generation. Prompteina makes
this connection more seamless: agents can incorporate experimental feedback into rich specifica-
tions realized directly in generated structures. Together, these results establish natural language as
an effective, powerful conditioning interface that lets agents directly connect scientific knowledge,
language-based reasoning, and experimental results to protein structure generation and design.

EXTENDED RELATED WORK
Recent protein structure generation models control different properties through distinct architectural
components, training procedures, and inference-time operations. We survey these mechanisms be-
fore describing the text-conditioning techniques that Prompteina adopts from image generation. The
comparison focuses on the dedicated input formats and procedures that existing models require for
each controlled property.
RFdiffusion (Watson et al., 2023).
RFdiffusion fine-tunes a pretrained RoseTTAFold structure
prediction network as a denoiser for the reverse diffusion process. Five separately fine-tuned vari-
ants handle unconditional generation, fold-conditioned generation, binder design, fold-conditioned
binder design, and enzyme active-site scaffolding.
Fold control takes per-residue secondary-
structure labels and a residue-by-residue block adjacency matrix that specifies long-range contacts.
Binder design takes a user-specified subset of target-chain residues as interface hotspots and can
combine them with fold properties to control binder scaffold topology. Motif scaffolding fixes the
sequence identity and three-dimensional coordinates of motif backbone and side-chain atoms during
denoising. Symmetric oligomer design takes a cyclic, dihedral, tetrahedral, octahedral, or icosahe-
dral point group and initializes noise with the corresponding symmetry. Each task therefore uses a
distinct input format and separately trained model.
RFdiffusion2 (Ahern et al., 2026).
RFdiffusion2 uses the RoseTTAFold All-Atom architecture
and combines Euclidean flow matching for atom coordinates with Riemannian flow matching on
SE(3) for backbone frames. The model is trained from random initialization and focuses on enzyme
active-site scaffolding. Atomic motif conditioning supplies individual heavy atoms from catalytic
side chains. During training, a random residue subset forms the motif, and each selected residue is
represented either as a backbone frame or as an atomized subgraph of its heavy atoms. Unindexed
18


Preprint
motif conditioning removes sequence indices from these residues, requiring the model to infer their
positions in the scaffold. Relative accessible surface area (RASA) labels mark each ligand atom
as buried or exposed during 50% of training. An origin pseudo-atom gives an approximate center-
of-mass target for the scaffold relative to the ligand. Stochastic centering prevents the model from
recovering the motif–scaffold offset directly from partially noised inputs by adding a small Gaussian
translation to the training structure. A single model handles these tasks, but each property has a
dedicated input format and training procedure.
RFdiffusion3 (Butcher et al., 2025).
RFdiffusion3 uses a transformer-based U-Net for all-atom
protein generation. A single model handles unconditional generation, protein, nucleic-acid, and
small-molecule binder design, enzyme active-site scaffolding, and symmetric oligomer design
through several conditioning mechanisms. Atom-level features label target or ligand atoms with
property-specific annotations at varying rates during training. Donor and acceptor labels direct com-
plementary hydrogen bonds, RASA labels divide ligand-atom solvent accessibility into four burial
bins, and hotspots mark target atoms near the requested binder–target interface. Appended tokens
represent catalytic residues for unindexed motif scaffolding, leaving the model to infer each catalytic
residue’s sequence position. Inference-time operations symmetrize each diffusion step for oligomer
design and position the generated protein relative to the target center of mass. For nucleic-acid and
small-molecule targets, RFdiffusion3 can jointly diffuse the target conformation with the protein.
The shared model still requires a distinct input format and annotation pipeline for each property.
Proteina series (Geffner et al., 2025; 2026; Didi et al., 2026).
Proteina (Geffner et al., 2025)
is a non-equivariant flow-matching model for protein backbone generation. CATH fold condition-
ing uses learnable embeddings for a hierarchy of 5 classes, 43 architectures, and 1,336 topologies,
injected through adaptive layer normalization (AdaLN). Hierarchical dropout trains unconditional
generation and each level of the hierarchy. The model drops all labels with probability 0.5, retains
only class with probability 0.1, retains class and architecture with probability 0.15, and retains all
three levels with probability 0.25. La-Proteina (Geffner et al., 2026) extends Proteina to all-atom
generation through a partially latent representation and adds indexed and unindexed motif scaffold-
ing, but omits fold conditioning. Complexa (Didi et al., 2026) extends La-Proteina to binder design
with separate protein–protein, CATH-conditioned protein–protein, and ligand–protein checkpoints.
Protein and small-molecule targets use different representation pipelines. Binder design takes target
interface hotspots, and the CATH-conditioned checkpoint uses Proteina’s hierarchical embeddings
and AdaLN but applies only the class level at inference.
Chroma (Ingraham et al., 2023).
Chroma composes a library of separate conditioners at sam-
pling time. Each independently trained or hand-designed conditioner steers generation through its
gradient with respect to the noised structure. Symmetry conditioning tessellates an asymmetric sub-
unit into Cn, Dn, T, O, or I point groups, and shape conditioning minimizes an optimal-transport
distance to a user-provided point cloud. Fold-class conditioning inverts a separately trained CATH
classifier through its gradients. Substructure conditioning fixes motif coordinates during registered
or unregistered scaffolding. Text conditioning trains a structure-to-caption predictor and uses the
gradient of caption log-likelihood to steer generation toward the prompt. Guidance strength must
be balanced against the prior: weak gradients have little effect, whereas strong gradients overwhelm
the prior and degrade structural coherence. The authors report that guidance effects vary widely
and that robust natural-language conditioning remains challenging. The ProCap captioner pairs a
frozen structure encoder with GPT-Neo 125M and is trained on approximately 45,000 captions from
PDB descriptions and UniProt functional comments. The authors report that this dataset size con-
strained their architecture choices. Any subset of conditioners can be composed at inference, but
each requires a trained classifier, analytical energy function, or separate gradient computation.
HelixProtX (Chen et al., 2024).
HelixProtX is a multimodal language model that maps among
protein sequence, structure, and text. Generating structure from text emits backbone torsion angles,
and control is reported at the level of the whole structure rather than individual residues or mo-
tifs. Its released repository does not include the pretrained HelixProtX parameters, which prevents
evaluation on our benchmark.1

Pinal (Dai et al., 2025).
Pinal generates amino-acid sequences from prompts that describe a pro-
tein’s function and specify its length. It first converts the prompt into Foldseek structure tokens,
which encode the local backbone geometry around each residue. A structure-aware protein lan-
guage model then predicts an amino acid at each position from these tokens and the prompt. Pinal
returns a sequence, which we fold with ESMFold to obtain coordinates for structural evaluation.
BioMatrix (Pei et al., 2026).
BioMatrix represents natural language, amino-acid sequences, pro-
tein backbones, and small molecules as token sequences processed by one decoder-only model. In-
structions specify which representation the model receives and which representation it generates for
a given task. For the text-guided protein design task evaluated here, BioMatrix receives a functional
description and generates an amino-acid sequence. BioMatrix has no length input, so we constrain
decoding to the sampled length and fold each sequence with ESMFold for structural evaluation.
DISCO (Rector-Brooks et al., 2026).
DISCO jointly generates protein sequences and structures
with Euclidean coordinate diffusion and absorbing-state masked sequence diffusion. Biomolecular
context enters as discrete tokens with three-dimensional coordinates for small molecules, metallo-
cofactors, reactive intermediates, or nucleic acids, which are denoised with the protein. Property-
specific control uses Feynman–Kac reward tilting, with a custom reward function, distance thresh-
olds, and hyperparameters for properties such as disulfides or cation–π interactions. Target-specific
binding guidance evaluates two aligned denoiser calls per timestep and requires a hyperparameter
sweep for each target pair.
PAR (Qu et al., 2026).
PAR uses an autoregressive model and flow-matching decoder to gener-
ate protein backbones by progressively upsampling a coarse representation. Its two conditioning
mechanisms operate on this hierarchy without fine-tuning. Structural prompting replaces the coars-
est generation step with user-provided three-dimensional points that define an approximate layout,
which PAR then upsamples into a full backbone. Motif scaffolding restores the ground-truth co-
ordinates of motif residues after every prediction scale, keeping the motif fixed as the surrounding
scaffold is generated.
BoltzGen (Stark et al., 2026).
BoltzGen extends Boltz-2 into a single model for folding, un-
conditional design, binder design, motif scaffolding, and non-protein interface design through seven
training tasks. Four conditioning mechanisms use separate representations and training distributions.
Binding-site conditioning assigns binding, non-binding, or unspecified labels to target residues.
Structure groups use a distance matrix to preserve pairwise distances among residues in the same
group. Secondary-structure conditioning labels each designed residue as helix, strand, coil, or un-
specified. Covalent-bond conditioning supplies an atom-pair bond matrix for cyclic proteins, disul-
fide staples, and helicons. Each property has its own representation, dropout scheme, and training-
time sampling distribution.
ESM3 (Hayes et al., 2025).
ESM3 is a multimodal masked language model that generates protein
sequence, structure, and function, with each modality or property represented by a dedicated token
track. For conditioning structure generation, partial information can be provided on any combina-
tion of property tracks: per-residue secondary structure labels, solvent-accessible surface area bins,
function keywords derived from InterPro and Gene Ontology terms, residue-level functional anno-
tations, partial structure (as discrete tokens or atomic coordinates), and partial sequence. Each track
requires its own tokenization: structure uses a VQ-VAE with a 4096-entry codebook, function key-
words require parsing free text into a 68,103-entry vocabulary, quantizing via 8 locality-sensitive
hash functions, and a dedicated 3-layer transformer to invert the hashing. Each track also has its
own noise schedule and dropout rate. Adding a new conditioning property requires designing a to-
kenization scheme, selecting a noise schedule, and training any auxiliary encoders or decoders the
representation needs. Chroma and RFdiffusion already represent ESM3’s per-residue secondary-
structure input in the property-specific comparison, so we omit a redundant ESM3 run.
Genie series (Lin & AlQuraishi, 2023; Lin et al., 2024; 2026).
Genie (Lin & AlQuraishi, 2023)
generates protein backbones by diffusing Cα coordinates and conditions only on protein length.
Genie 2 (Lin et al., 2024) introduces motif scaffolding by training entirely on scaffolding tasks. It
represents motif sequence as one-hot vectors in the single-residue features and motif structure as
20


Preprint
SE(3)-invariant pairwise Cα distances. This representation leaves Genie 2 to infer the relative con-
figuration of multiple motifs. Genie 3 (Lin et al., 2026) uses a branched-polymer representation for
all-atom generation and adds binder design. Its training distribution allocates 90% of examples to
motif scaffolding and 10% to binder design, with subtasks that vary the motif and binder informa-
tion supplied. Binder design takes target interface hotspots and can expand them to nearby surface
residues or iteratively add residues from successful prior designs.
Text conditioning for diffusion models.
Protein structure generation models increasingly use
transformer backbones also found in text-conditioned image models (Geffner et al., 2025; Didi
et al., 2026; Butcher et al., 2025; Rector-Brooks et al., 2026; Stark et al., 2026). That literature
supplies the components Prompteina uses for natural-language conditioning. Frozen pretrained lan-
guage models encode the text (Saharia et al., 2022), and later systems combine encoders to capture
complementary information (Podell et al., 2023; Esser et al., 2024). Cross-attention carries token
representations into the denoiser (Rombach et al., 2022), and adaptive layer normalization supplies
a pooled representation (Peebles & Xie, 2023). PixArt-α combines these pathways and first trains
the text projection while freezing the pretrained generation model (Chen et al., 2023). Classifier-free
guidance trains conditional and unconditional predictions by dropping the conditioning on a fixed
fraction of examples, then scales their difference at inference (Ho & Salimans, 2022). Prompteina
adapts these components to protein structure generation.

B.6
TEXT–RESIDUE ALIGNMENT ANNOTATIONS
After generating captions, we map text spans to residue positions to produce supervised align-
ment masks. The selected source annotations identify which property text to locate in each cap-
tion.
We find that text with regular-expression anchors and TF–IDF-weighted token matching
against the formatted annotation. Captions that include residue positions use direct matches to
identifiers such as Cys8 (§B.3).
For example, given the caption in Box B.1 and the annota-
tion Structural class:
Alpha Beta, the annotator computes TF-IDF weights for the
tokens structural, class, alpha, and beta, scans the caption, and identifies alpha-beta
structural class as the best-matching region. We then map this span to the helix and strand
residues within the CATH domain boundary.
The property type determines the residue set assigned to each matched text span.
Secondary-
structure phrases map to helix, strand, or sheet residues, and CATH fold labels map to secondary-
structure residues within the annotated domain boundary. Disulfides, salt bridges, metal coordina-
tion, and interface interactions map to both participating residues as pairs. Whole-chain properties
such as contact order and compactness map to all modelled positions. Database-level properties,
including ECOD labels, quaternary structure, Gene Ontology terms, subcellular locations, and free-
text function, receive no residue mask. We omit an annotation from the mask when its text span
cannot be located reliably.

C.6
TRAINING
Training first introduces the text pathways for monomer generation and then adapts the model to
generate a binder against a fixed target structure. The monomer phase contains three stages.
Monomer training.
We follow the staged adaptation used by PixArt-α (Chen et al.,
2023).
The first stage warms up the text projectors gs and gt, cross-attention parameters
{WQ, WK, WV , WO, αℓ}, and AdaLN conditioning layers on paired text–structure data while
freezing the pretrained denoiser vϕ. The second stage unfreezes all parameters for text-conditioned
continual pretraining on PDB and AFDB monomers with LFM. The third stage removes the flow-
matching loss and fine-tunes the alignment pathway with Lalign. During this stage, we freeze the
denoiser, AdaLN layers, cross-attention gates, and query and key projections. We train the value
and output projections in the last five cross-attention layers together with the text projectors and
alignment head Wa.
26


Preprint
Restricting alignment fine-tuning to the late value and output projections preserves fold accuracy.
Higher learning rates or unfrozen query and key projections degrade fold accuracy within one to two
epochs, whereas training the last five cross-attention layers matches all fourteen layers with one third
as many trainable parameters. During warmup and both continual-pretraining stages, we replace cs
and ct with zero vectors with probability puncond = 0.2. This dropout trains the unconditional
prediction used for classifier-free guidance.
Binder training.
We continue training on Teddymer dimers (Didi et al., 2026) and PDB multi-
mers. Only the binder tokens are noised, leaving the target tokens clean and fixed for the denoiser to
attend to at every step. An optional hotspot mask marks target residues that the binder should con-
tact and enters through the pair and sequence features used for the target. We sample hotspots from
true contact residues with dropout so that the training distribution also includes prompts without a
specified epitope. Input text describes the binder, while the target structure enters separately.
Training the final monomer and binder models required approximately 12,900 H100-equivalent
GPU-hours, or about 67 days on one eight-H100 node. This estimate excludes ablations, dataset
construction, and evaluation.

Training distribution rebalancing.
A small number of architectures account for most PDB
chains, so uniform chain sampling presents common folds many times per epoch and rare folds
only a few times. We cluster structures by the structural features described in their captions and in-
terpolate each cluster’s sampling weight between its empirical frequency and a uniform distribution
over clusters. A parameter β ∈[0, 1] controls this interpolation. Setting β=0 recovers uniform sam-
pling over chains, and larger values progressively upweight rare feature combinations, by roughly
an order of magnitude for the rarest architectures. A cosine schedule alternates between the natural
distribution and phases that emphasize rare folds. Rebalancing produces the largest measured gain
in fold control, and cosine cycling performs comparably to a monotonic ramp (CATH class accuracy
92.0% with cosine cycling, 87.3% with a linear ramp, and 51.3% without rebalancing; App. D).
Timestep distribution.
Complexa samples timesteps from ptx = 0.02 · Unif(0, 1) + 0.98 ·
Beta(1.9, 1.0) and ptz = 0.02 · Unif(0, 1) + 0.98 · Beta(1.0, 1.5), concentrating training near clean
data (t →1). We replace the backbone schedule with ptx = Unif(0, 1) and retain the side-chain
schedule ptz. The uniform backbone schedule gives more weight to intermediate timesteps, where
the model makes structural decisions and text conditioning is most informative.

ABLATIONS
We ablate the three choices that determine how text enters the model and which training examples
and timesteps supply its learning signal: the conditioning architecture, the β data-sampling schedule,
and the noise schedule. All ablations share the same parent checkpoint, dataset, and training duration
of 20 epochs. Evaluation uses the same protocol as §3.1: 50 structures per prompt, 318 prompts,
and CFG w=2.0.
Variants.
Complete model: pooled + cross-attention, cosine β cycling, uniform noise. Cross-attn
only: removes the pooled/AdaLN text signal. Pooled only: removes cross-attention and the token-
level encoder. No β: uniform cluster sampling (β=0), removing rare-class rebalancing. Linear β:
monotonic β ramp 0→1 (vs. cosine cycling). Default noise: backbone Cα noise sampled from
Beta(1.9, 1.0) instead of uniform.
Ablation results.
The complete model obtains 92.0% CATH class accuracy and 27.0% CATH
architecture accuracy (Table 8). CATH class accuracy is 86.7% with cross-attention alone, 68.7%
with the pooled pathway alone, and 92.0% with both. It is 51.3% without rare-class rebalancing,
87.3% with a linear β schedule, and 92.0% with cosine β cycling. Uniform and inherited Beta
backbone-noise schedules give 92.0% and 68.7% CATH class accuracy, respectively.
Natural-language and CATH-label comparison.
We compare natural language with a dedicated
hierarchical CATH-label conditioner using the same structure generation model, training structures,
training step, β schedule, requested labels and lengths, evaluator, and CFG. Pooled text and CATH
labels perform similarly through AdaLN, whereas adding token cross-attention to the text path sub-
stantially raises CATH class and architecture accuracy, and longer CATH-label training to 3.2 times
the matched budget does not close this gap (Table 9).

MONOMER GENERATION EVALUATION
We evaluate text-guided monomer generation across 18 tasks: 14 single-property and 4 composite
tasks. Each prompt produces 50 structures with classifier-free guidance at w=2.0 and 400 SDE dif-
fusion steps. A prompt requesting property x draws lengths from the empirical training distribution
of structures annotated with x, using a piecewise-linear CDF constructed from 11 quantile break-
points (P5 through P95). For CATH class and architecture, this protocol follows Proteina’s treatment
of empirical compatibility between chain length and CATH label (Geffner et al., 2025) and avoids
unrealistic label–length combinations.
Fold and topology.
CATH class (3 prompts) and architecture (35 prompts) are evaluated with
GearNet (Zhang et al., 2023), a pre-trained graph neural network for fold classification. We report
the fraction of generated structures whose GearNet-predicted CATH label matches the requested
label. Contact order (5 prompts) is computed from Cα contacts and binned into 5 classes. We de-
tect nine internal symmetries—cyclic groups C2–C8 and dihedral groups D2–D3—with CE-Symm
(Myers-Turnbull et al., 2014).
We evaluate multi-domain CATH class and architecture with separate sets of 16 prompts. Each
prompt specifies the class or architecture of every domain in N-to-C order, such as an N-terminal
3-Layer(αβα) Sandwich domain followed by a C-terminal Orthogonal Bundle domain. We segment
each generated structure into domains with Chainsaw (Wells et al., 2024) and classify each domain
with GearNet. A generated structure is correct only if Chainsaw finds the requested number of
domains and GearNet predicts the complete sequence of requested CATH labels in order. All text-
conditioned methods receive the same prompts. We restrict the class and architecture compositions
to those with at least 500 training examples.
Secondary structure.
We compute helix count (20 prompts), strand count (20 prompts), and sheet
count (14 prompts) from DSSP assignments (Kabsch & Sander, 1983). For each property, Spearman
ρ measures the association between requested and realized counts across all generated structures.
Matched-length control.
Property-matched length sampling is necessary because some requests
require sufficient chain length; for example, an 80-residue protein cannot reasonably contain 20
helices. For count-valued properties, however, this protocol could also make length predictive of the
requested value and thereby inflate requested–realized correlations. We test this potential confound
on helix and strand count, for which Prompteina shows strong control in Table 1b. For each property,
we select the widest contiguous five-count window whose shared P20–P80 training-length range lies
below 200 residues, requiring at least two strands. This gives helix counts 1–5 at 134–193 residues
and strand counts 2–6 at 112–169 residues.
Within each property, we reuse the exact same list of 50 lengths at every requested count, making
length uninformative about the requested value by construction. We also generate an empty-prompt
control using the same lengths. The text-conditioned correlations remain strong, whereas both con-
trol correlations are near zero (Table 12).
Tertiary structure.
We detect disulfide bonds from Sγ–Sγ distances below 2.5 ˚A in the generated
coordinates and report Spearman ρ over seven requested counts from 1 to 7. For salt bridges, we use
Asp/Glu–Lys/Arg atom-pair distances below 4.0 ˚A and report the correlation across 15 prompts.
Global shape and surface.
We evaluate compactness with three prompts each for size and shape.
Size is the radius of gyration normalized by chain length, Rg/N 0.4, and shape is the ratio of the
first two singular values of the Cα coordinate matrix. We compute solvent-accessible surface area
propensity (SAP, 3 prompts) from the solvent-accessible surface geometry.
Composite evaluations.
Four evaluations pair CATH class with a helix, strand, sheet, or disulfide
count. Both disulfide-count evaluations use counts 1–7. We report GearNet accuracy for CATH
class and Spearman ρ for the count, using the same generated samples for the two reported metrics.
Baselines.
Each baseline model is evaluated only on properties its conditioning interface can ex-
press. Chroma (Ingraham et al., 2023) text mode covers all categories via free-text caption guidance.

E.3
STRUCTURAL NOVELTY
We measure novelty only among designable structures, preventing structurally failed outputs from
appearing novel merely because they match no protein fold. For each design, Foldseek (van Kem-
pen et al., 2024) with TM-align searches the training structures used by that model, and novelty
is one minus the maximum query-normalized TM-score (Zhang & Skolnick, 2004) to any training
structure. Taking the maximum tests the nearest potential training-set match rather than average
similarity. Query normalization asks how much of the generated design is explained by that match,
so a short design contained within a longer training structure is conservatively treated as non-novel.
We use model-specific training references because the relevant memorization opportunity is the cor-
pus to which each model was exposed; consequently, cross-model values are descriptive rather than
a controlled comparison against a common database.
Prompteina’s conditional and empty-prompt samples share the same 780,195-structure PDB–AFDB
training reference, so their matched comparison isolates the effect of supplying text. Both sets begin
with 2,510 structures; 2,000 conditional and 2,138 empty-prompt structures pass the scRMSD < 2 ˚A
designability criterion and enter the novelty analysis.
For Complexa, 731 of 2,510 structures are designable. Complexa does not release its original chain-
level monomer manifest, so we reconstruct the reported 110,976-chain PDB monomer set from the
checkpoint filters and archived PDB metadata, retaining 110,969 after the checkpoint’s explicit ex-
clusions. We combine these monomers with the released 45,856-entry PDB multimer manifest.

with the 289,222 AFDB targets gives 506,972 Foldseek targets. Including both PDB components
prevents Complexa from appearing artificially novel because part of its training exposure is absent
from the search database. Both model-specific references omit Teddymer structures; this can over-
estimate absolute novelty, but the reconstructed Complexa reference corrects the previous additional

FUNCTIONAL SPECIFICATION EVALUATION
We test whether a text prompt can specify a protein family and generate recognizable members. The
structural properties in §3.1.1 have property-specific verifiers, but no corresponding classifier covers
arbitrary protein families.
Benchmark families.
The benchmark comprises 122 sequence-coherent protein families. We de-
fine each candidate family as an ECOD topology group (Cheng et al., 2014) whose PDB members
exceed 30% median pairwise sequence identity, the threshold below which alignment stops imply-
ing homology (Rost, 1999). This requirement removes topology groups that share a fold without a
single family-level referent.
Eligibility is computed from the median identity over all pairs of PDB sequences in a group before
any structure is generated. No design, method, or evaluation score enters the benchmark definition.
Prompt formats.
The models were trained with different textual vocabularies, so a single prompt
format would favor the model for which that wording is in-distribution. We evaluate every method on
four formats and report its highest score for each metric. The ECOD annotation combines an ECOD

topology name with an EC number or cofactor annotation when available, matching Prompteina’s
caption vocabulary. The functional description paraphrases a Swiss-Prot FUNCTION annotation
with Claude, matching Pinal’s training and evaluation setting. The protein metadata combines the
protein name with an EC number and subcellular location when available, matching BioMatrix’s
keyword setting. The InterPro label contains only the InterPro entry name, which is a second format
used by Pinal (Dai et al., 2025; Pei et al., 2026).

Generation protocol.
Every method generates 50 structures per family and format under the com-
mon sampling protocol (App. E). Pinal and BioMatrix emit sequences, which we fold with ESMFold
before computing structure similarity.
ProTrek similarity.
ProTrek is a contrastively trained trimodal encoder mapping protein se-
quences, structures, and text into a shared embedding space (Su et al., 2025). Both scores are
temperature-scaled cosine similarities between the prompt embedding and a generated sequence or
structure embedding.
Confidence-weighted similarity reduces the contribution of generated sequences that ESMFold pre-
dicts with low confidence without filtering any design or family. For each design, it multiplies
ProTrek similarity by mean per-residue pLDDT/100. We obtain pLDDT by applying ESMFold
to the exact generated sequence, using Prompteina’s co-designed sequence or the sequence emitted
by Pinal or BioMatrix. Scores are averaged first within each family and then across the 122 fam-
ilies, keeping the family as the unit of analysis. We estimate 95% confidence intervals by cluster-
bootstrapping the 122 families 10,000 times and taking the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.
Baseline training and metric overlap.
Pinal is designed for text-to-protein generation, whereas
BioMatrix is a generalist model instruction-tuned for the same task. Both generate protein sequences
from functional descriptions and include UniProt-derived training data. Prompteina is trained to
generate all-atom structures from captions that combine structural and functional properties.
We retain ProTrek because the original Pinal and BioMatrix evaluations report this metric. We note
that the use of ProTrek for evaluation is not independent of Pinal’s training. Pinal’s TrEMBL50-
ProTrek corpus was built by scoring TrEMBL cluster representatives against Swiss-Prot descriptions
with ProTrek and retaining about 412M of the highest-scoring sequence–description pairs (Dai et al.,
2025). Pinal’s structure tokens use ProTrek’s Foldseek tokenization, and Pinal selects candidates by
ProTrek score. Prompteina uses neither these data nor these selection procedures.

BINDER DESIGN PROTOCOLS AND EVALUATION
G.1
INTERFACE-RESIDUE STEERING
Interface residue detection.
Following Jones & Thornton (1996), we define interface residues as
binder residues whose solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) decreases by more than 1.0 ˚A2 upon
complex formation. SASA is computed with FreeSASA using default radii. The interface residue
count reported throughout §3.2.1 is the number of binder-side residues meeting this criterion.
Benchmark targets.
We use all 20 targets in the Complexa benchmark (Didi et al., 2026), retain-
ing its target structures, binder-length ranges, and hotspot residues where provided. The agentic
binder protocol lists the complete panel below.
Prompt template.
We specify only the requested binder–target interface residue count: ‘‘The
binding interface involves {n} residues.’’ Requested counts range from 8 to
30; scaffold topology, contact chemistry, and other binder properties remain unspecified.
Generation protocol.
We sample 50 structures per requested count with classifier-free guidance
at w = 2.0. The single-pass protocol omits search, reward models, inverse folding, and structure
prediction so that these components cannot alter the requested-count response. Within a target,
every requested count uses the same 50 binder lengths, sampled uniformly from the target-specific
range with a fixed seed. The target structure and benchmark hotspots also remain fixed, leaving the
requested contact-residue count as the only manipulated variable.
Unconditional baseline.
We sample pretrained Complexa with unchanged target inputs, disabled
text, and the same binder lengths as the text-conditioned samples. Classifier-free guidance is unde-
fined when both text and fold conditioning are absent.

G.2
AGENTIC BINDER DESIGN PROTOCOL
G.2.1
TARGET PANEL
The panel contains 20 protein targets drawn from the AlphaProteo (Zambaldi et al., 2024) and Bind-
Craft (Pacesa et al., 2025) targets aggregated by Didi et al. (2026). We exclude influenza H1 and the
SARS-CoV-2 receptor-binding domain because our prompt-expansion pipeline returned no usable
specifications, and apply this exclusion to every method. IL-17A and TNF-α remain in the panel,
although Complexa excludes them after no evaluated method succeeds within its runtime budget.
Each target contributes a structure and binder-length range; curated hotspots are available for 18
targets. In the agentic setting, each method receives the structure and length range and selects an
epitope for each design. In the non-agentic setting, each method receives those curated hotspots.
G.2.2
GENERATION PROTOCOL
Every method generates 200 designs per target in both the agentic and non-agentic settings. We
divide the target’s binder-length range into 20 contiguous bands and draw 10 designs in each, pre-
venting a method’s length prior from concentrating its samples in part of the range. Designs are
sampled independently without inference-time search, a reward model, or candidate selection. The
common budget and generation procedure isolate differences among conditioning channels.

G.2.3
SEQUENCE CONDITIONS
Each generated backbone is scored under three sequence conditions. The self-sequence condition
uses the sequence produced by the structure generation model. The redesign condition samples eight
ProteinMPNN sequences per backbone and retains the highest-scoring one (Dauparas et al., 2022).
The fixed-interface redesign condition also uses ProteinMPNN but fixes the generated identities of
39


Preprint
binder interface residues, changing only the scaffold sequence. A binder residue belongs to the
interface when any of its heavy atoms lies within 6 ˚A of a target heavy atom.
The gap between the two redesign conditions isolates how much of a method’s score is carried by
ProteinMPNN rewriting the interface rather than by the interface the model generated. Didi et al.
(2026) report the same condition for this reason.
Reporting all three conditions permits comparison among methods that use inverse folding differ-

would omit part of the published method; Complexa and Prompteina require no redesign.
G.2.4
AGENTIC SPECIFICATION GENERATION
We use Claude Opus 4.6 as the agent in a two-stage pipeline. A research stage gives a language
model tool access to web and scientific-literature search, PDB structure search and entry lookup,
and UniProt lookup. The model uses these sources to prepare a structured dossier about target
structure, binding biology, known binders, and biophysical properties (Box G.2). An expansion
stage reads the dossier and emits the design specifications consumed by each structure generation
model (Box G.3).
Each method receives the same four classes of input, with only the method reference differing:
• The research dossier, byte-identical across methods for a given target.
• The task instruction, which states how many designs to produce, the length band, and the re-
quirement that designs be diverse. It is byte-identical across methods and names none of them.
• A method reference, the only input that differs. It describes what that method is, the channels
it exposes, the closed set of values each channel accepts, and the exact emission format. Each is
self-contained and refers to no other method.
• Structural query tools over the target, listed in Table 18, together with the target configuration
(target chain and residue range, binder length range, PDB identifier).

BINDER SCORE CALIBRATION
AlphaProteo AF2 filters used by Complexa.
AlphaProteo fit three AlphaFold2 thresholds to
the binding data of Cao et al. (2022): interaction PAE below 7 ˚A, complex pLDDT above 0.90,
and binder-aligned RMSD below 1.5 ˚A (Zambaldi et al., 2024). The thresholds were selected by
ranking experimental success rates within each target, subject to at least ten designs passing the
filters. Complexa subsequently adopted these AF2 filters as its in-silico success criterion (Didi
et al., 2026). AlphaProteo instead used its AF3 filters for its final benchmark because they performed
better across its targets. These differences motivate recalibrating the evaluation criterion for method
comparison.
Calibration data.
We assemble 5,227 designed binders with experimental bind/no-bind labels
from four public releases: 3,532 designs over 13 targets from Overath et al. (2025), 1,189 over 15
targets from Shanehsazzadeh (2026), 361 over 13 targets from the BoltzGen deposit (Stark et al.,
2026), and 145 over 13 targets from the BindCraft deposit (Pacesa et al., 2025). We define a group
as all designs for one target within one release because releases with the same target name may use
different target constructs and different sets of designed binders. Of 54 such groups, 46 contain both
binders and non-binders and can be used to fit and evaluate a threshold; these groups contain 5,045
designs. We therefore average results equally across these 46 groups rather than pooling all designs.
Prediction and calibration protocol.
We refold every design once with ESMFold2-Fast and
ESMFold2 (Candido et al., 2026), AlphaFold2 (Jumper et al., 2021), and AlphaFold3 (Abramson
et al., 2024). AlphaFold2 uses initial guess, the standard binder-scoring configuration that initializes
the structure module from the designed coordinates. We recompute the published AlphaProteo AF2
filter terms from the saved structures and predicted aligned error matrices under all four models.
For 394 designs in the Shanehsazzadeh (2026) release, the released sequence and deposited design
structure contain different sets of target residues, so binder-aligned RMSD cannot be computed for
ESMFold2-Fast, ESMFold2, or AlphaFold3. AlphaFold2 starts from the deposited design structure
and therefore retains the residue correspondence needed for RMSD. We count the 394 designs as not
passing filters that require RMSD rather than removing them. For thresholded criteria, we repeat-
edly fit the threshold on 45 groups and evaluate it on the remaining group. The median fitted iPAE

ipSAEmin evaluation.
ipSAEmin restricts a TM-score-like transform of predicted aligned error to
residue pairs across the interface and scales its distance parameter by interface size (Shanehsazzadeh,
2026). The min variant takes the smaller of the asymmetric binder→target and target→binder scores.
Its macro average precision exceeds that of iPAE under every prediction model (Table 20). Adding
binder-aligned RMSD lowers macro-F1 under three predictors (0.519 to 0.469 for ESMFold2-Fast)
and raises it slightly only for AlphaFold2 (0.441 to 0.448), and thus provides no consistent benefit.
ESMFold2-Fast and AlphaFold3 give macro average precision values of 0.587 and 0.566, with me-
dian runtimes of 1.61 and 9.07 seconds per design, respectively, when both process the same 3,532
Overath designs; the benchmark therefore uses ESMFold2-Fast. We report continuous ipSAEmin
directly because the best cutoff depends on the data used to fit it. Within each target, we average
the k highest-scoring designs to measure the part of the distribution that a design campaign would
order. We then average the 20 target-level values so that each target receives equal weight. Every
method contributes 200 designs on each of the same 20 benchmark targets in every comparison.

Limitations.
The calibration is not fully independent of the criteria it evaluates. Of the 3,532 de-
signs in the Overath release (Overath et al., 2025), 2,585 originate from Cao et al. (2022), whose data
AlphaProteo used to set its AF2 filters. The designs tested by Shanehsazzadeh (2026) were them-
selves selected using ipSAEmin, so that release cannot independently establish a general cutoff for
the same score. For method comparisons, we therefore use continuous ipSAEmin and the threshold-
free expected top-k statistic rather than classifying designs with a fitted cutoff. The calibration also
covers only single-chain targets, whereas three benchmark targets contain multiple chains. We use
the same rule to merge target chains throughout, but have not measured how this choice affects
multichain targets. Finally, the negative examples are campaign designs selected for experimental

H.1

Each iterative loop uses the same 200-design budget as the one-shot benchmark, divided into four
rounds of 50 (§3.2.2). The first round follows the one-shot agentic protocol, so iterative and inde-
pendent generation differ only after the first 50 designs are scored (App. G.2). Generation, redesign,
and scoring otherwise remain identical.
Each 50-design round divides the target’s binder-length range into 10 bands and draws five designs
per band. We recompute these bands from the full range instead of merging the 20-band grid used
for 200 designs, reproducing the length distribution of an independent 50-design run.

I
GM2A EXPERIMENTAL VALIDATION
I.1
CANDIDATE SELECTION
We generated 1,000 backbone designs in five parallel iterative-optimization threads, each contribut-
ing 200 designs over four rounds (§3.2.3). Four threads targeted distinct GM2A epitopes; the fifth
also required β-rich binder topologies. Each backbone yielded 33 sequence variants: the generated
sequence and eight samples from each of ProteinMPNN, ProteinMPNN-FI, SolubleMPNN, and
SolubleMPNN-FI. We screened every variant with one seed of ESMFold2-Fast and retained the top
50 backbones from each thread, yielding 250 backbones for deeper evaluation. For deeper evalua-
tion, we folded each of their 8,250 sequences with two ESMFold2-Fast seeds and two AlphaFold3
seeds.
Sequence and interface filters.
We rejected sequences containing a homopolymer run of at least
four residues, a hydrophobic run of at least 12 residues, four consecutive Lys/Arg residues, or a
PP pair within the first 20 residues. Additional vetoes covered the PURE-expression stall motifs
PPP, DPP, PPN, PPG, APP, GPP, PPD, PPE, and PPW (Peil et al., 2013). A backbone remained
eligible when at least one of its sequence variants passed these filters. Beyond sequence-level quality,
the calibration data revealed a second failure mode: very small predicted interfaces. Designs in
the lowest decile of interface buried surface area performed worse in the calibration data, whereas
interface size did not distinguish designs above this cutoff. We therefore removed sequences below
the within-target 10th percentile of ESMFold2-Fast interface buried surface area, which was 574 ˚A2
for GM2A. The sequence and interface filters retained 6,031 sequences from 249 backbones.
Metric development for experimental selection.
Metrics based on absolute model confidence
can rank complexes highly even when none of the designs bind. We therefore required the ranking
metric to enrich binders within targets that yield binders and to avoid assigning high scores to de-
signs from targets where every tested design failed. The development panel combined data from the
threshold-calibration panel in Appendix G.4 with 2,221 additional designs from Adaptyv Protein-
Base. We define a group as all designs for one target within one release because releases may use
different target constructs and design sets. The development panel contained 6,402 designs in 64
groups, including 1,057 binders and seven groups with no binders.
We evaluated established metrics and developed 16 new candidates on this panel. To limit selection
bias during this search, we stated a physical hypothesis and a negative control before evaluating each
candidate, then tested candidates individually without fitting combinations of existing features.
