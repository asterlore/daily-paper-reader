ABSTRACT 

Antibody therapeutics have transformed treatment across diverse therapeutic areas and have become central to modern medicine. While recent machine learning approaches aim to accelerate antibody discovery through structure-based design and target-affinity optimization, successful candidates must also satisfy broad developability constraints, including structural compatibility, low immunogenicity, and favorable biophysical properties, while remaining sufficiently diverse to provide sufficient coverage of design space to mitigate later-stage development failures. We introduce a preference-conditioned multi-objective generative flow network (GFN) fine-tuning pipeline for fixed-framework CDRH3 loop redesign that fine-tunes a biological sequence model against many inexpensive proxies covering backbone compatibility, sequence likelihood, immunogenicity risk, novelty, and developability liabilities. It combines up to fourteen predictive rewards in a single policy trained to sample CDRH3 loops across 623 SAbDab antibody structures, leveraging a reward reduction strategy to preserve Pareto dominance under this learned map. We also introduce a novel autoregressive span-in-filling variant of AMPLIFY-350M that serves both as a scaffold-conditioned CDRH3 generation prior and as a direct conditional likelihood scorer. We evaluated reward scores, diversity, and design quality on loops sampled on 45 held-out antibody complexes to assess structural consistency, confidence, aggregation propensity, stability, and antigen binding. Compared with the inverse-folding methods ProteinMPNN and ESM-IF, our approach produces roughly 10 × more distinct acceptable candidates per design at 50% sequence identity, while holding 10% of the 14-objective Pareto front pooled across methods, trading per-sample quality for greater exploration of novel possible structures. The results suggest that by relying on a more diverse data distribution and reward distribution, we are able to generate a wider array of promising candidates. All code and data will be made publicly available upon publication. 

1 INTRODUCTION 

Antibody therapeutics are a major drug modality across therapeutic areas, with more than 200 antibodies and antibody-like biologics now approved (Chan et al., 2025). Current antibody discovery still relies heavily on experimental screening of large libraries, followed by extensive engineering and optimization (Laustsen et al., 2021). Yet such libraries sample only a small fraction of the vast antibody sequence space; a typical 13 residue loop alone allows 20 13 ≈ 8.2 × 10 16 possible sequences. Computational and machine learning-driven approaches increasingly aim to direct this search in high dimensional space (Laustsen et al., 2021; Bennett et al., 2026; Shanehsazzadeh et al., 2024; Nabla Bio & Biswas, 2025; Agarwal et al., 2025), focusing primarily on affinity, structural plausibility, or sequence likelihood, with developability usually addressed later in development. However, binding rank-based methods can perform at parity with or worse than random mutational screens (Erasmus et al., 2026), motivating a broader exploration of sequence design space. Moreover, 1arXiv:2610.16920v1 [cs.CE] 12 Oct 2026 Under review antibody design is inherently multi-objective: candidates must balance structural compatibility with low immunogenicity and favorable biophysical and developability properties (Khetan et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023b; Raybould et al., 2019). These objectives can conflict (Adams et al., 2001) and maintaining diversity provides alternatives when otherwise promising candidates fail later in development. This is particularly important, as validation studies repeatedly show that once in-silico structural or binding scores exceed an acceptable quality threshold, further score improvements do not necessarily translate into a better candidate ranking (Chu et al., 2026; Cotet et al., 2025), motivating the consideration of a broader suite of design criteria when selecting candidates to progress to the lab for further therapeutic development (Overath et al., 2025). This paper approaches fixed-framework redesign of antibody heavy-chain complementarity-determining region 3 (CDRH3) loops as a sequence generation problem in the context of a fixed structural scaffold. CDRH3 is a major determinant of antigen binding and specificity due to its central position in the binding interface and high sequence diversity (Xu & Davis, 2000; McConnell & Casadevall, 2025) 1, Each design replaces CDRH3 within the fixed framework of a known antibody-antigen crystal complex, avoiding the need for antibody-antigen pose search required in many de novo 

design settings. We adopt Multi Objective Generative Flow Networks (MOGFNs; Jain et al. (2023)) to train a (multi-) preference conditioned CDRH3 sequence sampler across 623 experimentally-derived antibody-antigen complexes, enabling the sampling of diverse, high-reward designs representing different trade-offs along the Pareto front. We use this approach to fine-tune a new span-in-filling variant of AMPLIFY-350M (Fournier et al., 2024), conditioned on both scaffold and preference. Our oracle-agnostic framework supports additional or alternative oracles without changing the generator architecture, and we demonstrate this flexibility by training with up to M = 14 predictive rewards. To avoid the combinatorial training and deployment burden of sampling preferences across a growing 

M -simplex, we learn a dominance-preserving, strictly positive, row-stochastic projection from the 

M normalized rewards onto D ≪ M preference slots (Park & Sung, 2025). We evaluate reward performance, diversity, and structural quality on 45 held-out antibody complexes. In sum, we contribute: 1. A preference-conditioned MOGFN fine-tuning pipeline for fixed-framework CDRH3 re-design, optionally incorporating a learned preference projection to support many rewards. 2. A modular suite of fourteen antibody-design oracles covering inverse-folding backbone compatibility, general and antibody-specific sequence likelihood, immunogenicity risk, novelty, and developability liabilities. 3. An autoregressive span-in-filling version of AMPLIFY-350M, serving as a fast and memory-efficient scaffold-conditioned CDRH3 generation prior. 4. A structural quality-diversity evaluation pipeline designed to produce a Chai-1 (Chai Dis-covery, 2024) and Rosetta (Alford et al., 2017) ranked shortlist of promising CDRH3 loop candidates using estimator scores weakly correlated with the training rewards. 

2 RELATED WORK 

Antibody and binder design. Computational antibody design spans structure-based de novo design (Bennett et al., 2026; Stark et al., 2025), antigen-specific generators (Shanehsazzadeh et al., 2024; Nabla Bio & Biswas, 2025; Levine et al., 2026; Mille-Fragoso et al., 2026; Wasdin et al., 2025), affinity prediction and optimization (Agarwal et al., 2025), sequence infilling (Shuai et al., 2023), and antibody-specific foundation and inverse-folding models (Guloglu et al., 2025; Dreyer et al., 2023). These systems typically optimize binding, sequence likelihood, or structure recovery and often rely on structural information, which covers only a small fraction of antibody sequence space: SAbDab contains ≈ 10 4 antibody–antigen structures (Schneider et al., 2022), compared with > 10 9 observed antibody sequences in OAS (Olsen et al., 2022). In contrast, we train a single CDRH3 generator across many scaffolds using structural priors, sequence likelihood, developability, immunogenicity, and novelty as rewards. The resulting model generalizes to new scaffolds at test time, unlike antigen-specific generators that treat each target as a separate design problem (Shanehsazzadeh et al., 2024;

These approaches use at most a handful (2-5) and largely emphasize binding energy, whose predictive accuracy remains limited for mutated antibody-antigen interfaces (Sirin et al., 2016), and out-of-domain antigens (Hummer et al., 2025). We instead train a single multi-scaffold (623 bound SAbDab complexes), multi-task sampler to explore the sequence space of promising CDRH3 variants over a broader suite of up to fourteen objectives. 

GFlowNets. Generative Flow Networks learn policies whose terminal states are sampled propor-tional to a positive reward (Bengio et al., 2021), preserving diversity among high-reward candidates. Multi-objective GFNs (Jain et al., 2023) and HN-GFN (Zhu et al., 2023) extend this idea to Pareto-optimal generation under competing objectives. Yin et al. (2025) leveraged a GFN to sample high likelihood sequences under a product of experts (ProGen2 fromNijkamp et al. (2023), and a Potts model, respectively providing global protein sequence distribution and local antibody-domain dis-tributions) to generate diverse CDR candidates. Instead, we condition on preferences over up to fourteen rewards to take advantage of the same diversity-seeking behaviour of GFNs. 

Objective reduction. Multi-objective optimization often relies on objective reduction to accom-modate many rewards, via methods such as PCA (Deb & Saxena, 2006) or dominance-preserving reduction with an explicit δ-error (Brockhoff & Zitzler, 2006; 2009), motivated by the observation that Pareto dominance degenerates as the objective count grows. Recent work in multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL; Park & Sung (2025)) showed that learning to sample from the Pareto front of an affine, all-positive, row-stochastic reward reduction provably produces a policy that samples from regions of the non-reduced objective set Pareto front. We adopt this method and transfer it from value-based MORL to a preference-conditioned MOGFN which targets the whole reward distribution, and reward compression therefore trades against both diversity and optimality. 

Structural filtering of designs. Forward-folding self-consistency and co-folding are standard practices for validating designed sequences; we rely on both an antibody-specific structure predictor ABodyBuilder3 (Kenlay et al., 2024) and the general structure prediction model Chai-1 (Chai Discovery, 2024), which does not require multiple sequence alignment. Fold confidence and empirical, energy-based binding estimators like FoldX (Delgado et al., 2025) and Rosetta (Alford et al., 2017) are useful as binary gates for selecting promising binders for experimental validation, although their ranking performance remains limited (Pacesa et al., 2025; Bennett et al., 2023; Sirin et al., 2016; Hummer et al., 2025). 

3 METHODS 

3.1 TASK OVERVIEW 

We denote a candidate CDRH3 amino acid sequence by x = ( x1, . . . , x L), where L is the CDRH3 loop length. For a fixed antibody scaffold s, we redesign only the CDRH3 loop while fixing the surrounding framework context. Each design task is a multi-objective optimization problem which seeks feasible candidates x⋆ ∈ X that jointly maximize M objectives R(x) = [ R1(x), . . . , R M (x)] 

i.e., max x∈X R(x). When objectives conflict, no single candidate simultaneously maximizes all objectives, so we seek Pareto-optimal candidates where improving one objective dimension would compromise another one. Our method introduces four components: 1) a modular reward suite for antibody design (Section 3.2), 2) a learned reward projection onto a fixed-size preference simplex (Section 3.2), 3) a scaffold-conditioned span-in-filling policy that proposes CDRH3 sequences in framework context (Section 3.3), and 4) a preference-conditioned GFN objective that samples diverse high-reward sequences under different tradeoffs (Section 3.4). We optimize up to M = 14 independent reward terms in six categories: structural stability, immuno-genicity, developability, liability, likelihood, and novelty (see Table 11). All variance in the rewards is driven by changes to the CDRH3 loop, regardless of whether the oracles required additional framework-specific context. Structural stability guides the sampler to produce loops with high likelihood under the common inverse-folding models ESM-IF, Boltz-IF, and ProteinMPNN (Hsu et al., 2022; Dauparas et al., 2022; Stark et al., 2025). Immunogenicty is measured by an MHC-II binding predictor built on a frozen ESM-2 8M backbone (Lin et al., 2023) to detect antibody designs that could trigger immune responses. Developability captures whether an antibody can be practically manufactured, and is measured using a fine-tuned ESM-2 650M sequence classifier (Lin et al., 2023) on SAbDab-derived binary labels (Chen et al., 2020). Sequence liability (chemical and post-translational) was measured using a motif scan adapted from the Liability Antibody Profiler (Satława et al., 2024). Sequence likelihood was estimated under our AMPLIFY-AR model, a CDRH3-specific masked language model h3berta (Rodella & Lemmin, 2026), and ablang2 (Olsen et al., 2024), which scores the loop inside its target’s paired variable domain and is less germline-biased. Novelty 

was incentivized using the minimum normalized Levenshtein distance and BLOSUM62 (Henikoff & Henikoff, 1992) substitution score to the training CDRH3 set, minimized over training loops of the same length. All rewards were normalized using an online min-max normalizer to facilitate multiobjective training. See appendix B.4 for details for more details.

We trained an autoregressive span-in-filling variant of AMPLIFY-350M (Fournier et al., 2024) from scratch on the original UniRef100 (Suzek et al., 2015), OAS, and SCOP (Chandonia et al., 2022) data (1,050,000 steps; 1.77 × 10 11 in-filling tokens; appendix A.1) to initialize πθ . Sequences were rearranged in FIM format: 

<bos> prefix <mask> suffix <sep> infill <eos> ,

and the model was trained with causal cross-entropy on the missing span. Span lengths were sampled uniformly from 1–50 residues, covering the 5–24-residue CDRH3 range. This enables teacher-forced log-probability calculation in one forward pass rather than the L passes required for masked-model pseudo-likelihood, yielding a 10.7 × throughput improvement on held-out CDRH3 loops (see appendix A.1). Conditioning used sequential task and preference cross-attention after self-attention and before each conditioned layer’s feed-forward network. Task conditioning used unpooled per-residue native heavy-chain representations from the frozen backbone, computed once per target. Preference weights wd were thermometer-encoded, combined with learned slot embeddings, and projected to the AMPLIFY hidden dimension. This ordering allows the same preference trade-off to act differently across target-conditioned hidden states. Each cross-attention output projection was initialized to zero. During sampling, the model predicts each CDRH3 residue conditioned on the framework prefix and suffix, previously generated CDRH3 residues, and scaffold/preference conditioning using two sequential cross-attention modules per conditioned layer. The resulting trajectory log-probability is: log PF (τ | w) = PLt=1 log πθ (xt | x<t , xframe , w). We train a preference-conditioned MOGFN (Jain et al., 2023) to sample CDRH3 sequences along the Pareto frontier of the design objectives. For a fixed preference vector w, the GFN target distribution is proportional to the scalarized reward, R(x; w)/Z (w), where Z(w) is the preference and context-dependent normalizing partition function. The FIM policy above defines the forward transition probability PF : we use πθ when emphasizing the per-step action distribution and PF when referring to trajectory-level probabilities. We train with the trajectory balance (TB) objective (Malkin et al., 2022). Let PF (τ ) and PB (τ )

denote the forward and backward probabilities of a complete trajectory τ that generates sequence 

x. TB requires log Z(w) + log PF (τ | w) = log R(x; w) + log PB (τ ), but as each autoregressive trajectory has a unique backward decomposition, PB (τ ) = 1 and can be eliminated. Rather than learning a separate log Z(w) for each conditioning vector, we use a VarGrad-style centered-residual estimator (Richter et al., 2020; Malkin et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023a). Post-Filter Cascade. We calculate scores on the distribution of designs from each sampler with oracles of interest to the drug development pipeline, including liability, developability, structural plau-sibility, and structural compatibility. Each stage of the funnel is increasingly more computationally expensive to run, and is performed on a smaller subset of the previous stage’s designs. Briefly the sampled pool undergoes a Stage 0 sequence liability motif scan, followed by a Stage 1 forward fold under ABodyBuilder3 (collecting the CDRH3 pLDDT confidence scores and scRMSD structural fidelity scores), a Stage 2 measure of self-aggregation propensity, a Stage 3 forward fold under Chai-1 (pLDDT & RMSD), followed by a Stage 4 estimation of relative binding and stability energies as measured by FoldX and Rosetta. Stage 3’s budget is 1000 per arm due to computational constraints, and Stage 4’s is 250 (budgets referred to as B3 and B4 in text). See appendix D for all details of the evaluation. 

Dataset. Antibody-antigen complexes were curated from the Structural Antibody Database (SAbDab; Dunbar et al. (2014)) and split by heavy-chain scaffold to evaluate generalization of CDRH3 to novel antibody frameworks, yielding 623 training, 10 validation, and 55 held-out test targets. Antigen chains were used only to confirm that held-out test targets represent distinct binding problems. Each arm was evaluated at a matched sampling budget (32,768 designs per arm per target for the ranked pipeline, 100 designs per arm for the uniform pipeline). We compare three GFlowNet arms (a pre-defined 9/14-reward mapping and two learned objective-slot models with D = 4 and 

D = 5 ), the AMPLIFY-AR policy provided the loop design performance of the prior. We also evaluated a uniform random policy, and compared against two structural inverse-folding baselines: ProteinMPNN & ESM-IF. Full details are provided in appendix B. 

Evaluation. We evaluate all arms both for their ability to produce high quality antibodies on the test scaffolds (as defined by both the reward set and the held out postfilter pipeline) conditions and sample diversity. On the held out scaffolds, we report oracle scores, postfilter score distribtions, distinct clusters among the acceptable designs (50% loop identity), pareto front enrichment by the postfilter pipeline, and sequence diversity in both sequence and embedding space. 

4 RESULTS 

4.1 LEARNED PROJECTION 

An analysis of the reward landscape can be found in Section F.1. Briefly, our learned projections recover expected groupings in the data. Fitted on the native-PDB reward matrix at D = 4 , the slots resolve to an inverse-folding stability cluster, a novelty cluster, the prior likelihood clusters with the Antibody ProteinMPNN and BoltzIF inverse fold logits, and developability & immunogenicity comprise the final axis. These groupings were not found to vary with other training hyperparameters. See Appendix G.1 for more details. We also report results for a GFN trained on 9 objectives with a fixed reduction mapping: all stability oracles collapsed into one, developability, AMPLIFY-AR likelihood, and immunogenicity. 4.2 QUALITY -D IVERSITY : M ORE DISTINCT ACCEPTABLE CANDIDATES PER DESIGN 

An analysis of pool-wise rewards across all arms (Appendix B.4) reveals that the GFNs sample designs far from the AMPLIFY-AR prior, and score higher on novelty, immunogeniciy, and developability than any trained model. The liability filter removes ≈ 30% of sampled designs from the D = 4 and 

D = 5 GFNs, as well as the inverse folding baselines, compared to ≈ 50% for the random baseline, AMPLIFY-AR prior, and Fixed GFN (which did not train on the liability reward). 5 LIMITATIONS & C ONCLUSIONS 

All experiments are computational and rely on learned or sequence and structure-derived oracles which may not reflect experimental outcomes. Generated candidates therefore require experimental validation before their potential therapeutic relevance can be assessed. Our sampler is also restricted to a fixed CDRH3 length per scaffold to support the inverse-folding oracles, substantially reducing the accessible sequence space. Future work could enable variable-length generation using alternative structural priors and potentially more accurate, albeit considerably more expensive, oracle evaluations. Our method frames fixed-framework CDRH3 redesign as preference-conditioned multi-objective generation followed by structural filtering. By fine-tuning a span-in-filling AMPLIFY backbone against fourteen modular oracles, we generate substantially more distinct acceptable candidates than inverse-folding baselines and explore regions of protein design space not recovered by structure-based approaches, at the cost of lower average per-sample quality. The practical value of this quality-diversity trade-off requires experimental validation. However, given that many functional proteins exist outside the sequence space represented in the UniRef90 and MGnify protein sequence databases (Diplock et al., 2026), sequence-based generators may provide a complementary approach for exploring novel functional protein sequence space. Our framework can incorporate additional objectives, subject to oracle and computational cost, while the projection mechanism prevents the cost of covering preference space from growing with the number of objectives. 9Under review 

6 ETHICS STATEMENT 

This work uses public protein sequence and structure data and does not involve human subjects, private personal data, or clinical deployment. The remaining risk is dual-use: generated binders should not be synthesized, tested, or deployed without appropriate biosafety review, target-risk assessment, experimental validation, and regulatory oversight. Our models inherit biases from SAbDab, UniRef100, and the learned reward models, including biases toward well-studied antibody families, antigens, and assay conditions. This work should not be considered safe to use in any autonomous discovery program. 

7 REPRODUCIBILITY STATEMENT 

We will release all code, including dataset curation scripts, model training code, oracle training code, and the postfilter pipeline scripts, as a open-source monorepo upon publication. We will additionally release all oracles, the FIM AMPLIFY-AR model, and fine-tuned GFNs as open source checkpoints. The release will also include the SAbDab train and held-out splits, oracle preprocessing code, and method for generating all cached training artifacts (e.g., inverse-folding logits) in a reproducible manner. We will additionally release training configurations, random seeds, evaluation scripts, and figure-generation scripts. These artifacts are intended to allow the community to inspect the work, improve the framework within the domain of antibodies, as well as adapt it to other biological applications. 

8 AI A SSISTANCE DISCLOSURE 

Large language models (LLMs) were used to assist with manuscript drafting (primarily consistency checks, accuracy of claims validation, and LaTeX automation), and for code generation for all model evaluations. Many elements of this codebase predate the advent of human-level coding agents, so most core methods and oracles were implemented entirely by hand, with some assistance on individual methodological improvements. LLMs were used under heavy supervision for figure and table generation. They were not used for research ideation, method design, or experiment design. (2025) show that the regression loss g applied to trajectory-balance residuals determines an f -divergence between the target and the learned distribution;Fawkes & Hartford (2026) extend the correspondence to translation-invariant losses and off-policy data. They classify losses by two properties of that divergence: zero-forcing ( f (0) = ∞, which penalises putting mass outside the target distribution, i.e., favouring exploitation) and zero-avoiding ( f ′(∞) = ∞, which penalises missing mass, favouring exploration). The squared loss g(t) = 12 t2 is zero-forcing and not zero-avoiding. The three losses they propose all grow faster than quadratic as t → +∞, but only Shifted-Cosh does so in both tails: Linex(1) and Linex(1/2) are linear as t → −∞ , which is why neither is zero-forcing. Log-cosh sits at the opposite end from the squared loss, so it is neither zero-forcing nor zero-avoiding, and does not contain the squared loss exploitation pressure. Our annealing of c(t) moves the loss back towards quadratic over the course of training, growing progressively biased toward exploitation, in an attempt to reduce the magnitude of tail residuals without discarding them; Chen et al. (2026) anneal the policy mixing weight instead. The gradient is tanh( βloss δ) — 0.81 , 0.55 and 0.38 times the squared-error gradient at 1σδ , 2σδ and 3σδ — so the penalty acts as a smooth per-sample gradient clip that relaxes as c(t) decreases. B.1 SA BDAB CURATION 

The SAbDab (Dunbar et al., 2014) dataset comprises paired heavy/light chain sequences with antigen co-crystal structures, associated metadata (resolution, species, affinity where available), and PDB 22 Under review coordinate files. We removed entries missing heavy-chain, light-chain, antigen-chain, or antigen-type annotations, excluded multi-chain antigens, and retained only protein and peptide antigens. Entries were deduplicated by PDB code and heavy-chain identifier. Heavy-chain, light-chain, and antigen sequences were parsed from PDB ATOM records using Biopython (Cock et al., 2009), with non-standard residues mapped to canonical one-letter amino acid codes where possible. CDRH3 boundaries were identified using ANARCI (Dunbar & Deane, 2016) under the IMGT numbering scheme (Lefranc et al., 2003) (positions 105–117), and gap characters at insertion positions were removed. Structures with CDRH3 length < 5 were excluded because their sequence space is sufficiently small to enumerate exhaustively. Structures resolved better than 2.3 Å were retained, leaving 873. The retained set spans 1.2–2.3 Å (median 2.0) over 469 protein and 315 peptide antigens. We additionally remove 72 samples due to parser disagreements between the structures and inverse-folding models: 17 entries are excluded by a maintained list, and the rest fail the per-structure consistency check of the loop logits. Train, validation, and test sets were defined based on the heavy-chain scaffold to evaluate whether the loop-design policy generalizes to novel antibody frameworks rather than memorizing CDRH3 sequences associated with nearly identical scaffolds. Because antibody frameworks are germline-conserved (median scaffold sequence identity ≈ 0.85 across unrelated antibodies in our dataset), we used a near-identity threshold of ≥ 0.95 to identify potential leakage. This removes exact and closely related affinity-matured scaffold duplicates across splits while retaining members of the same germline family as distinct design problems. Antigen information was not used to define the primary split, but was used to verify that held-out targets represented distinct antigen-binding problems. After curation, filtering, and leakage control, the final dataset comprised 623 training, 10 validation, and 55 held-out test targets from an initial universe of 784 structures, with 161 structures excluded during processing.

B.5 DIVERSITY METRICS 

Pool diversity is measured over ESM-2 650M sequence embeddings, with the Vendi score (Friedman & Dieng, 2023) (the effective number of distinct modes; the exponential of the Shannon entropy of the eigenvalues of K/n , where Kij is the embedding similarity between designs i and j and n is the pool size, which is scale-free and mode-count-aware) and mean pairwise L2 distance. We do not report 2D UMAP convex-hull area as a diversity metric due to volumes in this space being a projection artifact. UMAP is used for visualization only. Vendi counts effective modes and therefore grows with sample size, so every arm is subsampled to the same n before scoring. Embeddings are calculated with the loop in context of it’s heavy chain. The GFN methods are close to the untrained control on both diversity measures while adding objective controllability: A Vendi score at one sample size cannot separate many modes from a dense sample of few modes, because it is a single number at a single n. We therefore also resample each arm’s pool at increasing draw sizes and report how the effective count grows. The same populations can also be shown in the space itself rather than as a count, which says where 

in the landscape an arm still has designs once the cascade has run.

The MHC-II binding oracle predicts peptide–allele binding affinity to estimate immunogenicity risk for candidate CDRH3 sequences. The model scores each candidate against a panel of 27 reference human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class II alleles covering the major HLA-DP, HLA-DQ, and HLA-DR supertypes (Greenbaum et al., 2011). The model uses ESM-2 (8M parameters, 6 layers, 320-dimensional hidden states) (Lin et al., 2023) as a frozen backbone encoder. Peptide and MHC-II pseudo-sequences are encoded independently through the backbone and mean-pooled across sequence positions (ignoring padding). The two 320-dimensional representations are concatenated into a 640-dimensional vector and passed through an MLP with two hidden layers ( 640 → 512 → 512 → 1), GELU activations, dropout ( p = 0 .25 ), and a sigmoid output activation. The backbone is frozen throughout training. Only the MLP head (591K parameters) is optimized. Total model size including the frozen backbone is 8.4M parameters. We train on binding affinity data from NetMHCIIpan 4.0 (Reynisson et al., 2020), which provides log 50 k-transformed binding affinities ( y = 1 − log 50 k(IC 50 nM ), y ∈ [0 , 1] ) for peptide–allele pairs across 79 HLA class II alleles. We perform 5-fold cross-validation using the provided BA1–BA5 partitions ( ∼87 K training samples per fold). The model is trained for 40,000 steps with batch size 128 using AdamW (lr = 10 −3, no weight decay) and mean squared error (MSE) loss. A linear warmup schedule (500 steps) followed by linear decay is applied. At inference time, the five fold-specific heads share the frozen backbone and their output probabilities are averaged. Each candidate CDRH3 is scored against all 27 reference alleles in parallel. Higher rewards correspond to lower predicted immunogenicity. Table 12 reports 5-fold cross-validation performance of the ensemble-averaged model evaluated on each fold’s held-out test set. Binder classification uses the standard 500 nM threshold ( y > 0.426 ). We use a SAbDab-derived dataset (Chen et al., 2020) that assigns binary developability labels to 2,409 antibodies based on the Developability Index (Lauer et al., 2012). The original definition of the index combines the spatial aggregation propensity of the CDRs with net charge computed on the full-length antibody structure. Chen et al. (2020) computes it from variable-region structures alone, so the net-charge term in these labels is a variable-region quantity. Antibodies in the bottom 20% of Developability Index scores, corresponding to the lowest aggregation risk, are labeled developable. This yields an imbalanced dataset with approximately 482 positives and 1,927 negatives. The developability classifier fine-tunes ESM-2 650M (Lin et al., 2023) with a BOS-token-pooled sequence classification head and 2-class output. The heavy-chain sequence is provided as input. Only the head and the layer-normalization parameters are fine-tuned, yielding an efficient adaptation of the pretrained backbone. Training uses 10-fold stratified cross-validation for 15 epochs with the Adam optimizer (Kingma & Ba, 2015) (learning rate 5 × 10 −4), batch size 8 via gradient accumulation, and early stopping with patience 10. Note that this oracle is a sequence classifier of ease of synthesis and contains no spatial-aggregation or charge-patch descriptors. It is nontheless correlated with the the structural aggregation signal supplied in the postfilter analysis. 37 Under review F.4 NATURALNESS AND ANTIBODY -R EPERTOIRE ORACLES

The motif catalog is adapted from the Liability Antibody Profiler (LAP) (Satława et al., 2024), which catalogs 70 motifs and retains 30 that either recur across sources or carry strong evidence. Hard-flagged (gate-rejecting in the post-filter, maximally weighted in the reward): deamidation NG ,isomerization DG , N-linked glycosylation sequon N-x-[S/T] , and free or unpaired cysteine, which LAP likewise treats as severe. The cysteine rule is a deliberately conservative sequence-only heuristic — every cysteine in the designed loop is hard-flagged, since pairing cannot be determined from sequence. Soft-flagged (recorded and weighted, not gating): slow deamidation and isomerization variants, methionine and tryptophan oxidation, and DP fragmentation. The scanner emits per-motif occurrence counts, so the same code serves as a training reward, a post-filter gate, and an evaluation statistic. LAP is a screening tool which consumes a paired variable domain, folds it with ABodyBuilder2, and returns a flag list. Our sequence-based scan is unable to identify solvent-based scans as it flags buried liability counts identically to exposed ones. Second, we do not include the "germline" or "therapeutic" exclusions to prevent our sampler from being biased towards those distributions. Third, we only evaluate the designed region and treat this score as a binary accept/reject criteria. F.6 NOVELTY ORACLES 

Both novelty oracles precompute their reference set once at task setup from the resolved training split , never from the full universe — assembling the reference by hand is only correct when training on all targets, and is silently wrong for any subset. The reference is bucketed by loop length, both for speed (distance is at least |∆L|, so most buckets prune immediately) and, for the BLOSUM variant, because distance is minimized over training loops of the same length only. CDRH3 length is a primary determinant of loop conformation, so comparing within a length class avoids introducing gap-open and gap-extend penalties as two free parameters that would then drive most of the length-dependent behaviour. The training set spans every length from 5 to 24 with no gaps. Note that both novelty rewards are constant on the native reference set by construction (a native loop scored against a reference containing itself is exactly zero), so they must be scored leave-one-out when building the offline reference matrix for the projection warm start, or they vanish from the spectrum entirely. This variant is applicable to low-resolution or coarse-grained structures where full backbone coordinates are unavailable. In general, rewards did not correlate with sequence length, with some exceptions at extremely short loop lengths. See Figure 21 for a per-reward breakdown. Length dependence nonetheless dominates several pipeline quantities — Stage-0 liability survival spans 19–61% across targets and tracks CDRH3 length at r = −0.97 — which is why every comparison in this work is paired per target.
