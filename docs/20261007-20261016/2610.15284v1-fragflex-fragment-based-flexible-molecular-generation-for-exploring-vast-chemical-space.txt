## Abstract 

Drug discovery requires efficient exploration of an astronomically large chemical space. Molecular graph diffusion models offer a promising approach to this task, but many require graph size to be fixed before generation. Although atom-level insertion and deletion can enable flexible-size generation, jointly modeling molecular size, atom types, and bonds while maintaining chemical validity remains challenging. We introduce FragFlex, a flexible-length discrete diffusion model that performs node insertion and deletion at the fragment level. During denoising, FragFlex jointly learns the size, fragment identities, and inter-fragment connectivity of the fragment graph. A neural attachment-site predictor and valence-aware assembler then convert the generated fragment graph into an atom-level molecule. In large-scale experiments generating 10 million molecules, FragFlex achieves near-perfect chemical validity while maintaining high uniqueness, demonstrating a strong balance between the two. 

## 1 Introduction 

Drug discovery requires exploring an astronomically large chemical space. Estimates of the size of drug-like chemical space vary substantially with the underlying molecular constraints and enumeration strategies, ranging from approximately 10 33 molecules [25] to 10 60 molecules [4]. Efficiently searching this space is therefore key to reducing the computational and experimental costs of finding promising drug candidates [27, 32]. De novo molecular generation offers a promising strategy for exploring chemical space by directly proposing novel molecular structures. Deep generative models can learn molecular distributions from existing compound datasets and generate new molecules. Notably, denoising diffusion models have driven substantial progress across image generation [9], discrete text generation [1, 21], and molecular graph generation [33]. However, many existing molecular graph diffusion models keep the number of nodes fixed throughout denoising, requiring molecular size to be determined before generation begins. Attempts to overcome fixed-length generation are not unique to molecular graphs: masked diffusion models (MDMs) [26] have been extended to support flexible-length text generation through mask-token insertion [15]. For molecular graphs, Ninniri et al. proposed GrIDDD [23], allowing atom nodes to be inserted and deleted during denoising. Despite this flexibility, maintaining high chemical validity remains challenging when molecular graph size is allowed to vary through atom-level insertion and deletion. This difficulty arises because the model must jointly learn molecular size, atom types, and bond assignments while satisfying strict chemical constraints, such as atomic valence. Fragment-based molecular generation offers a natural way to mitigate this challenge. Instead of constructing every ring and functional group atom by atom, these methods assemble reusable substructures, reducing invalid intermediates [10, 11]. Fragment-level diffusion and flow models further improve efficiency and scalability by denoising a coarser graph [18]. In particular, Levy

and Rector-Brooks proposed FragDiffusion [19], extending discrete graph diffusion from atoms to fragments. Nevertheless, the two capabilities have largely developed separately: flexible-size diffusion typically operates on atoms or generic tokens, whereas representative fragment diffusion models initialize a fragment graph whose size is sampled before denoising. To bridge this gap, we introduce FragFlex, a flexible-length discrete diffusion model that performs node insertion and deletion at the fragment level. The model jointly learns fragment count, frag-ment identities, and inter-fragment connectivity while dynamically adjusting the graph size during denoising. This design allows molecular size and composition to co-evolve within a single generative trajectory while retaining the chemical inductive bias of fragment-based generation. Finally, a neural attachment-site predictor and valence-aware assembler convert the generated fragment graph into an atom-level molecule. In large-scale experiments generating 10 million molecules, FragFlex maintains both high uniqueness and chemical validity, showing a favorable trade-off between the two. Our main contributions are as follows: • We introduce FragFlex, a discrete diffusion model that enables flexible molecular-size generation through fragment-level node insertion and deletion. • We develop a neural attachment-site predictor and valence-aware assembler for converting fragment graphs into valid molecules. • In experiments generating 10 million molecules, FragFlex achieves high chemical validity and a favorable uniqueness–validity trade-off. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 De Novo Molecular Generation 

De novo molecular generation aims to learn distributions over molecular structures for unconditional or property-guided sampling [24]. Early neural approaches often represented molecules as SMILES strings and applied recurrent models or variational autoencoders [8, 16, 28]. Graph-based methods instead operate directly on atoms and bonds, using variational autoencoders, autoregressive models, and normalizing flows [20, 22, 29, 30]. More recently, score-based and diffusion models have formulated molecular generation as iterative denoising of molecular graphs [13, 14]. In particular, discrete diffusion methods such as D3PM [1] enable diffusion over categorical variables, providing a natural framework for molecular graphs with discrete atom and bond types. DiGress applies this framework to graph generation using categorical transition kernels and a permutation-equivariant graph transformer [33]. 

2.2 Flexible-Length Molecular Generation 

Autoregressive models can naturally generate molecules of varying sizes through a stopping decision, whereas many graph diffusion models sample the graph size beforehand and keep it fixed during denoising. DiGress, for instance, samples the graph size from the training-set size distribution before denoising and keeps it fixed throughout generation [33]. Recent methods instead allow graph size to change during generation. IFH uses insertion, filling, and halting operations [5], while GrIDDD deletes nodes during forward diffusion and inserts them during reverse generation [23]. In the sequence domain, FlexMDM enables flexible-length diffusion through mask-token insertion [15]. FragFlex applies this size-adaptive principle at the fragment level. Starting from a single root fragment, it predicts fragment insertions and jointly denoises fragment identities and connectivity. 

2.3 Fragment-Based Molecular Generation 

Fragment-based molecular generative models construct molecules from reusable molecular sub-structures, or fragments, rather than individual atoms. This coarse representation can reduce the number of generative decisions while incorporating chemically meaningful substructures into the generation process [12, 34]. Fragment-level denoising methods instead operate directly on coarse fragment graphs. FragDiffusion applies discrete diffusion to BRICS-derived fragment graphs whose size is selected before denoising and reconstructs atom-level molecules using attachment modes stored in a precomputed lookup table [19]. Unlike prior fragment-level denoising methods, it allows the number of fragments to change during generation while jointly modeling fragment identities and connectivity. It further predicts attachment sites directly and uses a valence-aware assembler to reconstruct the final atom-level molecule. 

## 3 Methods 

Overview. FragFlex generates a molecule in two stages (Figure 1). First, starting from a single root fragment, a discrete diffusion model generates a coarse graph by jointly modeling fragment identities, connectivity, and graph size (Figure 2). Second, a neural attachment-site predictor and a valence-aware assembler convert this fragment graph into an atom-level molecular graph. Our model combines FragDiffusion [19], a discrete diffusion model over fragment graphs, with GrIDDD [23], an atom-level diffusion model that enables variable graph sizes through node insertions and deletions. 

Problem definition. Let V = {F1, . . . , F K } be a fixed vocabulary of BRICS-derived fragment types extracted from the training molecules [6]. Each Fk specifies an atom-and-bond substructure and may contain either a single heavy atom or multiple heavy atoms. We retain the K = 100 

most frequent fragment types. We represent an atom-level molecule M by a clean fragment graph 

G0 = ( X0, E 0, S 0) with N fragments. The node label X0,i ∈ { 1, . . . , K } identifies the fragment at node i, and E0,ij ∈ { 0, 1} indicates whether fragments i and j are connected. For each connected fragment pair (i, j ), S0,ij and S0,ji denote the local indices of the attachment atoms in fragments i

and j, respectively, with S0,ij ∈ { 0, . . . , A i − 1}, where Ai is the number of atoms in fragment i.Our goal is to jointly model fragment identities, topology, attachment sites, and graph size. Unlike FragDiffusion [19], which assembles molecules using a fragment-pair lookup table precomputed from attachment frequencies in the training data, FragFlex represents fragment connectivity with binary edges and directly predicts the corresponding attachment atoms.

## 5 Conclusion 

We introduced FragFlex, a flexible-length molecular diffusion model that performs node insertion and deletion at the fragment level. FragFlex jointly predicts the number, identities, and connectivity of molecular fragments. A neural attachment-site predictor and valence-aware assembler then convert the generated fragment graph into an atom-level molecule. Together, these components allow molecular size to evolve throughout generation while retaining structural information encoded by fragments. At a scale of 10 7 generated molecules, FragFlex produces more than 9.5 million distinct valid molecules, demonstrating a strong balance between validity and uniqueness. These results suggest that fragment-level structural flexibility provides an effective foundation for large-scale chemical-space exploration. This flexibility also suggests a promising extension to reinforcement learning, where property-based rewards could balance the exploration of new fragment combinations with the exploitation of promising molecules. The effectiveness of size-adaptive diffusion models such as GrIDDD in molecular property targeting motivates extending FragFlex to protein-target-conditioned generation. This extension would allow fragment composition and molecular size to adapt to target-specific binding environments. A.2 Fragment Graph Preprocessing 

Each molecule is decomposed into fragments using the preprocessing pipeline of FragDiffusion. In FragDiffusion, each connection between two fragments is represented by a single categorical code called an attachment mode. For an edge (i, j ), this code identifies the atom in fragment Fi, the atom in fragment Fj , and the bond type connecting them. For example, one attachment mode may represent “connect atom 3 of Fi to atom 1 of Fj with a single bond.” The possible attachment modes and their empirical frequencies are obtained from the training data and stored in a lookup table. FragFlex uses this lookup table only during preprocessing to convert the attachment-mode labels into neural attachment-site targets. For each fragment edge (i, j ), preprocessing recovers the two local atom indices and stores them as S0,ij and S0,ji , while replacing the attachment-mode edge with the binary labels 

E0,ij = E0,ji = 1 .

Consequently, the model is trained and sampled using binary fragment topology and explicit directed attachment-site targets. Connectivity repair. Because fragment edges are sampled independently, the generated fragment graph is not guaranteed to be connected. If a newly inserted node is resolved into a fragment without an edge to the existing graph, the sampler connects it to the existing node with the highest predicted probability of a real edge. After the final reverse step, any remaining components are joined by repeatedly adding the highest-scoring edge between two different components. If the final graph contains C connected components before this repair, exactly C − 1 edges are added. 

Attachment-site prediction. The attachment-site logits do not participate in the categorical reverse posterior. In a cyclic graph, an invalid predicted edge can be skipped because the remaining edges may still keep the graph connected. Skipping is penalized and is not allowed for trees, where every edge is required for connectivity. Beam states are ranked by the following priorities: more successfully added fragment edges, fewer skipped edges, a higher accumulated neural pair score, and fewer non-top-1 attachment choices. After each fragment edge, only the best Bbeam states are retained. Thus, k limits the local atom choices for each edge, whereas Bbeam limits the number of global partial assemblies. After all edges are processed, beam molecules are examined in the same ranking order. They are fully sanitized, required to be connected, and deduplicated by canonical SMILES. At most Pmax unique valid products are collected into a pool P, and the final molecule is sampled as 

cM ∼ Unif( P), |P| ≤ Pmax . (14) Assembly fails if P = ∅.The search is score-based until P is constructed, while the final product is sampled uniformly from this pool. This prevents the assembler from always selecting the highest-scoring valid product and increases atom-level assembly diversity for the same fragment graph. Because both the local candidate sets and beam states are truncated, assembly may fail even when an unexamined valid assembly exists. 

## B Additional Results 

B.1 Scaffold Diversity and Molecular Quality 

We additionally evaluate scaffold-level diversity by counting unique non-empty Bemis–Murcko (BM) scaffolds [2]. As shown in Table 3, models generating more unique BM scaffolds tend to produce molecules with less favorable QED and SA profiles, characterized by lower QED and/or higher SA scores. Although FragFlex does not achieve the highest scaffold diversity, it obtains the best QED and SA scores while still generating millions of unique BM scaffolds. These results indicate that FragFlex provides a favorable balance between scaffold diversity and molecular quality rather than maximizing scaffold count alone. 

B.2 Ablation Study
