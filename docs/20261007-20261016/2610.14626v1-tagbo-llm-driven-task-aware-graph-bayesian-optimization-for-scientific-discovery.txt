# Abstract 

Mixed-variable optimization in scientific discovery often requires exploring large combinatorial spaces under costly experimental evaluations, making sample-efficient Bayesian optimization (BO) essential. However, existing BO methods typ-ically rely on structural descriptors to define similarity among categorical variables, implicitly assuming that structural similarity reflects similarity in task performance. In scientific optimization, this assumption is often violated, leading to surrogate representations that are poorly aligned with the optimization objective. To address this limitation, we propose TagBO (Task-aware Graph Bayesian Optimization), a method that integrates LLM-derived relational priors with descriptor-based struc-tural information for mixed-variable Bayesian optimization. Rather than directly using LLM outputs as predictive scores, TagBO treats LLM outputs as a potentially noisy source of task-aware relational priors and extracts coarse ordinal relational signals from LLM reasoning to construct task-aware similarity structures for scien-tific optimization. TagBO then performs constrained Gaussian process Bayesian optimization over the resulting joint discrete-continuous representation space. Ex-periments on diverse chemistry and materials formulation tasks demonstrate that TagBO consistently improves sample efficiency under fixed evaluation budgets across LLMs of varying capabilities. 

# 1 Introduction 

Accelerating scientific discovery through intelligent experimental design has become a central goal in AI for Science [16, 40]. In many scientific optimization problems, practitioners must jointly select categorical variables (e.g., reagent types, reaction pathways) and tune continuous parameters (e.g., concentrations, temperatures), with each evaluation typically requiring costly real-world experiments, resulting in mixed-variable problem in which each experimental evaluation is typically costly and time-consuming [7, 14, 37, 42]. Bayesian optimization (BO) has emerged as a principled strategy for such problems, and a central challenge it must address is how to measure similarity among categorical choices so that the surrogate model can generalize across the combinatorial space [5, 10, 27]. A rich body of work has tackled this challenge using domain-specific descriptors—molecular fin-gerprints, chemical group encodings, learned embeddings, and other structural representations [17, 29, 39]. These methods have been widely adopted and are effective in many settings. Yet they

often rely on a task-agnostic notion of proximity, assuming that nearby candidates in descriptor space exhibit similar task behavior—an assumption that can fail in the presence of task-specific mechanisms or activity cliffs [25, 33]. This assumption implicitly defines the geometry over which BO generalizes. When it fails, the surrogate model may extrapolate along directions that are irrelevant, or even misleading, for the target task. For example, two solvents with nearly identical molecular structures may exhibit drastically different electrochemical stability because the mechanisms governing task performance are not captured by global structural descriptors. These observations suggest that the challenge is not merely to design better descriptors, but to enable task-aware generalization in Bayesian optimization. In mixed-variable BO, the surrogate model generalizes according to assumptions induced by descriptor representations. When these assumptions fail to reflect the functional relationships relevant to the target task, BO may generalize along misleading directions. Interestingly, scientific domains already contain extensive task-specific relational knowledge in the literature, including experimental findings, empirical heuristics, and mechanistic insights. Large language models (LLMs), pre-trained on broad scientific corpora, can implicitly encode how different materials or components relate under specific task contexts [6, 8, 36]. However , directly using raw LLM outputs to define optimization spaces remains challenging due to noise and inconsistency across candidates. At the same time, structural descriptors alone often fail to capture task-specific functional relationships. These observations suggest that neither source alone is sufficient. To address these limitations, we propose TagBO (Task-aware Graph Bayesian Optimization), a method that integrates LLM-derived relational priors with structural information for mixed-variable Bayesian optimization. Rather than directly using LLM outputs as predictive scores, TagBO extracts ordinal relational signals from LLM reasoning to represent task-aware relationships between categor-ical choices. These signals are combined with structural information derived from physicochemical descriptors, enabling the optimization space to reflect both task-specific functional behavior and local structural similarity. We evaluate TagBO on diverse scientific optimization benchmarks involving chemistry and materials formulation tasks. Experimental results show that TagBO consistently improves sample efficiency under fixed experimental budgets and remains effective across LLMs of varying capabilities. Our contributions are as follows: • Task-aware similarity modeling for scientific optimization. We propose TagBO, a method that incorporates LLM-derived relational knowledge to construct task-aware similarity structures for mixed-variable Bayesian optimization. • Ordinal relational modeling from LLM reasoning. TagBO treats LLM outputs as a potentially noisy source of task-aware relational priors, and extracts coarse ordinal signals from LLM reasoning for task-aware similarity modeling in scientific optimization problems. • Improved sample efficiency under fixed budgets. Across diverse mixed-variable benchmarks, TagBO consistently improves optimization performance under fixed experimental budgets and remains robust across LLMs of varying capabilities. 

# 2 Related Work 

Structured representations in Bayesian optimization. Many approaches construct similarity structures from domain-specific descriptors, learned embeddings, or latent representations [18, 15] to support Bayesian optimization in scientific design problems, such as LADDER, LOLBO [11, 23]. These methods enable the surrogate model to generalize across large combinatorial or molecular search spaces through representation-induced similarity. However, these approaches are primarily designed for optimizing or generating individual molecular structures in continuous latent spaces, and are less suitable for mixed-variable formulation problems involving constrained combinations of heterogeneous categorical and continuous variables. In contrast, prior work in discrete and combinatorial Bayesian optimization has focused on defining explicit similarity structures to enable generalization across candidate configurations. For example, COMBO [26] represents the search space as a graph Cartesian product and constructs kernels over this structure, allowing the surrogate model to generalize via graph-induced similarity. CoCaBO [30] further addresses mixed continuous and categorical optimization through kernels that share infor-mation across categorical variables. LLM-enhanced Bayesian optimization. Recent work has explored integrating LLMs into Bayesian optimization to guide candidate generation and improve search efficiency. LLAMBO [22] leverages LLMs to propose promising initial points and generate candidate solutions during optimiza-tion, while relying on standard acquisition functions for selection. BOPRO [1] performs optimization in a latent space induced by pretrained LLM embeddings. CAKE [35] employs LLMs to adapt Gaussian process kernels during optimization, enabling more flexible inductive biases for Bayesian optimization. More recently, LGBO [41] further incorporates LLMs to guide the search process through generation or scoring mechanisms. And LABO [9] incorporates LLM-generated predictions into Bayesian optimization through a confidence-gated dual-surrogate framework. However, existing approaches do not explicitly construct interpretable task-aware structures over the search space, which is particularly important in scientific discovery problems where understanding relationships among candidate configurations is crucial.

3.5 Representation-Level Interpretation 

We provide several representation-level interpretations to better understand why task-aware represen-tations may improve Bayesian optimization performance in mixed-variable scientific search. Our discussion focuses on three aspects: (i) Gaussian process modeling under representation warping, (ii) the relationship between representation quality and surrogate approximation behavior, and (iii) the adaptive trade-off between task-aware relational guidance and structural smoothness. Due to space limitations, technical assumptions and derivations are deferred to Appendix B. 

LLM relational priors as surrogate priors. Our method can be interpreted as transforming ordinal relational knowledge derived from LLM reasoning into a Gaussian process prior over the search space. Rather than directly using LLM outputs as predictive scores, TagBO incorporates relative ranking relations into the learned representation space prior to surrogate modeling. Let w : X → Z denote the learned representation map induced by the fused relational-structural graph, and let g ∼ GP (m, K )

be a Gaussian process defined on the representation space Z. Defining f (x) = g(w(x)) , induces a valid Gaussian process on the original input space with kernel 

Kw(x, x ′) = K(w(x), w (x′)) .

Under this interpretation, LLM-derived ordinal relations are not used as direct predictions, but instead shape the similarity structure and inductive bias of the surrogate model itself. This connects task-aware relational guidance with probabilistic surrogate modeling in Bayesian optimization. 

LLM ordinal priors and surrogate approximation. Bayesian optimization critically depends on whether the surrogate model can generalize smoothly across the search space. However, structural similarity alone may fail to reflect task-relevant behavior in scientific optimization problems. This mo-tivates using LLM-derived ordinal relations as an additional task-aware source of information. Here, we analyze the representation-level effect of the LLM-induced representation relative to purely struc-tural and one-hot representations; the subsequent graph-smoothing formulation further regularizes this signal with structural similarity. This interpretation suggests that the learned representation emerges from balancing coarse-grained ordinal relational guidance with fine-grained local structural smoothness. 

# 4 Experiments 

We evaluate the performance of TagBO on multiple optimization datasets from the domains of pharmaceuticals, chemistry, and materials science. These tasks are designed to provide a unified comparison of optimization efficiency and stability under limited evaluation budgets, benchmarking against both traditional Bayesian optimization methods and recent LLM-guided approaches. More experiments for stability and ablation are also provided in Appendix C. 

4.1 Experimental Setup Baselines. The following methods are used as baselines: 1) One-hot Feature BO: Discrete variables are encoded using one-hot representations, and the concatenated sparse vectors are fed into a Bayesian optimization model for iterative search. 2) Property Feature BO: Physicochemical properties of candidate substances are extracted and concatenated into continuous feature vectors, which are then fed into a Bayesian optimization model for iterative search. 3) COMBO [26]: A graph structure is constructed over the discrete combinatorial space, and Gaussian process modeling and optimization are performed based on graph kernels. 4) BOPRO [1]: Iteratively updates the LLM prompt using selected past generations to sample new candidates that explore or exploit different regions of the search space. 5) LGBO [41]: This method incorporates LLM predictions as low-cost evaluation signals into the BO method alongside real experimental results. A gating mechanism is used to dynamically balance the use of LLM predictions and real evaluations, thereby improving optimization efficiency. 

Tasks. We evaluate all methods on six optimization tasks spanning chemistry, materials science, and energy-related applications. Detailed data descriptions are provided in C.1: 1) Organic Photovoltaics [34] (2200 combinations): Maximize spectral overlap predicted by DFT by optimizing donor–acceptor building blocks in organic photovoltaic materials. 2) Suzuki–Miyaura Reaction [28] (384 combinations): Maximize reaction yield on a fixed-substrate Suzuki–Miyaura reaction subset by optimizing three categorical reaction conditions: ligand, base, and solvent. 7Figure 2: Optimization trajectories on six tasks. In the main experiments, the LLM used is Intern S1 241B Thinking, a large language model pretrained on scientific knowledge. All experiments were conducted using 4 NVIDIA H200 GPUs.

4.3 Ablation Experiences Representation source ablation. To investigate the roles of relational guidance and structural smoothness, we compare the full TagBO model with two reduced variants that use only LLM-derived relational features or only physicochemical property features. As shown in Fig. 4(A), the LLM-only variant achieves strong early-stage optimization performance, indicating that ordinal relational priors provide effective task-aware guidance during early search. However, its performance gradually saturates in later stages, suggesting that relational priors alone are insufficient for fine-grained surrogate refinement. In contrast, the structural-only variant exhibits consistently weaker performance throughout the optimization process. Effect of LLM prior quality. We compare LLM backbones with varying scales and reasoning capabilities, including Intern-S1-241B [4], Llama-3.3-70B [38], Qwen3-30B [3](Instruct and Thinking), as well as smaller variants such as Qwen3-4B and Llama-3.2-3B . As shown in Fig. 4(B) and Fig. 4(C), stronger models generally achieve lower Step@95% values and improved final sample efficiency. In particular, Intern-S1-241B Thinking consistently reaches 95% of the oracle optimum using the fewest evaluations, suggesting that domain-relevant scientific pretraining provides more informative relational priors for guiding optimization. Interestingly, optimization trajectories from different LLM backbones remain relatively close during early search, suggesting that the proposed ordinal relational formulation is less sensitive to precise numerical calibration from a specific LLM. Stronger models mainly improve later-stage refinement by providing more informative task-aware relational structure. 

4.4 Real-world Formulation Experiment Task setting. This task aims to maximize the measured thermal conductivity of a thermally conductive adhesive while satisfying mechanical and electrical performance requirements. The mixed-variable search space involves selecting 10 fillers from a library of 29 candidate materials and optimizing their 10 corresponding continuous composition variables, as summarized in Table 7. The thermal conductivity qualification threshold is 2.8 W /(m · K) . The formulation must also satisfy tensile strength ≥ 1 MPa , shear strength ≥ 2 MPa , and dielectric strength ≥ 7.9 kV /mm . The experiment includes 34 physical evaluations, each requiring approximately three days. 

Results. As shown in Fig. 6, the best measured thermal conductivity increases from 

0.1823 W /(m · K) at the first evaluation to 2.995 W /(m · K) after only 34 evaluations, exceed-ing the qualification threshold. The final formulation achieves a tensile strength of 3.36 MPa , a shear strength of 2.92 MPa , and a dielectric strength of 23 .5 kV /mm , satisfying all three performance requirements. Under the ninit = 2 d setting used in standard TuRBO [13] implementations, a 20-variable problem would require 40 evaluations for initialization alone. In this large combinatorial and continuous search space, TagBO thus meets all four engineering requirements within a total experimental budget smaller than this initial design, demonstrating its sample efficiency for expensive formulation optimization. 

# 5 Conclusion 

In this work, we proposed TagBO, a task-aware graph Bayesian optimization framework for mixed-variable scientific optimization. # D Limitations 

The effectiveness of TagBO depends on the quality of the task-aware relational priors extracted from the LLM. In domains where relevant scientific knowledge is sparse, highly specialized, or poorly represented in the pretraining corpus, the induced ordinal relations may become noisy or misaligned with the downstream optimization objective. We believe that retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and domain-specific knowledge integration may help alleviate this limitation by grounding relational reasoning in external scientific resources, and we plan to explore this direction in future work. In addition, TagBO primarily modifies the representation and surrogate prior of Bayesian optimization while leaving the underlying BO optimization procedure unchanged. Consequently, our theoretical analysis focuses on representation-level surrogate behavior rather than cumulative regret guarantees for the overall sequential optimization process. Deriving regret bounds that explicitly characterize the interaction between task-aware representations, kernel geometry, and acquisition dynamics remains an important direction for future research. 24 Figure 9: Optimization performance of repeated Intern-S1-241B Thinking variants for feature extraction on the Suzuki–Miyaura reaction dataset.
