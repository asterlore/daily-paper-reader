Abstract 

Multi-objective molecular optimization is a crit-ical process in drug discovery that aims to modify a given molecule to simultaneously improve multiple properties while maintain-ing a similar overall structure. This process often requires iterative modifications guided by computational or experimental feedback. Recent large language model (LLM)-based methods have begun to explore this iterative approach; however, these methods are not explicitly trained to leverage such feedback, while existing approaches primarily focus on single-turn molecular optimization. To ad-dress this gap, we introduce Multi-turn Feed-back Guided (MFG) molecular optimization, a reinforcement learning framework that trains an LLM to progressively optimize molecules using feedback from previous turns. MFG introduces a feedback vector capturing both property improvements and source molecule similarity, alongside a feedback-aligned re-ward function that promotes progressive im-provement across turns. We apply MFG over GeLLM 3O, a state-of-the-art LLM model for multi-objective molecular optimization, eval-uate GeLLM 3O-MFG on ten multi-objective molecular optimization tasks. The results demonstrate that GeLLM 3O-MFG achieves the highest average similarity-constrained suc-cess rate (81.50%), outperforming the base model GeLLM 3O ( 70 .81% ). 

1 Introduction 

Multi-objective molecular optimization (MOMO) (Sun et al., 2022) is a critical process in drug dis-covery (Hughes et al., 2011) that aims to modify a given molecule to simultaneously improve multi-ple properties while maintaining a similar overall structure. Such improvements are necessary be-cause even promising molecules may suffer from poor solubility, insufficient selectivity, or toxicity that hinders their development into viable drugs. Achieving these improvements often requires iter-ative structural modifications to balance multiple, potentially competing molecular properties. By it-eratively reasoning on how to modify molecules, chemists can rely on not only their expert knowl-edge, but also computational (Nicolaou and Brown, 2013) or experimental feedback. Large language models (LLMs) have shown promise on similar it-erative tasks through multi-turn reasoning (Naveed et al., 2025), providing a promising mechanism to mimic this approach to MOMO. Recent efforts (Ye et al., 2025; Zuo et al., 2025) have used LLMs for MOMO. Several methods (Liu et al., 2023; Le et al., 2024) incorporated iterative feedback at inference-time, prompting LLMs to modify molecules over multiple turns while incor-porating feedback on the modified molecules of previous turns. However, these methods are not trained on how to leverage such feedback. Instead, they rely only on the LLMs’ general-purpose pre-training and basic reasoning capabilities. Other methods have used supervised finetuning methods (Dey et al., 2025) and reinforcement learning (RL) (Liu et al., 2025) to train models for MOMO. How-ever, training is limited to non-iterative, single-turn generation, with no specialized training on multi-turn feedback. Here, we introduce MFG, a Multi-turn Feedback 

Guided framework for MOMO. MFG formulates MOMO as a multi-turn problem with feedback, using RL to train an LLM to iteratively optimize molecules by leveraging feedback from previous turns. MFG introduces a feedback vector that cap-tures both property improvements and similarity to the given source molecule at each turn, providing dynamically updated feedback to guide the LLM policy throughout the optimization process. MFG’s RL training is guided by a reward function that aligns with this feedback while also encouraging progressive improvement across turns. During in-ference, MFG can use its trained model to generate 1arXiv:2610.18851v1 [cs.AI] 13 Oct 2026 multiple candidate molecules at each turn and se-lect the best molecule for further turns, carefully balancing property improvement and similarity. To-gether, MFG serves as a powerful new framework for MOMO with feedback. To evaluate MFG, we instantiate GeLLM 3O-MFG, using GeLLM 3O, a state-of-the-art molecular optimization LLM, as the base model for MFG’s RL training. We compare GeLLM 3O-MFG against GeLLM 3O and other existing molecular optimization baselines on the 

MuMOInstruct dataset (Dey et al., 2025), a bench-mark featuring diverse property combinations and both in-distribution and out-of-distribution MOMO tasks. Across all tasks, GeLLM 3O-MFG achieves the highest average similarity-constrained success rate (81.50%), outperforming the best baseline (BioMedGPT-Mol, 77 .81% ) and GeLLM 3O(70 .81% ). An ablation study shows that multi-turn feedback and RL training each contribute adistinct advantage to MFG. Additionally, a case study illustrates how GeLLM 3O-MFG adapts its optimization strategy based on feedback. GeLLM 3O-MFG is publicly available at https: //anonymous.4open.science/r/MFG-85BD/ .

2 Related Work 

RL for Molecular optimization Existing molec-ular optimization LLMs such as GeLLM 3O (Dey et al., 2025) have explored instruction tuning of general-purpose LLMs on large-scale MOMO tasks (see Appendix A.1 for a summary of more related work). However, this supervised learn-ing approach may limit model’s ability to explore chemical space beyond the distribution of train-ing examples. Recent works have leveraged RL to further align molecular optimization LLMs with desired molecule properties. For example, DrugIm-proverGPT (Liu et al., 2025) employed reward-based RL to explore molecule modifications for improving multiple molecule properties. However, the sparsity of high-reward molecules in chemical space can make effective exploration challenging. To address this challenge, RePO (Li et al., 2026) leveraged reference optimized molecules to provide LLMs with templates of successful optimization. Despite different designs of RL methods, existing works primarily optimize source molecules in a single turn, treating each optimization as an inde-pendent process. Our work considers a new setting in which molecules are iteratively optimized over multiple turns, and develops a novel multi-turn training framework for this setting. To the best of our knowledge, our work is the first to improve molecular optimization LLMs through multi-turn RL. 

3 MFG Framework 

3.1 Problem Formulation 

Multi-objective molecular optimization (MOMO) seeks to modify a source molecule C0 into a struc-turally similar molecule C while improving m

properties P = ( p1, p 2, · · · , p m). Existing LLM-based approaches commonly formulate this task as a single-turn problem, where an LLM directly generates C in a single response when prompted with C0 and P. However, this single-turn approach offers limited opportunity to adjust the optimiza-tion strategy. MOMO can be very challenging, as structural changes that improve one property may degrade another, and improvement patterns may not remain consistent across different C0.To address this challenge, we reformulate MOMO as a multi-turn problem with feedback, where an LLM iteratively generates optimized molecules for a given C0 and P, adjusting its strate-gies based on previous turn’s property change and similarity to C0. 3.2 MFG Training 

MFG trains πθ to leverage feedback for multi-turn MOMO by maximizing an expected reward R(Ct)

(Section 3.2.2). Specifically, we use group-relative policy optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024) to train πθ by sampling multiple candidate molecules 

Ct at each turn t conditioned on C0, P, and τ1: t−1,and updating πθ based on their relative rewards. To optimize the expectation over t in Eq. 1, MFG samples t ∼ Uniform(1 , T ) during training, en-suring that πθ is trained to leverage feedback at every turn in multi-turn MOMO. MFG employs on-policy training, where the previous turns τ1: t−1

are generated using the same πθ that generates Ct.However, MFG treats τ1: t−1 as fixed condition-ing context rather than optimizing its generation based on R(Ct). Together, these design choices allow πθ to learn effective feedback-guided molec-ular optimization strategies by comparing candi-date molecules generated under the same condi-tioning context. As πθ is updated during training, the conditioning of previous turns τ1: t−1, which are generated on-policy and used as conditioning context, also reflect the policy’s changing output distribution. 

3.2.1 Candidate Advancement Criterion 

To encourage progressive improvements across turns, MFG defines a candidate selection criterion 

Advance( ·) with two purposes: (1) during training, identifying the best molecule from previous turns as a reference for progressive improvement; and (2) during inference, selecting a promising molecule to carry forward, along with its feedback, to guide subsequent optimization. Specifically, Advance( ·)

is a hierarchical selection criterion that balances im-provements across multiple target properties with structural similarity to C0 through prioritized com-parisons. It prioritizes molecules satisfying a pre-defined similarity threshold δ to C0, followed by molecules that improve all target properties. When no candidate improves all properties, those improv-ing more properties are preferred, preventing large gains in one property from outweighing broader improvements. Together, Advance( ·) prioritizes the breadth of property improvements over their magnitude while preserving structural similarity (see Appendix A.2 for details).

3.3 MFG Inference 

At inference time, MFG performs iterative molec-ular optimization using the trained policy πθ, con-ditioned on the source molecule C0, target prop-erty set P, initially empty set of previous turns 

τ . At each turn t, MFG samples N candidates and applies the selection criterion Advance( ·)

introduced in Section 3.2.1 to obtain Ct =Advance( {C1 

> t

, . . . , C Nt }). The selected molecule and its feedback vector ft, capturing property changes and structural similarity relative to C0,form the updated τ1: t = τ1: t−1 ∪ { (Ct, ft)}. This process repeats until turn T , with each selected molecule and its feedback incorporated into the conditioning context for the next turn. The final optimized molecule CT is then selected from all 

T × N generated candidates according to the crite-ria described in Section 4.3. Unlike the final output selection, Advance( ·)

contributes to feedback construction during infer-ence by determining which intermediate molecule and its associated feedback are carried forward to subsequent turns. This selection aims to carry for-ward a promising intermediate molecule that pro-vides a meaningful direction for subsequent opti-mization, allowing future turns to build on partial progress. Thus, Advance( ·) supports feedback-guided MOMO by limiting the propagation of unfa-vorable candidates and retaining informative feed-back that helps the model adapt across turns. 

4 Experimental Settings 

4.1 GeLLM 3O-MFG 

We use MFG to train GeLLM 3O-MFG, a multi-turn MOMO model based on GeLLM 3O-P(6) Llama ,a state-of-the-art single-turn MOMO model. GeLLM 3O-P(6) Llama is particularly suitable for training with MFG because its previous training on single-turn MOMO has allowed it to acquire sub-stantial knowledge of structure-property relation-ships. Implementation details of GeLLM 3O-MFG are provided in Appendix A.4. 

4.2 Dataset 

GeLLM 3O-MFG is trained and evaluated using the MuMOInstruct benchmark dataset following the training/testing split established by GeLLM 3O. 

MuMOInstruct includes multi-property optimiza-tion tasks on combinations of six target properties: blood-brain barrier permeability ( B), dopamine re-ceptor D2 binding affinity ( D), human intestinal absorption ( H), mutagenicity ( M), penalized logP, a measure of lipophilicity ( P), and quantitative esti-mate of drug-likeness ( Q). The optimization objec-tive is to increase the values of B, D, H, P, and Q,while decreasing that of M. Each task consists of a set of source molecules, paired with their corre-sponding reference optimized molecules that we use for reward normalization in Eq 3. Specifically, the property improvements achieved by the ref-erence optimized molecule relative to the source molecule are used to normalize G(·), while its struc-tural similarity to the source molecule is used to 4normalize S(·) (details in Appendix A.3). The evaluation set consists of five in-distribution (IND) tasks ( BDP , BDQ , BPQ , DPQ , and BDPQ ) and five out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks ( MPQ , BDMQ , BHMQ ,

BMPQ , and HMPQ ). IND tasks evaluate the model’s optimization capability on property combinations seen during training, while OOD tasks assess its zero-shot generalization to unseen property combi-nations. 

4.3 Evaluation Metrics 

Following GeLLM 3O, we evaluate optimization performance using three complementary metrics: 

(1) Success Rate (SR α), which is the propor-tion of source molecules successfully optimized to improve all property objectives while main-taining Tanimoto similarity of at least α to the source molecule; (2) Similarity (Sim ) that is the average Tanimoto similarity between the opti-mized molecules and their corresponding source molecules; and (3) Relative Improvement (RI )measuring the average relative change across the target properties with respect to the source molecules (Appendix A.5). Higher SR α, Sim and 

RI are desirable, indicating better overall optimiza-tion performance. For each source molecule C0,we select the optimized molecule C as the candi-date with the highest RI among those that improve all target properties and satisfy the similarity thresh-old α. If no such candidate exists, the optimization is considered unsuccessful. 

4.4 Baselines 

We evaluate GeLLM 3O-MFG against the base model GeLLM 3O-P(6) Llama , and two categories of LLMs with different levels of chemistry-specific training: general-purpose LLMs and LLMs for chemistry which are trained on large-scale chemistry instruction tasks. We use GeLLM 3O-P(6) Llama to directly assess the im-provement introduced by MFG while keeping the underlying model architecture fixed. In terms of general-purpose LLMs, we select two of the largest and most capable open-weight models available to us at the time of evaluation, Qwen3.5-9B (Qwen Team, 2026) and Ministral-3-8B (Liu et al., 2026). They serve as references for molecular opti-mization without adaptation to the chemistry do-main. We choose LlaSMol (Yu et al., 2024) and BioMedGPT-Mol (Zuo et al., 2025) as LLMs for chemistry baselines due to their reported strong performance on MOMO tasks. For fair compar-ison, all baselines use identical inputs, inference settings (Section 4.5), and output processing proce-dures, with model and prompt details provided in Appendix A.6. 

4.5 Inference Settings 

We evaluate all methods under both single-turn and multi-turn settings, denoted as ST and MT, respec-tively. For baselines originally designed for ST optimization, we extend them to the MT setting by applying iterative inference with the same feedback vector and selection criterion as MFG. This allows us to separate the contribution of MFG inference from MFG training. Following the evaluation protocol of GeLLM 3O, we generate a maximum of 20 molecules through-out the optimization process for each source molecule. For T -turn optimization, we generate an equal number of candidates at each turn. To en-sure a comprehensive evaluation, we independently evaluate each method with T ∈ { 1, 2, 3, 4} turns. We report the results corresponding to the highest 

SR across the evaluated values of T for each task. The full results for each value of T are provided in Appendix A.7.

5.1.2 Comparison with GeLLM 3O

As shown in Tables 1 and 2, GeLLM 3O-MFG con-sistently outperforms its base model, GeLLM 3O, across all IND and OOD tasks. Under the MT setting, GeLLM 3O-MFG outperforms GeLLM 3Oacross all IND and OOD tasks, achieving an aver-age increase of 10 .69% in SR0.5 and 0.47 in RI .Moreover, the gap between ST and MT perfor-mance is substantially larger for GeLLM 3O-MFG than for GeLLM 3O. Moving from ST to MT in-creases GeLLM 3O-MFG’s average SR0.5 by 5.27% (compared with only 0.76 for GeLLM 3O) and im-proves RI on 9 tasks (compared with only 2 for GeLLM 3O), while decreasing average Sim by only 0.02. As both models share the same backbone and are evaluated under identical inference settings, the larger gains from ST to MT setting suggest that MFG’s training better equips GeLLM 3O-MFG to leverage intermediate molecules and their feed-back to guide subsequent optimization. These find-ings are also consistent with MFG’s reward design, which promotes progressive improvement over pre-viously generated molecules.

5.3 Case Study 

Figure 2 shows a successful two-turn optimization by GeLLM 3O-MFG on four properties: B, D, P,and Q. In this optimization, the first turn substan-tially improves B, P, and Q, while the second turn focuses on improving D. The source molecule (Fig-ure 2a) contains multiple polyphenolic esters that contribute to low lipophilicity and high molecu-lar weight, both of which reduce B and Q. In the first turn, GeLLM 3O-MFG reduces polarity by hy-drolyzing one phenolic ester and replacing another with a 1,1,1-trifluoropropyl ester. These modi-fications also reduce the molecular weight from 718 to 461, which improves Q by aligning bet-ter with molecular weight guidelines for drug-like molecules, such as Lipinski’s Rule of Five (Lip-inski et al., 2001). However, the changes intro-duced in this turn change very little to improve the molecule’s predicted DRD2 activity, increasing from 0.03 to 0.05. Based on this feedback, GeLLM 3O-MFG pri-oritizes D in the second turn, replacing the free dihydrobenzofuran carboxylic acid with a 4,4-difluoropiperidinyl group and hydrolyzing the 1,1,1-trifluoropropyl ester. This produces a sub-stantial improvement on D, while also further im-proving B, P, and Q. The improvement on D may be driven in part by the introduction of a cyclic amine, which helps the molecule better resemble patterns in established drugs targeting DRD2. Taken to-gether, this multi-turn optimization example illus-trates how GeLLM 3O-MFG shifts priorities across turns based on feedback, resulting in higher-quality candidate molecules after multiple turns. 

6 Conclusion 

In this work, we introduce MFG for molecu-lar optimization, a multi-turn RL framework that trains LLMs to iteratively optimize molecules us-ing previously generated candidates and their prop-erty feedback. By training a SOTA molecular optimization LLM, GeLLM 3O, with MFG, we develop GeLLM 3O-MFG. Across 10 MOMO tasks, GeLLM 3O-MFG outperforms GeLLM 3Oand achieves competitive performance against ex-isting baselines, improving molecular properties while preserving structural similarity. Extensive analyses demonstrate MFG’s effectiveness in lever-aging multi-turn feedback for progressive molecu-lar optimization. Limitations 

Despite the strong performance of MFG in multi-objective molecular optimization, several limi-tations remain. (1) Our experiments are con-ducted on MuMOInstruct , which includes MOMO tasks involving six target properties. Although GeLLM 3O-MFG demonstrates strong generaliza-tion to out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks with unseen property combinations, its effectiveness on addi-tional property objectives remains unexplored. (2) MFG relies on property oracles to provide inter-mediate property feedback and compute rewards for generated molecules. The reliability of this feedback depends on the accuracy of the property oracles, and the improvements observed in our eval-uations have not been experimentally validated. (3) During training, the reward function uses the target molecule provided by MuMOInstruct to normalize property improvements and structural similarity. This requirement may limit the applicability of the current training framework to datasets without cor-responding target molecules. (4) We apply MFG to GeLLM 3O to develop GeLLM 3O-MFG. Although the results demonstrate the effectiveness of MFG on this model, further evaluations on other molecu-lar optimization LLMs are needed to establish the generalizability of our framework. 

Ethical considerations 

MFG for molecular optimization aims to improve multiple property objectives through iterative gen-eration guided by intermediate property feedback. Although our framework is developed for molecu-lar optimization rather than harmful applications, its ability to generate and optimize candidate molecules may also be used to produce potentially unsafe compounds. Moreover, improvements in target property objectives and structural similarity to the source molecule do not necessarily indicate that the generated molecules are safe for practi-cal use. We therefore emphasize that molecules generated by MFG should undergo careful safety evaluation by expert chemists before being con-sidered for synthesis or experimental testing. Any practical application of the framework and its gen-erated molecules should also adhere to relevant safety regulations, ethical guidelines, and estab-lished professional practices.

A.1 Related Work Molecular optimization Computational meth-ods for molecular optimization has been ex-plored through a variety of paradigms, including molecule string translation and graph-based gener-ation. Chemformer (Irwin et al., 2022) and Prompt-MolOpt (Wu et al., 2024) used transformer models to translate input molecule strings into optimized molecules. Modof (Chen et al., 2021) and MI-MOSA (Fu et al., 2021) instead formulated molec-ular optimization as a graph modification problem and designed graph neural network-based meth-ods to edit substructures. However, these methods are developed for specific objectives and may have limited generalizability across diverse optimization tasks. More recently, LLMs have shown the poten-tial for molecular optimization by treating molecule string as a chemical language. For example, Chat-Drug (Liu et al., 2023) and Re 2DF (Le et al., 2024) designed inference-time methods to iteratively re-fine molecules based on LLM dialogue with ex-ternal domain feedback. DrugAssist (Ye et al., 2025) and GeLLM 3O (Dey et al., 2025) further instruction-tuned general-purpose LLMs specifi-cally for molecular optimization. BioMedGPT-Mol (Zuo et al., 2025) incorporated molecular optimiza-tion as one of multiple chemical tasks in a founda-tion chemical language model. Building on these ideas, our work finetunes a molecular optimization LLM via reinforcement learning (RL) so that it can be applied multiple times to generate progressively improved molecules.

A.4 Implementation Details Training We use a group size of four for GRPO per prompt. For groups with uninformative re-wards, we apply dynamic resampling with up to three attempts. We set the upper clipping parame-ter to 0.28 , the KL coefficient to 0.1, and the max-imum completion length to 96 tokens. Training uses fused AdamW with β1 = 0 .9, β2 = 0 .95 , ϵ =10 −8, weight decay of 0.1, and gradient clipping at 

1.0. For the training data, we sample 100 source molecules for each tasks in MuMOInstruct , exclud-ing the 5 OOD tasks. Training GeLLM 3O-MFG took approximately 36 hours using two NVIDIA A100 GPUs. For parameter-efficient training, we use LoRA with rank 16, scaling parameter 32, and dropout 0.05. We apply LoRA to the attention and Feed-Forward Network (FFN) projection layers in every Transformer block, while keeping all pretrained model parameters frozen. Training of GeLLM 3O-RL follows the same setup as GeLLM 3O-MFG, but excludes generated context, feedback, and the reward bonus (Eq. 4). 0.4.

> - Similarity to input molecule: [value] ([band]) - [property]: [source] -> [candidate] ([change], [status]; [direction])
