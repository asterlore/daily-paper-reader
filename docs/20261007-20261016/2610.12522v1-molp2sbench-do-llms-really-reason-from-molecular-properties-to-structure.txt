## ABSTRACT 

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in molecular discovery and structure elucidation, where decisions depend on physical observations. Exist-ing evaluations primarily focus on end-to-end molecular reconstruction, where correct predictions do not necessarily indicate that these results are grounded in the supplied properties. To address this, we introduce MolP2SBench, a bench-mark for evaluating molecular property-to-structure (P2S) reasoning in LLMs. Each task presents a masked molecular scaffold with given properties, requir-ing the model to select the missing structure from plausible candidates. To as-sess whether predictions genuinely rely on the provided properties, we test how models respond when properties are shuffled or replaced, thereby evaluating their reliance on correct property-structure pairing and their responsiveness to coun-terfactual property changes. We also establish a channel-wise leaderboard us-ing Property-Grounded Recovery (PGR), which gives equal weight to source and counterfactual recovery. In our evaluation of seven models, we find that LLMs can leverage physical properties for molecular structure prediction, but their per-formance varies substantially across property types. More importantly, higher accuracy with a given property does not necessarily imply greater responsiveness to that property. For example, a model may benefit more from 1H NMR than from 13 C NMR, yet be less likely to change its prediction when the 1H data is replaced. Interestingly, LLM performance does not necessarily reflect the rel-ative difficulty of these properties for human chemists. For instance, although UV–Vis spectra provide less direct structural information than NMR spectra, As-tra achieves higher accuracy with UV–Vis data than with NMR peaks. In sum-mary, MolP2SBench provides a framework for studying how LLMs extract and use information from different physical properties, revealing performance patterns that may differ from expectations based on conventional chemical intuition and offering a foundation for developing more reliably property-grounded models for molecular and quantum-chemical inference. The code and dataset are available at https://github.com/DUAL-Xiao/MolP2SBench. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

LLMs are increasingly used in molecular science, including molecular generation (Cai et al., 2026), molecular discovery (Ye et al., 2025), and structure elucidation (Guo et al., 2024; Su et al., 2025; Noh et al., 2026). In many of these settings, decisions depend on more than recognizing molec-ular motifs; they require relating physical observations, such as spectra, electronic properties, or molecular geometry, to competing structural hypotheses. Different property channels encode differ-ent structural information, and models may not exploit them equally well. Thus, a key question is not simply whether an LLM can produce the correct molecular structure, but whether its decision actually responds to the physical evidence provided. Existing evaluations (Liu et al., 2026; Bushuiev et al., 2024; Han et al., 2026) do not directly answer this question. Aggre-gate accuracy does not reveal which property channels are actually useful to the model, nor whether the model would change its prediction if the evidence changed; gains under property-conditioned input therefore do not by themselves establish evidence use (Feng et al., 2019). To systematically evaluate how well models use the supplied physical properties, we formulate property-to-structure prediction as a multiple-choice masked completion task (Figure 1). Each task presents a masked molecular scaffold with given properties, requiring the model to select the missing structure from plausible candidates. To evaluate how well models use the supplied properties, we apply three controlled intervention schemes. Removing them tests whether the supplied properties confer any benefit beyond the scaffold alone; shuffling them across tasks measures the model’s reliance on correct property-structure correspondence; and replacing them with counterfactual (CF) properties from an alternative candidate within the same family assesses the model’s responsiveness: whether its selection dynamically adapts to the newly supplied evidence (Gardner et al., 2020). Collectively, these comparisons address not merely whether a model is correct, but whether its decision tracks the physical observations it was given. Our results show that while LLMs can leverage physical properties for molecular structure predic-tion, their reliance on this data varies substantially across models and property types. Reliance and responsiveness also show different observed patterns across property channels. For example, Sol has a larger accuracy gain from correctly paired atomwise 1H NMR than from 13 C peaks, yet a lower conditional flip rate toward the corresponding CF candidate when the atomwise 1H properties are substituted. Interestingly, model performance does not consistently reflect the relative difficulty of these properties for human chemists. For instance, although UV–Vis spectra provide less di-rect structural mapping than NMR, models such as Astra achieve higher accuracy with UV–Vis data than with familiar NMR peaks. Beyond these interventions, combined-input analyses reveal model-specific gains and losses: extending the strongest property bundle to all properties improves recovery for Sol and Astra but reduces it for Luna and Opus. Furthermore, in paired NMR compar-isons, net accuracy gains often hide significant shifts in model behaviors, as higher-recovery models still fail on sizeable subsets of tasks successfully solved by weaker models. Case studies show that 2Preprint while Astra demonstrates a superior ability to utilize local chemical structures for NMR interpreta-tion, all evaluated models systematically err when global molecular dipoles contradict local polarity heuristics. In summary, whether a model selects the correct structure, whether its decision is genuinely grounded in the supplied evidence, and whether its stated justification is consistent with the evi-dence are distinct questions. MolP2SBench disentangles these dimensions through controlled inter-vention schemes and targeted case studies, exposing variations in property utilization that traditional accuracy metrics fail to reflect. Our contributions are fourfold: 

1. A channel-wise recovery leaderboard. We establish a leaderboard using Property-Grounded Recovery (PGR), which balances source recovery with recovery under candidate-targeted coun-terfactual property substitutions. By averaging counterfactual recovery within each family prior to cross-family aggregation, PGR ensures equal weighting regardless of varying candidate counts. Fur-thermore, reporting scores independently across property channels reveals model-specific strengths without collapsing heterogeneous properties into a single aggregate ranking. 

2. Disentangling reliance and responsiveness. We introduce two complementary metrics to char-acterize distinct capabilities in how models utilize supplied molecular properties. To measure static reliance, Reliance Margin (RM) quantifies the performance change when the correct property–task pairing is disrupted by shuffling. To evaluate dynamic responsiveness, Counterfactual Flip Rate (CFR) measures whether predictions follow targeted substitutions of the supplied property among cases that are initially solved correctly. The observed channel rankings differ between the two met-rics, motivating separate evaluation of reliance and responsiveness. 

3. Substitution-ready benchmark construction. We construct MolP2SBench with 136 distinct task families spanning 11 molecular-property channels, yielding 1,496 property-conditioned source tasks paired with matched shuffled controls and candidate-targeted counterfactual variants. Addi-tionally, we analyze within-family property separability and verify that our benchmark produces robust metric estimates and stable model rankings under substantial subsampling. 

4. Beyond aggregate accuracy. We show that recovery depends strongly on the supplied properties, and that a higher-recovery model does not simply solve a superset of a weaker model’s tasks: across paired comparisons, net gains span 11.0 to 32.4 pp while 40.4% to 45.6% of tasks change outcome in one direction or the other. Model explanations can also conflict with candidate-level physical values, with all four closed-weight models failing more often when molecular dipoles contradict a local carbonyl-based polarity heuristic.

## 3 BENCHMARK DESIGN 

MolP2SBench formulates property-to-structure prediction as a multiple-choice masked completion task to systematically evaluate how well models use the supplied physical properties. Each task’s input is carefully constructed around three core elements: (i) a molecular scaffold where property-critical fragments are masked; (ii) physical properties chosen for their high sensitivity to structural changes; (iii) a candidate family list containing a unique ground-truth completion. Through con-trolled intervention on the provided properties, this design explicitly isolates and evaluates two key dimensions of model capability beyond pure reconstruction: reliance on correctly paired properties; 

responsiveness to counterfactual properties. Figure 1 summarizes the model-visible inputs and the intervention scheme. 3.1 PROBLEM FORMULATION 

Masking isolates local structural inference from open-ended molecular generation. Reconstruct-ing an entire molecule usually combines evidence interpretation with structural search and output generation. Evaluating such outputs is also ambiguous when a generated structure differs from the reference but exhibits similar properties. We therefore mask property-critical fragments while preserving the remaining scaffold and boundary bond orders. This focuses the task on a localized structural difference within a fixed molecular context. 

Property Selection. We select physical properties from three groups that characterize different aspects of molecular structure: electronic properties comprising dipole magnitude, the HOMO– LUMO gap, and UV–Vis observations; vibrational properties comprising IR and Raman spectra; and NMR properties comprising 1H and 13 C observations. These groups characterize electronic structure, bond vibrations, and local nuclear environments, respectively. We obtain 11 property channels by evaluating each electronic and vibrational property separately and using three represen-tations for each NMR type: peaks-only, integration-augmented, and atomwise for 1H; peaks-only, degeneracy-augmented, and atomwise for 13 C. NMR inputs provide calculated shielding values for nuclei in the visible scaffold. Representation details appear in Appendix D. 

Candidate families make property interventions directly comparable. Each task provides two to four chemically valid options compatible with the scaffold and its attachment constraints. Con-struction combines three stages: (i) scaffold matching groups molecules with an identical canoni-cal scaffold after masking; (ii) family assembly selects structurally distinct completions, maintains molecule-disjoint tasks, and aligns their electronic, vibrational, and NMR properties; (iii) property-specific filtering excludes a family if any verifiable candidate pair falls below its channel-specific separation threshold. The final benchmark contains 136 task families and 394 distinct candidate structures. Its 11 prop-erty representations provide 1,496 source-property instances, matched shuffled-property controls, and 2,766 eligible within-family CF substitutions. Across 1,000 subsamples of 100 families, ag-gregate rankings for the diagnostic metrics RM and CFR are retained in 97.7–99.3% of draws (Ap-pendix G.5). Channel-wise PGR yields mean rank correlations of 0.940–0.999 and mean absolute score deviations of 0.91–1.34 pp (Appendix G.6). This organization supports controlled compar-isons of property reliance and responsiveness with fixed structural alternatives. Construction and coverage details appear in Appendix B. 3.2 TASK CONSTRUCTION

A positive RM indicates that the correct condition-target alignment explicitly improves selection accuracy (reported in percentage points in Table 3(a)). 

Counterfactual Flip Rate (CFR) measures the model’s responsiveness : how consistently a model flips to the corresponding CF candidate after property substitution, conditioned on a correct initial prediction. We compute the proportion of successful flips across eligible CF substitutions for these qualifying tasks, then average the results. CFR specifically quantifies responsiveness within each evaluated cohort. Table 3(b) reports this metric as a percentage. We align molecular records from QM9 (Ramakrishnan et al., 2014), QM9-NMR (Gupta et al., 2021), and QM9S (Zou et al., 2023). QM9 supplies the HOMO–LUMO gap, QM9-NMR supplies gas-phase NMR shieldings, and QM9S supplies dipole, IR, Raman, and UV–Vis observations. Appen-dices B and D document data alignment, observation scopes, units, and preprocessing. Appendix C explains the theoretical background of the properties. Reliance and responsiveness show different observed property rankings. Recall that RM quan-tifies a model’s static reliance on correct property-structure pairing, whereas CFR measures its dy-namic responsiveness to counterfactual evidence. Luna, Sol, and Astra all show a higher RM for atomwise 1H than for 13 C peaks, but a lower CFR. For Sol, the RM values are 25.2 versus 12.6 pp, whereas the CFR values are 53.6% versus 67.3%. CFR is evaluated on each view’s initially correct source cohort. This observed reversal motivates reporting reliance and responsiveness separately: RM measures the benefit of correct property pairing, whereas CFR measures responses to targeted property substitutions. Disentangling these dimensions may inform downstream applications, such as human-in-the-loop molecular design, where models must adapt to updated experimental feed-back. 

LLMs can perform better with properties that are less intuitive to chemists. NMR offers fa-miliar links between spectral features and local chemical environments, whereas UV–Vis provides less direct structural information. Nevertheless, Astra achieves 80.1% source recovery with UV– Vis, compared with 69.9% and 66.2% with 1H and 13 C NMR peaks, respectively. Sol shows the same ordering. These observed results highlight UV–Vis as a useful input for molecular selection alongside the more familiar NMR representations. In summary, these results establish property-dependent selection, while leaving the validity of the supporting chemical argument open. After examining combined-property inputs and the interpre-tation of model comparisons, we use case studies to examine how models connect the supplied observations to candidate-specific environments and geometry. 4.3 COMBINED PROPERTY ANALYSIS 

We compare single properties, related-property bundles, and all-properties input while keeping con-stituent values and candidate order matched (Appendix H). 

Combining properties does not always improve recovery. Within the GPT family, Sol and Astra improve over the strongest constituent in all three bundles, with larger gains for Astra; for example, electronic recovery rises from 72.8% to 73.5% for Sol and from 80.1% to 88.2% for Astra. Luna instead improves only with IR and Raman (81.6% to 83.8%), while electronic and NMR recovery fall from 65.4% to 61.8% and from 49.3% to 44.1%, respectively. Opus also benefits from electronic and vibrational combinations, but its NMR recovery drops from 66.2% to 55.1%. Thus, combining related channels can help, but does not consistently outperform the strongest individual input. 

A successful within-bundle combination does not guarantee a successful cross-bundle com-bination. Among closed-weight models, adding the remaining properties to the strongest bundle (vibrational for all four) improves Sol from 89.7% to 93.4% and Astra from 96.3% to 98.5%, but reduces Luna from 83.8% to 78.7% and Opus from 90.4% to 89.0%. Thus, Luna and Opus ben-efit from combining IR and Raman but not from extending that bundle to all properties. What Astra’s Advantage Reveals about Property Interpretation and Molecular Structure Un-derstanding. Task 97CCF0B132ED0CC7 (Appendix J) contrasts the ether CO[C@@H](C)C(C)C 

with the ester COC(=O)[C@@H](C)C(C)C , whose property values are supplied. The retained methine carbon is directly oxygen-bound in the ether but adjacent to the carbonyl carbon in the ester. Given 13 C shieldings of 134.3052–168.7333 ppm, Astra selects the ester, arguing that the ether’s oxygen-bound methine should be more deshielded and explicitly noting that the masked car-bonyl carbon is not observed. Luna and Opus select the ether, invoking the absence or expected contribution of a carbonyl signal; Sol also selects the ether, assigning the two lowest shieldings to two oxygen-bound retained carbons. The distinction therefore requires connecting candidate-specific connectivity to the actual observation scope, not merely recognizing that an ester contains a carbonyl. When the property input is replaced by the ether’s scaffold shieldings, including 101.2638 and 130.2122 ppm, Astra switches to the ether and attributes the stronger deshielding to its oxygen-bound methine and methoxy carbons. Only Astra selects the property-providing candidate in both conditions: Luna reverses the two assignments, while Sol and Opus select the ether throughout. 

Models make more errors when property values conflict with local heuristics. For task 49E99A99315E5E96 (Appendix I.3), the carbonyl-containing target has a dipole of 1.45724 D, be-low the reduced candidate’s 5.37590 D. All four models select the reduced candidate under dipole-magnitude input; Astra argues that the additional carbonyl is less consistent with the low magnitude. The reference values contradict this local polarity heuristic. Across 35 within-task matched car-bonyl/reduced pairs, ten similarly reverse the expectation that adding a carbonyl increases molecular dipole. Astra makes errors on 7/10 reversal tasks versus 5/25 nonreversal tasks; Luna, Sol, and Opus also have higher error rates in the reversal subset (Appendix I.3). These post-hoc contrasts identify a limitation of the heuristic, not geometry as its isolated cause: the substitutions change electronic structure and conformation together. 

## 5 CONCLUSION AND LIMITATION 

MolP2SBench establishes a controlled framework for evaluating property-to-structure reasoning in LLMs beyond answer accuracy. Across seven models, reliance and responsiveness rank property channels differently, additional properties can reduce recovery, and case studies reveal physical in-consistencies in model explanations. By combining curated completion tasks, a channel-wise recov-ery leaderboard, and matched property interventions, MolP2SBench provides the community with a reusable testbed for diagnosing evidence use and evaluating future training strategies toward more reliable property-grounded molecular inference. 9Preprint 

Limitation. MolP2SBench covers small-molecule completion with computed properties and cu-rated candidates; experimental spectra and open-ended structure elucidation remain outside its scope. The current benchmark’s coverage is constrained by the effort required for careful dataset construction and manual validation. Future work could expand molecular families and property channels through LLM-assisted dataset construction while retaining expert review and validation.

## A RELATED WORK 

Table 1 compares the benchmarks discussed below. 

Molecular reasoning and structure elucidation. FGBench evaluates functional-group property reasoning (Liu et al., 2026); ChemCoTBench and MolLangBench test molecular operations (Hao et al., 2026; Cai et al., 2026); ChemIQ examines structural reasoning and NMR elucidation (Runcie et al., 2026). Spectrum-to-structure studies address generation, retrieval, and interactive property acquisition (Guo et al., 2024; Su et al., 2025; Bushuiev et al., 2024; Han et al., 2026; Xiang et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2026). MolP2SBench fixes a masked scaffold and complete candidate molecules, then substitutes aligned physical observations to test whether decisions follow the evidence. 

Property-conditioned reconstruction. Masked graph modeling reconstructs molecular graphs under property conditions (Mahmood et al., 2021); C5T5 reconstructs corrupted molecular names conditioned on properties (Rothchild et al., 2021); and the Regression Transformer combines prop-erty prediction with conditioned generation (Born & Manica, 2023). These approaches develop models through conditional reconstruction. We instead use local completion to evaluate general-purpose language models, constructing candidate-aligned observations that enable controlled prop-erty substitutions. Our contribution lies in the benchmark formulation and matched evaluation de-sign, rather than masking itself. 

Diagnostic evaluation and verification. MolecularIQ uses graph-derived symbolic verifica-tion (Bartmann et al., 2026); consistency studies and ChemDIRT vary molecular representations or equivalent instructions (Yan et al., 2025; Inae et al., 2026); and ChemCoTBench V2 evaluates intermediate chemical states (Guo et al., 2026). MolP2SBench changes the supplied property values while preserving the structural hypotheses: an appropriate response may therefore require chang-ing the answer. We connect these behavioral comparisons to candidate-level checks of physical compatibility and stated explanations, separating correct selection from the validity of the argument supporting it.

## D PROPERTY REPRESENTATIONS 

The property information supplied to a model depends on both the computed source and its con-version into the prompt. The following descriptions specify the reported representations and their preprocessing (Appendix B). 

Electronic gap. We use the QM9 HOMO–LUMO gap in Hartree. QM9 reports properties calcu-lated at the B3LYP/6-31G(2df,p) level (Ramakrishnan et al., 2014). This scalar is used as property information for choosing a completion, not as a regression target for the evaluated language model. 

Dipole. QM9S provides dipole information in Debye. The norm representation contains its scalar magnitude; the vector representation contains its three Cartesian components. A vector depends on the coordinate frame in which it is represented. We treat norm and vector as different property representations. 

NMR. The NMR channel uses gas-phase isotropic shielding from QM9NMR, calculated at the mPW1PW91/6-311+G(2d,p) level (Gupta et al., 2021). Values are shielding constants in ppm, not experimentally referenced chemical shifts. Only nuclei belonging to the retained scaffold are ex-posed. Symmetry classes are determined in the completed molecule, so a hidden-group change can alter both a retained nucleus’s shielding and its equivalence class. The peaks-only view re-ports class-averaged shieldings. The views labeled integration ( 1H) or degeneracy ( 13 C) also report retained-nucleus counts for these equivalence classes. These are computed counts, not measured peak areas or spin–spin splitting multiplicities. The raw view contains one value per retained nucleus without internal atom identifiers. These views probe representation sensitivity without changing the molecular candidate set. 

IR and Raman. The spectra are derived from QM9S calculations at the B3LYP/def-TZVP level (Zou et al., 2023). The supplied property information is a compact peak list from the broad-ened spectrum rather than a raster plot or its full 3,501-point grid. Wavenumbers are in cm −1 and intensities are relative. Peak extraction uses relative-prominence thresholds of 0.02 for IR and 0.03 for Raman. 

UV–Vis. The UV–Vis view uses the first ten QM9S TDDFT transitions, exposing excitation energy in eV, wavelength in nm, and transition-dipole norm in Debye. 

Combined properties. The all-properties condition concatenates the seven canonical channels: dipole norm, gap, 1H NMR peaks with retained-nucleus counts, 13 C NMR peaks with degeneracy, IR, Raman, and UV–Vis. 19 Preprint D.1 WITHIN -F AMILY PROPERTY SEPARABILITY

NMR: restricted observations and errors in interpreting available property information. The task references below link to their source molecules, candidate lists, and scaffold drawings in Ap-27 Preprint pendix K. Across several models, the supplied NMR representations yield lower source recovery than other informative property channels. For example, Astra obtains 69.9% with 1H peaks and 66.2% with 13 C peaks, compared with 80.1% with UV–Vis; Sol obtains 54.4% for both NMR views, compared with 72.8% with UV–Vis. This ordering is not universal: Opus performs better with 13 Cpeaks than with UV–Vis, and Astra’s 1H result is similar to its HOMO–LUMO-gap result. The rele-vant question is therefore why the particular NMR property information supplied by this benchmark leaves substantial room for error, rather than whether NMR is intrinsically less informative than other molecular properties. An important feature of the task is that the NMR observation is restricted to nuclei retained on the visible scaffold (Section 4.5). Nuclei within the masked group, including its attached hydrogens, are excluded. The model consequently has to infer a completion from its effect on the shielding of the remaining nuclei, without direct access to that group’s own NMR signals. This restriction differs from the whole-molecule scope of the other property channels. For example, if a carbonyl carbon belongs to the masked group, its own 13 C shielding is unavailable even when the candi-date structure contains a carbonyl. The observation also consists of calculated gas-phase isotropic shieldings (Gupta et al., 2021), with no experimental multiplet patterns, J-couplings, or atom as-signments. Applying a familiar whole-spectrum recognition strategy therefore requires care: the model must determine which expected signals are actually within the supplied scope. Removing hidden-group nuclei eliminates potential discriminative cues, but it does not necessarily eliminate all information about the completion. A concrete example of a difficult residual distinction is Task 79CD04C3DFC1C559. 1 The retained scaffold is [*:1]C(=O)NC1CCC1 , and the candi-dates are a methyl carbamate, COC(=O)NC1CCC1 , and a urea, NC(=O)NC1CCC1 . The methoxy carbon of the carbamate lies outside the observed scaffold. The remaining 13 C peak lists are numer-ically close: the retained-carbonyl shieldings are 28.2095 and 28.0280 ppm, and the Wasserstein-1

(W1) distance between the two scaffold distributions is only 0.3562 ppm. All four closed models select the urea when the property information comes from the carbamate. Astra invokes a more deshielded urea carbonyl, while other explanations also rely on general carbonyl-environment argu-ments. These responses illustrate how broad chemical trends can fail to resolve a fine quantitative distinction after direct substituent signals have been removed. The two computed reference distri-butions are different. At the dataset level, candidate separation is associated with model performance. When tasks are grouped using the final cohort’s one-third and two-thirds quantiles of the nearest-alternative W1 dis-tance (lower includes the first cutoff; middle includes the second), Astra’s 1H accuracy increases from 47.8% to 75.6% to 86.7%; its 13 C accuracy increases from 34.8% to 73.3% to 91.1%. More-over, all 136 targets have distinct reference distributions at zero tolerance in each NMR representa-tion.
