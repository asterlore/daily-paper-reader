Abstract 

The growing sophistication of drug discovery calls for new advances in artificial intelligence (AI). Transformer architecture has underpinned many of the recent breakthroughs in AI, yet its conventional source-target attention mechanism can become a bottleneck for modeling complex interactions. Here, we introduce TopoLIA, a topology-inspired latent interaction attention mechanism that learns not only how much information should be exchanged between interaction partners, but also how that information should be transformed. Source-target-dependent attention is mediated through a compact latent interaction space, providing a structured representation of complex interactions. For complex data, we further incorporate topological spectral representations to capture multiscale data geometry and extract algebraic relationships. TopoLIA thus incorporates topological principles into both data representation and neural network architecture. We demonstrate TopoLIA on protein–ligand binding, where persistent spectra encode multiscale topological and geometric patterns in three-dimensional (3D) protein–ligand complexes, while latent interaction attention (LIA) enables structured, source-target-dependent information exchange. We pretrain TopoLIA by reconstructing masked multiscale spectral tokens from unlabeled molecular structures, yielding transferable structural representations without task-specific annotations. Across multiple scoring and docking benchmarks, TopoLIA achieves state-of-the-art performance and consistently outperforms its standard-attention counterpart. Systematic rank analyses further show that compact latent dimensions yield the best performance, supporting compact interaction spaces as an effective inductive bias for biomolecular learning. More broadly, LIA provides a general framework for structured, source-target-dependent information exchange that is applicable to diverse scientific and AI problems involving complex heterogeneous interactions.

1arXiv:2610.13006v1 [q-bio.BM] 9 Oct 2026 1 Introduction 

Accelerating drug discovery remains a major challenge in modern healthcare, yet the transition from drug candidate identification to clinical approval often requires more than a decade and billions of dollars [1]. The limitations of traditional methodologies, including molecular docking, free energy perturbation, and empirical modeling, have motivated the development of deep learning approaches [2–4]. However, conventional deep learning architectures may struggle to represent the non-Euclidean, irregular, and multiscale geometry of 3D protein–ligand complexes. Although transformer models have established a powerful paradigm for representation learning [5–7], standard architectures do not explicitly encode the high-order geometric relationships present in biomolecular systems. More fundamentally, standard attention primarily captures source–target dependencies through scalar attention weights, while the transformed value of each source remains independent of the specific target. To better capture the structural complexity of biomolecular systems, advanced mathematical frame-works have increasingly been explored in biomolecular machine learning [8–12]. Persistent homology (PH) [13, 14] has successfully reduced complex biological data to robust topological signatures [15]. Persistent spectral theory (PST) [16] extends PH beyond topological invariants by incorporating non-harmonic spectra that capture subtle geometric and shape-dependent variations across multiple scales [17]. These topological representations further motivated the development of topological deep learning (TDL) in 2017 [18], establishing topology-aware learning as an increasingly important direction in molecular science [19]. However, most existing TDL models use topology primarily for feature extraction rather than incorporating it directly into neural architectures. More recently, topological principles have begun to directly shape neural network architectures [20]. Copresheaf topological neural networks (CTNNs) [21, 22] provide one such example. Rather than assuming a single global latent space, CTNNs associate local regions with their own feature spaces and mediate information exchange between them through learnable copresheaf structure maps. This perspective suggests that information exchange should involve not only how much information is passed, but also how that information is transformed across different representations. However, directly implementing such interaction-dependent transformations in a high-dimensional feature space can be computationally expensive and may introduce unnecessary flexibility. We therefore hypothesize that biomolecular information exchange can be effectively mediated through a much lower-dimensional latent interaction space than the original feature space. Inspired by this perspective, we introduce latent interaction attention (LIA), a structured attention mechanism in which information exchange is mediated through a learned low-dimensional interaction space. In standard attention, pairwise relationships primarily determine how much information is exchanged from each source through scalar attention coefficients, while the same source representation is shared across different targets. LIA additionally learns how the source value should be transformed for different targets during information exchange. Specifically, each source representation is first projected into a compact latent interaction space through a source-dependent transformation, and the resulting latent representation is then mapped to the target space through a target-dependent transformation. Their composition thus defines a structured source-to-target transformation through the latent interaction space, enabling target-dependent information transformation without explicitly parameterizing a separate high-dimensional mapping for every source–target pair. This low-dimensional bottleneck provides a structured inductive bias for heterogeneous biomolecular interactions while controlling the computational cost of interaction-dependent transformations. Building on LIA, we develop TopoLIA by integrating latent interaction attention with persistent spectral representations and self-supervised pretraining for learning three-dimensional protein–ligand interactions. Persistent spectra encode the multiscale topological and geometric organization of protein– ligand complexes, while LIA enables these heterogeneous structural representations to exchange information through compact latent interaction spaces using source- and target-dependent projections. To exploit large collections of molecular structures without requiring task-specific annotations, we further employ a self-supervised masked pretraining strategy [6, 7, 23]. The model reconstructs masked multiscale spectral tokens from their molecular context, encouraging it to learn dependencies among atomic composition, spatial organization, topological cavities, and non-harmonic geometric variations. The resulting structural 2representations can subsequently be transferred to downstream molecular tasks. Across multiple protein– ligand benchmarks, TopoLIA achieves state-of-the-art performance in both scoring and docking tasks and consistently outperforms its standard-attention counterpart. Systematic rank analyses further support the low-dimensional interaction hypothesis, with compact latent dimensions consistently yielding the best performance.

TopoLIA integrates multiscale persistent spectral representations, topology-inspired latent interaction attention, and self-supervised pretraining for protein–ligand interaction learning (Fig. 1). At the repre-sentation level, persistent spectral embeddings encode multiscale topological and geometric information from three-dimensional protein–ligand complexes and serve as structural tokens for masked self-supervised pretraining. During pretraining, a subset of these tokens is masked and reconstructed through an encoder-decoder architecture, encouraging the model to capture dependencies across complementary structural 3representations (Fig. 1a). The pretrained encoder is subsequently transferred to downstream molecular tasks through fine-tuning, with its representations aggregated by pooling and mapped to task-specific outputs through a multilayer perceptron (Fig. 1b). At the neural architecture level, both the encoder and decoder are built from latent interaction transformer (LIT) layers, which preserve the overall transformer block structure while replacing standard self-attention with latent interaction attention (LIA) (Figure 1c). LIA separates two complementary aspects of information exchange: how much information should be exchanged and how that information should be transformed (Figure 1d). The attention weight determines how much information is exchanged, whereas the source-target-dependent transformation determines how information from a source is adapted to a target. In standard attention, a source-specific value representation is typically shared across targets, with source-target dependence entering primarily through scalar attention weights. LIA additionally makes the transformation of the transported information dependent on both its source and target through a compact latent interaction space. For biomolecular interactions, this compact latent interaction space reflects the hypothesis that effective biomolecular information exchange can be mediated through a substantially lower-dimensional interaction space than the full feature space. 

# 2.2 Datasets 

TopoLIA was evaluated on multiple benchmarks from PDBbind database [24]. For self-supervised pre-training, we curated 19,462 non-overlapping, unlabeled protein–ligand complexes from PDBbind v.2007, v.2013, v.2016, and v.2020. For scoring power, TopoLIA was evaluated on CASF-2007, CASF-2013, and CASF-2016 core sets, with the corresponding refined sets as training after excluding core-set samples [25–27]. Docking power was evaluated on the CASF-2013 of 195 ligands, with additional GOLD-generated poses used for training[10]. Generalization over sequence similarity was assessed on the Atom3D-curated LBA30 and LBA60 datasets [28]. Ten-fold cross-validation was performed on the PDBbind v.2020 refined set. These datasets and evaluation protocols are summarized in Table S1, with additional details provided in Section S1 of the Supplementary Information. 

# 2.3 Scoring Power on CASF Benchmarks 

For scoring power evaluation, TopoLIA was quantified through the Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE). To ensure the statistical robustness of the scoring results, we performed ten independent runs using different random seeds. The final metrics reported for each benchmark are based on the average predictions across these ten iterations. While binding affinities in PDBbind are provided in units of pK d, predicted values were multiplied by a factor of −1.36 for conversion to kcal/mol. TopoLIA demonstrates robust scoring performance across successive PDBbind datasets of increasing complexity. Overall, it achieves an industry-leading average performance across all evaluated datasets (Fig-ure 5f), with a comprehensive quantitative comparison against representative state-of-the-art approaches provided in Table S4. On the CASF-2007 benchmark, TopoLIA was trained on a refined set of 1,105 complexes and evaluated on the standard 195-complex test set. It achieved a competitive PCC of 0.832, matching the leading methods presented in Figure S1a and showing a strong linear correlation between experimental data and predicted affinities in Figure S1b. The scoring power was further validated using the CASF-2013 benchmark. Trained on a refined set of 2,764 complexes strictly excluding the 195 CASF-2013 core set entries, TopoLIA achieved a PCC of 0.814 and an RMSE of 1.908 kcal/mol. This comparative advantage is visualized in Figure S1c, with the correlation between experimental and predicted values in Figure S1d. # 2.4 Robustness and Generalization in Affinity Prediction 

While CASF benchmarks provide a standard metric for scoring power, practical drug discovery often involves evaluating targets with low sequence similarity to known data. To evaluate model generalization under such conditions, we conducted experiments on the LBA30 and LBA60 datasets [28], where training and test proteins are separated under strict sequence-identity thresholds. This partitioning strategy evaluates the model’s true generalization ability across unseen protein families. We compared TopoLIA with 12 representative baseline models spanning sequence-based, structure-based, and pre-trained approaches. As shown in Figure 5d, Figure 5e, and Table S6, TopoLIA achieves the best overall performance across both sequence-identity splits. On the highly challenging LBA30 dataset, TopoLIA secures a leading PCC of 0.641 and an RMSE of 1.307 pK d, outperforming recent structure-based methods like BindNet [46]. On LBA60, TopoLIA significantly outperforms the current state-of-the-art method SableBind, achieving a PCC of 0.806 and lowering the RMSE to 1.220 pK d. These results demonstrate that the topological signatures learned by TopoLIA support robust affinity prediction and enable effective generalization across diverse protein families. Beyond sequence identity evaluations, we further assessed the robustness and performance consistency of TopoLIA using 10-fold cross-validation on the PDBbind v.2020 refined set [24]. As one of the most comprehensive and up-to-date collections of high-quality protein–ligand structures, this dataset poses a substantial predictive challenge due to its expanded structural diversity. The cross-validation setting mitigates sampling biases and ensures a robust evaluation across diverse data partitions. TopoLIA achieved a PCC of 0.811 and an RMSE of 1.152 kcal/mol (Figure 5g). In comparison, recent graph-based models such as PLAIG [50] and GNNSeq [51] yielded considerably lower PCC scores of 0.763 and 0.784, accompanied by RMSEs of 1.251 kcal/mol and 1.336 kcal/mol, respectively. These results confirm the robustness of TopoLIA on large and structurally diverse protein–ligand datasets. 

# 2.5 Docking Power Evaluation 

The capacity of a scoring function (SF) to distinguish native binding poses from non-native decoys is a critical metric for molecular docking. We evaluated the docking proficiency of TopoLIA on the CASF-2013 benchmark using the standard protocol where a prediction is considered successful if the pose with the highest predicted binding affinity falls within a 2.0 Å root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) threshold of the experimentally determined native structure. To ensure statistical robustness, the final success rates are derived from the median outputs of a multi-model ensemble, including ten independent TopoLIA runs, forty GBT models utilizing Euclidean distance-based persistent spectra features, and twenty GBT models utilizing electrostatic distance-based persistent spectra features. Recent deep learning models have improved docking performance [52], with representative methods including DeepDock (62.1%) [53], OnionNet-SFCT (76.8%) [54], and RTMScore (80.7%) [55]. On CASF-2013, TopoLIA achieved a superior docking success rate of 88%. 72.6 Ablation Study on Latent Interaction Attention 

To isolate the contribution of latent interaction attention (LIA), we constructed a baseline model, denoted TopoSA, that retains the same persistent spectral representations and overall transformer architecture as TopoLIA, while replacing LIA with standard self-attention [5]. This controlled comparison allows us to directly assess whether introducing source-target-dependent feature transformation beyond the scalar weighting of standard attention improves protein–ligand interaction modeling. We compared TopoLIA with TopoSA on the CASF-2007, CASF-2013, and CASF-2016 benchmarks, as shown in the radial bar chart of Figure 3a. Ten independent runs with different random seeds were performed and the final PCC and RMSE were computed from the averaged predictions of the ten models. To provide a unified geometric representation in polar coordinates, we report PCC together with inverse RMSE, such that outward radial expansion consistently indicates improved predictive performance. Across all evaluation axes, the TopoLIA profile (light blue) consistently extends beyond that of the TopoSA baseline (dark green). Quantitatively, TopoLIA achieves consistent improvements across all three benchmarks. Specifically, TopoLIA improves PCC from 0.754 to 0.817 and reduces RMSE from 2.153 kcal/mol to 1.908 kcal/mol on CASF-2007, improves PCC from 0.724 to 0.798 and reduces RMSE from 2.144 kcal/mol to 1.931 kcal/mol on CASF-2013, and improves PCC from 0.819 to 0.857 and reduces RMSE from 1.712 kcal/mol to 1.597 kcal/mol on CASF-2016. These consistent gains across all three benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of latent interaction attention relative to standard attention. Because the two models share the same persistent spectral representations and surrounding transformer architecture, the observed performance differences isolate the contribution of the attention mechanism. While standard self-attention effectively identifies relevant source information through scalar attention weights, it does not explicitly adapt the transformation of that information to different targets. The consistent advantage of TopoLIA therefore demonstrates the benefit of source-target-dependent feature transformation for modeling heterogeneous protein–ligand interactions, supporting the central design principle of LIA that effective information exchange involves not only determining how much information to communicate, but also how that information should be transformed. 

# 2.7 Ablation Study on the Dimensionality of the Latent Interaction Space 

To investigate how the capacity of the latent interaction space affects source-target information trans-formation in LIA, we systematically varied the dimension of the latent interaction space over r ∈{2, 4, 8, 16 , 32 , 64 }. Ten independent training runs with different random seeds were performed for each latent dimension, and the final PCC and RMSE were computed from the averaged predictions of the ten models. This experiment directly tests our hypothesis that effective protein–ligand information exchange can be mediated through a substantially lower-dimensional interaction space than the full feature space. The results on CASF-2007 reveal a clear dependence of predictive performance on the dimensionality of the latent interaction space. As shown in the top panel of Figure 3c, TopoLIA achieves its strongest generalization within a compact range of r ∈ [4 , 8], with the complete numerical results and computational costs summarized in Table S9. At r = 4, the model achieves a PCC of 0.808 and an RMSE of 1.959 kcal/mol, while r = 8 yields a nearly identical PCC of 0.808 and an RMSE of 1.970 kcal/mol. Beyond this range, increasing the latent dimension does not improve predictive performance. Instead, performance progressively declines, reaching a PCC of 0.765 and an RMSE of 2.133 kcal/mol at r = 64. These results indicate that a larger interaction space is not necessarily more expressive in a useful sense and that protein–ligand interactions can be effectively represented through a relatively low-dimensional latent space. The computational analysis further demonstrates an additional practical advantage of maintaining a compact latent interaction space. As shown in the bottom panel of Figure 3c, computational cost increases with the latent dimension, and an Out-of-Memory failure occurs at r = 111 on a 32 GB NVIDIA Tesla V100S GPU under the experimental configuration used here. Thus, increasing the dimensionality of the interaction space not only fails to improve predictive accuracy beyond the optimal range, but also introduces additional computational and memory demands. Taken together, these results provide empirical support for the low-dimensional latent interaction 8hypothesis underlying LIA. The optimal performance at intermediate dimensions suggests that effective source-target biomolecular information exchange does not require transformations in the full feature space. Instead, a compact latent interaction space appears sufficient to capture the interaction-relevant information exchanged between molecular features. Conversely, excessively large latent dimensions introduce additional degrees of freedom without corresponding predictive gains and may reduce generalization. The existence of an optimal intermediate dimension therefore suggests that the dimensionality of the latent interaction space is not merely a computational hyperparameter, but an important architectural factor governing how effectively LIA models protein–ligand interactions. 

# 2.8 Ablation Study on the Impact of Pretraining 

To assess the efficacy of self-supervised pretraining, we performed a systematic ablation analysis evaluating the downstream performance across a spectrum of pretraining data scales. The latent interaction transformer encoder was pretrained using progressively larger subsets of unlabeled protein–ligand complexes and then fine-tuned on downstream benchmarks. This analysis evaluates both the empirical utility of pretraining and the dependence of downstream performance on pretraining data scale. The hyperparameters utilized for pretraining and fine-tuning are detailed in Table S2. Empirical results across three benchmarks demonstrate that pretraining consistently improved down-stream performance. As shown in Figure 3b, we varied the number of the unlabeled pretraining samples, from 0 (random initialization) to 1k, 5k, 10k, and the complete set of 19.5k samples, with full quantitative results summarized in Table S7. The resulting trajectories reveal a clear law: expanding the pretraining data size improves downstream performance. Notably, using 5,000 unlabeled complexes already leads to a substantial improvement over random initialization, while further increasing the data size continues to enhance performance without obvious saturation. On CASF-2016, leveraging the full pretraining regime elevated the PCC from 0.805 to 0.857, while simultaneously reducing the RMSE from 1.767 to 1.597 kcal/mol. Figure S2 visually corroborates this advantage, demonstrating a markedly tighter linear fit for the pretrained model compared to the random baseline. To further validate the expressive power of the learned topological representations, we evaluated a linear probing configuration. By completely freezing the pretrained encoder and exclusively training a minimalist linear projection head (with a fine-tuning learning rate of 8 × 10 −4), the model still delivered strong predictive performance. It achieved a PCC of 0.613 (RMSE: 2.555 kcal/mol) on CASF-2007 and a PCC of 0.650 (RMSE: 2.356 kcal/mol) on CASF-2013. Particularly on the CASF-2016 benchmark, the model achieved a PCC of 0.676 (RMSE: 2.205 kcal/mol), with the robust linear correlation between experimental and predicted binding affinities explicitly visualized in Figure 3e. These results provide concrete evidence that our self-supervised pretraining phase successfully maps fundamental geometric and topological inputs into rich, highly informative structural representations, yielding robust predictive utility even in the absence of deep, task-specific adaptation. 

# 3 Discussion 

In this study, we introduced TopoLIA, centered on a new latent interaction attention (LIA) mechanism for modeling heterogeneous biomolecular information exchange. Conventional attention primarily determines how much information should be exchanged through scalar attention weights, while the transformation of a source value is shared across different targets. LIA extends this paradigm by additionally learning how source information should be transformed for a particular target. We integrate this interaction-dependent attention mechanism with multiscale persistent spectral representations of three-dimensional protein–ligand complexes, resulting in a framework in which topological structure informs both molecular representation and neural information exchange. The comparison with standard self-attention provides direct evidence for the contribution of LIA. Under the same configurations, including the same persistent spectral representations and surrounding transformer architecture, replacing LIA with standard self-attention consistently reduces predictive performance across the evaluated protein–ligand benchmarks. This controlled comparison isolates the 9contribution of the attention mechanism and supports the central design principle of LIA: effective information exchange involves not only determining how much information should be communicated, but also how that information should be transformed for a particular interaction. The latent-dimension analysis further reveals an important property of this interaction mechanism. TopoLIA achieves its strongest generalization within a compact intermediate latent dimension, with optimal performance observed around r = 4–8, whereas increasing the interaction dimension beyond this range provides no additional predictive benefit and instead reduces generalization performance. These results provide empirical support for our hypothesis that interaction-relevant information can be effectively exchanged through a substantially lower-dimensional space than the full feature space. Importantly, this does not imply that protein–ligand interactions themselves are intrinsically low-dimensional. Rather, it suggests that effective source-target communication within LIA can be mediated through a compact latent interaction space. The existence of an optimal compact dimension further suggests that the dimensionality of the latent interaction space plays an important role in governing the effectiveness of LIA, rather than serving merely as a computational design choice. The associated reductions in computational and memory costs provide an additional practical advantage. Persistent spectral representation and self-supervised pretraining provide complementary contributions at the structural representation level. The persistent spectral embeddings encode multiscale topological and geometric characteristics of protein–ligand complexes and serve as structural tokens for masked self-supervised learning. By reconstructing masked spectral tokens from their molecular context, the model learns transferable multiscale structural dependencies before exposure to downstream task labels. The consistent improvement in downstream performance with increasing pretraining scale, together with successful transfer across scoring and docking tasks, demonstrates that TopoLIA benefits from larger collections of unlabeled molecular structures. These results further indicate that persistent spectral tokens provide transferable structural information and suggest that continued scaling to larger collections of experimentally determined and computationally predicted structures may further improve generalization. The framework also suggests several directions for extension. Because LIA is not specific to protein– ligand systems, the same interaction-dependent transformation principle may be applicable to other heterogeneous biomolecular interactions, including protein–protein, protein–peptide, protein–nucleic acid, and mutation-induced interaction modeling. At the representation level, richer mathematical constructions, including persistent path Laplacians [56], persistent hyperdigraph Laplacians [57], and persistent Dirac operators [58, 59], may provide additional sensitivity to directed, multi-body, and higher-order molecular interactions. At the same time, our error analysis in Figure 3d and Section S4.2 highlights an important limitation of the current molecular representation. The present formulation primarily models the protein and ligand and therefore captures environmental factors only indirectly. Complexes whose binding behavior is strongly influenced by structural water molecules, metal ions, cofactors, or other components of the local chemical environment appear to remain more challenging. Explicitly incorporating such environmental components into the molecular representation and topological filtration process may improve both biological fidelity and predictive accuracy. Overall, TopoLIA suggests that topology-inspired learning can extend beyond structural representation to the design of neural information exchange. By combining multiscale persistent spectral representations with interaction-dependent feature transformations, TopoLIA demonstrates how mathematical structure can inform both what molecular information is represented and how that information is exchanged within a neural network. More broadly, LIA provides a principled mechanism for modeling heterogeneous interactions through source-target-dependent feature transformation and can be directly applied to all other large language models (LLMs) involving heterogeneous components.

# 4.3 Multiscale Topological Spectral Characterization 

As demonstrated by the empirical evaluations in Section S4.1, capturing a holistic representation of the protein–ligand interface requires the synergistic integration of both structural shapes and charge-based environments. To mathematically formalize the geometric–electrostatic dual topological deep learning (GE-TopoLIA) ensemble, we detail the construction of its two specialized constituent modules: G-TopoLIA and E-Phys. To achieve a granular and physically meaningful representation of the chemical environment, both modules employ an element-specific (ES) topological model. This approach partitions the molecular 14 complex into distinct atom sets based on their respective atomic species, allowing the network to distinguish between varying chemical identities. Specifically, the protein structure is decomposed into four primary atom sets representing Carbon (C), Nitrogen (N), Oxygen (O), and Sulfur (S). The ligand structure is similarly partitioned into five sets, encompassing Carbon (C), Nitrogen (N), Oxygen (O), Sulfur (S), and Phosphorus (P), resulting in 20 = 4 × 5 potential element-specific interaction combinations for a given complex. Let Ep and El denote the sets of 3D Cartesian coordinates for these specific atom types in the protein and ligand, respectively. By focusing exclusively on the bipartite interactions between a set from 

Ep and a set from El, the model isolates the critical cross-boundary relationships that govern binding affinity. This element-specific decomposition results in a series of distinct bipartite graphs, ensuring that the subsequent persistent spectral analysis captures the unique physical and chemical contributions of each atomic pairing. The primary structural backbone of our framework, the G-TopoLIA module, focuses on capturing the intrinsic geometric shape and complementarity of the binding pocket. Spatial relationships at the interface are formally defined by an interactive Euclidean distance matrix D. To ensure the model strictly emphasizes the protein–ligand interface, we assign an infinite value to distances between atoms within the same molecule, effectively ignoring protein–protein or ligand–ligand interactions. We define the binding core region by considering protein atoms within 10 Å of any ligand atom. The interactive Euclidean distance matrix is formulated as follows:

To rigorously evaluate the predictive and generative capabilities of the proposed TopoLIA model, we utilized benchmarks sourced from the PDBbind database [24]. PDBbind serves as a curated, gold-standard resource that systematically collects experimentally determined 3D structures of protein–ligand complexes alongside their corresponding binding affinity data, providing the essential structural foundation for our pre-training and downstream scoring and docking tasks. The dataset utilized for the self-supervised pre-training phase is a comprehensive compilation of protein–ligand complexes sourced from various versions of the PDBbind database, including PDBbind v.2007, v.2013, v.2016, and v.2020 [24]. To ensure the integrity of the pre-training process and eliminate data leakage, a rigorous curation process was conducted to remove redundancies and labels. This resulted in a total of 19,462 non-overlapping complexes dedicated to pre-training. For the standard scoring task, we evaluated TopoLIA on three widely recognized benchmarks: CASF-2007, CASF-2013, and CASF-2016 [25–27]. To maintain a strict evaluation protocol, the training set for each benchmark comprised the PDBbind refined set, with all entries belonging to the respective core sets explicitly excluded. The performance was then benchmarked on the independent core sets of CASF-2007, CASF-2013, and CASF-2016. The docking power evaluation was conducted using the CASF-2013 benchmark. This test set consists of 195 ligands, each accompanied by 100 decoy poses generated by three docking programs, GOLD v5.1, Surflex-Dock in SYBYL v8.1, and MOE v2011 [25, 26]. To prepare the training set for this task, 1,000 training poses were generated for each target ligand–receptor pair using GOLD v.5.6.33 [62]. The pose structures and their corresponding GOLD scores are available for reproduction at 

https://weilab.math.msu.edu/AGL-Score . Following the methodology established in [10], we conducted an individual training and testing process for each of the 195 test ligands in the CASF-2013 core set. The final success rate was computed as the percentage of the 195 ligands for which the model correctly identified a native pose as the top-ranked candidate. To evaluate the model’s generalization capabilities across targets with low sequence homology, we utilized the LBA30 and LBA60 datasets derived from the PDBbind v.2019 refined set. This set consists of 4,852 complexes, which are carefully curated based on several quality criteria, including a resolution of 

≤ 2.5 Å, an R-factor ≤ 0.25, and the absence of steric clashes or covalent bonds. Complexes with unreliable ligand binding data are excluded. To avoid overfitting, a protein sequence-based splitting strategy was implemented. Following the Atom3D [28] partitioning method, the dataset with a 30% sequence identity threshold (LBA30) contains 3,507 proteins in the training set, 466 proteins in the validation set, and 490 proteins in the test set. Similarly, the 60% sequence identity threshold (LBA60) partition consists of 3,678 proteins for training, 460 for validation, and 460 for testing. This strategy prevents homologous proteins from appearing in both the training and test sets. Finally, for the 10-fold cross-validation, binding affinity data for training and testing was retrieved from the PDBbind v.2020 database [24]. The refined set is made up of 5,320 protein–ligand complexes with known binding affinities. The refined set provides a comprehensive and structurally diverse environment for robust model evaluation. Further details regarding all dataset splits are summarized in Table 1.

This objective function ensures that the model penalizes larger deviations more significantly, thereby refining the accuracy of the topological embeddings. The TopoLIA framework was optimized using a two-stage training strategy: self-supervised pre-training followed by task-specific fine-tuning. The selection of hyperparameters was guided by the need to balance the expressive capacity of the latent interaction transformer with the stability required for convergence on complex biomolecular data. A comprehensive summary of these settings is provided in Table 2. During the pre-training stage, the model utilized an encoder-decoder architecture designed for a masked reconstruction task [23]. The encoder consisted of 5 layers with a hidden dimension of 768 and 8 attention heads, while the decoder was comprised of 3 layers with a hidden dimension of 512 and 8 attention heads. To manage the high dimensionality of the topological sequence, the stalk dimensions were set to 96 for the encoder and 64 for the decoder. A masking ratio of 0.5 using a span-based approach was implemented to force the model to learn robust structural invariants from unlabeled protein–ligand interfaces. For the fine-tuning stage, the decoder was discarded, and the pretrained encoder was integrated with a task-specific regression head for binding affinity prediction. This transition involved a reduction in the learning rate from 1 × 10 −4 to 5 × 10 −5 and a decrease in batch size from 64 to 32 to ensure stable gradients during supervised adaptation. Both stages utilized a low-rank optimization parameter ( r = 8) to enhance computational efficiency and prevent over-fitting. The optimization process employed the AdamW optimizer with a weight decay of 0.05. To mitigate the risk of gradient instability, common in Transformer-based architectures, a linear warm-up strategy was implemented with a rate of 0.05. This ensured that the learning rate gradually increased over the first 5% of the total training steps (100 epochs for pre-training and 30 epochs for fine-tuning) before following a standard decay schedule. Regularization was further maintained through a dropout rate of 0.1 across all layers. To further enhance the predictive fidelity of the framework, the integrated TopoLIA model incorporates an E-Phys component designed to capture auxiliary electrostatic and physical descriptors. This component utilizes a gradient boosting tree (GBT) framework to model high-dimensional feature interactions that complement the primary geometric representations learned by the latent interaction transformer. The GBT model is configured with 4,000 estimators and a maximum depth of 7, allowing the architecture to capture intricate, non-linear relationships within the physical feature space. To ensure robust generalization and mitigate the risk of over-fitting, a learning rate of 0.01 is employed in conjunction with a subsampling rate of 0.7. # 4.1 Ablation Study on Geometric and Electrostatic Modules 

The robust predictive scoring capabilities of our framework are driven by GE-TopoLIA, a heterogeneous ensemble that synergistically integrates two specialized modules: G-TopoLIA extracting geometric signatures via the latent interaction transformer, and the E-Phys module capturing electrostatic energy landscapes via gradient boosting trees. As quantitatively detailed in Table 3, this integrated GE-TopoLIA architecture consistently surpasses its standalone constituent modules across all evaluated benchmarks. This performance gain is clearly illustrated by the CASF-2007 dataset, where the ensemble approach achieved a leading PCC of 0.832 and an RMSE of 1.842 kcal/mol. In comparison, the standalone G-TopoLIA module yielded a PCC of 0.817 and an RMSE of 1.908 kcal/mol, while the E-Phys module produced a PCC of 0.805 and an RMSE of 1.970 kcal/mol. Such measured improvements validate that the fusion of geometric and electrostatic topological features provides a more comprehensive and accurate representation of protein–ligand binding affinity than models relying on a single feature domain. For clarity, the full GE-TopoLIA ensemble is hereafter denoted simply as TopoLIA.

To delineate the limitations of our framework, we conducted a systematic investigation into the complexes characterized by the largest predictive discrepancies. We compiled comprehensive performance Tables 10, 11, and 12 across the CASF-2007, CASF-2013, and CASF-2016 benchmarks, sorted by decreasing predictive error. For the structural analysis presented here, we selected the most poorly predicted PDB entries from each benchmark as representatives to identify the fundamental factors constraining the model’s current predictive accuracy. These factors are primarily categorized into the structural simplification of the binding environment and chemical novelty relative to the training distribution. A primary driver of predictive failure is the inherent discrepancy between the native crystallographic environment and the refined protein–ligand representations used for model training. A comparative analysis of representative outliers (PDB IDs: 2X00, 1Y6Q, and 3MYG) reveals that original structural records often contain essential components, such as coordinated water molecules, metal ions, and co-factors, that stabilize the binding pocket. As explicitly illustrated in the native structures in Figure 7, these complexes features extensive hydration shells and ionic environments (represented by blue and yellow spheres). In high-affinity complexes, these elements frequently mediate critical charge-transfer effects or form water-bridge interactions that are indispensable for the observed binding affinity. However, as shown by the corresponding refined representations in Figure 7, the TopoLIA model fundamentally relies on a highly simplified, desolvated protein–ligand input. By removing these critical environmental determinants during data refinement, the model is deprived of the requisite physicochemical features to justify high experimental affinities, thereby resulting in substantial under-prediction. Beyond structural omissions, chemical novelty remains a formidable obstacle. We quantitatively evaluated the Tanimoto similarity between outlier ligands and the training distribution, as reported in Tables 10, 11, and 12. This analysis reveals that outliers with the largest errors in the CASF-2013 and CASF-2016 benchmarks, such as PDB IDs 3MYG and 2X00, possess exceptionally low similarity scores of 0.205 and 0.193, respectively. This chemical divergence indicates that the model struggles to generalize to novel molecular scaffolds that are poorly represented in the training set, emphasizing the need for broader data diversity or more robust transfer learning strategies in future iterations.
