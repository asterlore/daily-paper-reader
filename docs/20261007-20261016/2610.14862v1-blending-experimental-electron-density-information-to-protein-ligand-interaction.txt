ABSTRACT
Machine learning models for protein–ligand interactions commonly rely on
atomic coordinates, which are constructed by fitting molecular models to experi-
mental electron-density maps. However, most models represent each complex as
a single fitted structure, which can omit information about conformational hetero-
geneity, positional uncertainty, and local density support for individual atoms at
binding interfaces. To fill this gap, we present CODE, a Coordinate and Density
Encoder, pre-trained on experimental density maps. We represent each binding
site on a shared three-dimensional voxel grid combining atomic occupancy, exper-
imental electron density, and its gradient magnitude. Using approximately 100K
curated complexes, we pre-train a channel-wise vision transformer to jointly re-
construct masked atomic and density channels, learning a representation that in-
tegrates structural and experimental information. We evaluate the frozen encoder
on two downstream tasks: (i) binding-affinity regression under leakage-controlled
splits and (ii) pocket-conditioned ligand generation. Across these settings, CODE
improves over an identically pre-trained coordinate-only encoder and remains
competitive with task-specific baselines, showing that density complements fitted
atomic coordinates for protein–ligand interaction modeling and that the density-
blended representation is useful for structure-based ligand generation.

INTRODUCTION
Modern machine learning for proteins primarily relies on amino acid sequences and three-
dimensional atomic coordinates. Sequence models learn evolutionary and functional patterns (Rives
et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2023), while structure-based models capture molecular geometry and spatial
interactions from atomic coordinates (Jumper et al., 2021; Abramson et al., 2024). Their widespread
use is supported by large-scale resources such as UniProt (Consortium, 2019) and the Protein Data
Bank (Berman et al., 2000). However, these models often use only a single set of coordinates fitted
to X-ray diffraction data, which does not convey how strongly the data support each fitted posi-
tion or whether alternative conformations are present. This gap is consequential at protein–ligand
interfaces, where binding depends on local structure.
Crystallographic electron-density maps offer a complementary view of the same complex, reflecting
how well the data constrain each atom. A 2mFo-DFc map combines observed diffraction amplitudes
with model-dependent phase estimates and weights (Read, 1986). Its local pattern can reflect oc-
cupancy, atomic displacement, and unresolved alternative conformations, alongside resolution and
model error (Kuzmanic et al., 2014; Lang et al., 2014). For instance, greater atomic displacement
or disorder, reflected in a higher B factor, spreads the local density over a larger volume (Sun et al.,
2019). The density map at a protein–ligand interface may therefore help a model interpret both a
proposed contact and the structural ambiguity around it (van Zundert et al., 2018).
Yet, experimental electron-density maps remain underused for learning protein–ligand interactions,
while established applications mostly concern crystallographic structure determination (Kowiel
et al., 2019). Existing molecular models often construct density-like inputs from coordinates through
occupancy grids (Ragoza et al., 2022; Pinheiro et al., 2024) or quantum-chemical calculations (Is-

Recent work evaluates electron density maps as alternatives to atom-based inputs for affinity predic-
tion (Suriana et al., 2025). It remains unclear whether experimental electron-density maps comple-
ment the fitted coordinates in representing protein–ligand interactions.
Contribution. To fill this gap, we empirically examine the value of experimental electron density as
a complementary input for modeling protein–ligand interactions. We present CODE, a Coordinate
and Density Encoder, pre-trained by jointly reconstructing masked atomic occupancy, density, and
density gradient magnitude on a shared voxel grid, encouraging the representation to encode struc-
tural ambiguity or disorder. Curated PLINDER complexes (Durairaj et al., 2024) paired with exper-
imental 2mFo-DFc maps support pre-training, with a matched coordinate-only encoder and channel
ablations providing controlled comparisons. We evaluate the frozen representations on two down-
stream tasks: (i) binding-affinity regression, which probes protein–ligand interaction modeling, and
(ii) pocket-conditioned ligand generation, a representative structure-based drug-design task. Both
tasks use the reference’s experimental density map for the target of interest, allowing us to assess
the utility of the representations in scenarios where crystallographic data are available.
In our experiments, we make two observations:
• Electron-density map information is indeed predictive of protein–ligand interactions.
CODE
improves over the matched coordinate-only encoder, CoE, in binding-affinity regression and
pocket-conditioned ligand generation. When used to condition VoxBind, it also improves docking
scores over the original generator and remains competitive with task-specific baselines, while its
molecule-quality metrics stay close to VoxBind. Additionally, it produces more consistent local
geometry, with gains growing on large rigid fragments.
• The benefit of density is associated with structural disorder. Without being trained to predict B-
factors, the frozen CODE encoder more accurately decodes these values at the complex, residue,
and atom levels than CoE. Its affinity-ranking advantage over CoE is larger in higher complex
contact B-factors, consistent with density helping most where coordinates are less certain.

RELATED WORKS
Experimental electron-density maps. An experimental electron-density map is reconstructed from
the diffraction intensities of an X-ray experiment, conventionally as a likelihood-weighted 2mFo-

DFc map (Read, 1986). Since the experiment measures no phases, the map combines the observed
amplitudes Fo with model-derived amplitudes Fc and phases, weighted by the figure of merit m
and the model error D. Because it retains Fo, the map preserves evidence of partial occupancy and
alternative conformations that the fitted model omits (Lang et al., 2010; 2014). Prior works use such
maps mainly to support the fitting itself, e.g., ligand identification (Kowiel et al., 2019; Karolczak
et al., 2025) and local model assessment (Miyaguchi et al., 2021). Only recently, maps have been
used as an input for affinity prediction (Suriana et al., 2025), attempting to replace atomic inputs
rather than complementing them. Lin et al. (2025) shares a similar approach, tokenizing electron
clouds computed from coordinates by quantum chemistry and using hierarchical codebooks. In
contrast, we learn directly from experimentally measured maps.
Binding affinity regression.
Binding-affinity regression predicts the interaction strength of a
protein–ligand complex from experimentally measured binding constants (Wang et al., 2005),
with methods differing in the structural detail they consume. Sequence-based models use a lig-
and SMILES and protein sequences without 3D geometry ( ¨Ozt¨urk et al., 2018; Huang et al.,
2021; Shenoy et al., 2026), whereas structure-based models use atomic coordinates through voxel
CNNs (Stepniewska-Dziubinska et al., 2018), graph neural networks (Valsson et al., 2025; Kong
et al., 2026), or equivariant architectures (Kong et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024). Since labeled data
are limited, self-supervised methods pre-train with denoising (Jin et al., 2023) or interaction-based
objectives (Feng et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2024). Since data are scarce and redundant, which distorts
evaluation, leakage-controlled splits and benchmarks have been proposed, removing test complexes
resembling the training set (Graber et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Wei et al., 2026).
Pocket-conditioned ligand generation. Structure-based drug design uses target structures to iden-
tify or optimize ligands (Blundell, 1996; Anderson, 2003). Recent pocket-conditioned generators
build 3D ligands directly inside a given target pocket. Autoregressive models place atoms or frag-
ments sequentially (Luo et al., 2021; Peng et al., 2022), diffusion models denoise atom types and
coordinates (Guan et al., 2023; Schneuing et al., 2024), and voxel models denoise a continuous
atom occupancy grid (Pinheiro et al., 2023; 2024). A separate line of approaches supplies the gener-
ator with extra information about the target site; DecompDiff uses arm and scaffold priors estimated
from a reference ligand (Guan et al., 2024), while others use ligand-shaped electron density and grow
molecules into it (Wang et al., 2022; Li et al., 2025). Whereas Wang et al. (2022) use experimental
density maps for task-specific ligand generation, we ask whether a representation pre-trained jointly
on coordinates and density transfers to both binding-affinity regression and ligand generation.

METHOD
CODE represents a protein–ligand complex as a voxel grid combining atomic occupancy with exper-
imental electron density. We pre-train an encoder on this grid by masked reconstruction and transfer
its frozen representation to regression and generation tasks. Figure 1 illustrates the full pipeline.
Experimental electron-density map. Our input signal is the experimental electron-density map
used to refine each X-ray crystal structure. For each complex in PLINDER (Durairaj et al., 2024),
we fetch its 2mFo-DFc map through PDBe (Armstrong et al., 2020) by the PDB ID and normalize
it. Though the map is phased with the deposited model, it retains the observed amplitudes and thus
evidence of what the model omits. To ensure the ligand is resolved in the map, we keep complexes
with a ligand real-space correlation coefficient (Branden & Jones, 1990; Jones et al., 1991, RSCC)
of at least 0.8 (Fusani et al., 2020). We also remove every downstream validation and test complex
to prevent leakage. Further details are in Appendix A.
Voxel representation of protein–ligand complexes. To place atoms and density in a common
frame, we build a binding-site grid centered at the ligand centroid (Pinheiro et al., 2024). The
grid has G3 voxels with G = 64 at 0.25 ˚A spacing, covering a 16 ˚A cube. It contains the ligand
heavy atoms and the protein heavy atoms only within 10 ˚A of the ligand, cropped to the cube. Each
complex then becomes a 13-channel tensor x as follows:

The occupancy a ∈RG3×11 has seven ligand (C, O, N, S, F, Cl, P) and four pocket (C, O, N, S)
atom-type channels. Each channel c is the combination of Gaussian blobs gv,i over its heavy atoms
i ∈Ac (Orlando et al., 2022). A blob gv,i decays with the distance from the atom center ri to the
voxel center rv, at a width set by the element-specific van der Waals radius Ri. The density channel
ρ is the map tri-linearly interpolated at each voxel center. Its gradient magnitude ∥∇ρ∥is computed
on the same grid (Suriana et al., 2025). Further details are in Appendix A.
Masked pre-training. We then learn representations of the grid without labels, following masked
image modeling (He et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2022). A Channel-ViT (Bao et al., 2023) encoder fθ
splits the grid into (G/P)3 blocks of P 3 voxels, with P = 8 corresponding to a 2 ˚A cube. We
mask 75% of the blocks and broadcast the mask to a voxel-level mask M ∈{0, 1}G3. The encoder
only sees the visible input ex = x ⊙(1 −M), with masked blocks zeroed rather than dropped. A
lightweight reconstruction MLP head hψ then reconstructs all channels with a loss L as follows:

zero-initialized convolution, before the denoising U-Net. Only the generator and this fusion layer
are trained, while the encoder stays frozen. For further details, refer to Appendix B.

4.1
BINDING AFFINITY REGRESSION
Setup. We first use protein–ligand binding affinity regression as a controlled test of whether exper-
imental density adds information beyond atomic coordinates. The goal of this experiment is not to
establish a practical affinity predictor, but rather to determine whether density encodes complemen-
tary information. We use complex data from PDBBind v2020 (Liu et al., 2015), keeping only those
with a crystallographic density map and affinity measured as Kd or Ki. We exclude validation and
test complex data from the PLINDER-based pre-training set. We adopt the LP-PDBBind split (Li
et al., 2026) to reduce leakage from complex-level interaction similarity, and report results on both
the full test set and its CL3 subset, restricting evaluation to higher-quality complexes.
Evaluation. We measure linear and rank agreement by the Pearson (r) and Spearman (ρ) correla-
tions, and the predictive error by RMSE. Results are averaged over five seeds, except for zero-shot
pre-trained scorers with single predictions. Other results come from our own runs under identi-
cal protocols: pre-trained encoders use the same frozen-probe protocol as CODE with their own
probing heads, while the remaining methods are trained end-to-end with their own heads. Some
baselines pre-train on data that overlaps with the LP-PDBBind test split, e.g., the co-folding model
Nesso-1 (Shenoy et al., 2026) and affinity-supervised IPNet (Huang et al., 2024). We mark them
with † and include them for context rather than as leakage-controlled comparisons.
Main results. Table 1 shows that CODE improves over COE on both test sets, and the pre-trained
COE itself is competitive with structure-based baselines. CODE is comparable to the strongest
structure-based baseline, ProFSA (Gao et al., 2024). The sequence-based co-folding model Nesso-1
(Shenoy et al., 2026) reaches similar correlation without any structural input, though its training data
overlaps with the test split of LP-PDBBind. Without pre-training, CODE and COE perform com-
parably to weaker structure-based baselines, indicating that the pre-training provides transferable
representations. The larger gain of CODE further supports that density provides complementary
information. Overall, we read these results as evidence that experimental electron density carries
transferable information on binding.

we quantify through crystallographic B factors as a proxy for positional uncertainty (higher values
indicate less certain atomic positions). To test what the representation encodes, we keep the encoder
frozen and train lightweight MLPs to decode B factors at the complex, residue, and atom levels. In

Evaluation. Following Guan et al. (2023), we aim to generate 100 ligands for each pocket with ev-
ery method and ask three questions of the generated ligands. First, do they bind better to the pocket?
Stronger binding is the goal of pocket-conditioned ligand generation; therefore, we dock the ligands
with AutoDock Vina (Eberhardt et al., 2021) and compare Vina scores and sample quality. Second,
do the gains reflect new molecules rather than copies of the reference ligand? Since CODE condi-
tions on the reference complex, higher scores would be trivial if it simply replayed the reference,
so we measure the novelty and nearest-neighbor similarity against the training set. Third, are the
molecules physically plausible? A better docking score is meaningful only for a realistic pose; we
check molecular geometry with PoseCheck (Harris et al., 2023), validity with PoseBusters (But-
tenschoen et al., 2024), and geometry of the rigid fragments (Guan et al., 2023). For details and
additional results on each evaluation, refer to Appendices D and E.

better in VoxBind, yet CODE’s gain holds across heavy atom counts. Notably, COE repeatedly
generates the same ligands, leaving fewer distinct molecules within the same attempt budget (n in
Table 3), whereas density conditioning avoids this.

CONCLUSION
In this work, we introduced CODE, a voxel encoder jointly pre-trained on atomic occupancy and
experimental 2mFo-DFc maps by masked reconstruction. Across frozen-probe affinity regression
and pocket-conditioned ligand generation, CODE improves over a matched coordinate-only en-
coder and stays competitive with task-specific baselines, and its advantage is largest where the fitted
coordinates are least certain. These results support experimental electron density as a transferable
complement to coordinates for learning protein–ligand interactions.

Pocket-conditioned ligand generation
PLINDER. PLINDER (Durairaj et al., 2024) is our pre-training source. It curates the Protein Data
Bank (PDB) as of 2024-04-09 into 449383 protein–ligand interaction (PLI) systems, each annotated
with over 500 fields and with pocket-, ligand-, and interaction-level similarity. It also links 98473
experimental apo structures and 205300 AlphaFold2 (Jumper et al., 2021) predictions to their holo
entries. We use only holo X-ray systems, drawn from all PLINDER splits, since our downstream
tasks define their own test sets. Every downstream validation and test complex was excluded.
PDBBind 2020. PDBBind (Wang et al., 2005; Liu et al., 2015) provides our affinity labels for the
binding affinity regression downstream task. It pairs protein–ligand crystal structures with measured
binding constants (Kd, Ki, IC50). The 2020 release consists of three sets: (i) the general set consist-
ing of 19443 complexes, (ii) the refined set consisting of 5316 complexes that additionally satisfy
stricter criteria such as a crystallographic resolution of 2.5 ˚A or better and Ki, Kd only records, and
(iii) a core set of 285 complexes. The general, refined, core set was designed around structure qual-
ity rather than around similarity control. We therefore do not use the conventional split that trains
on the refined set excluding the core set and evaluates on the core set.
LP-PDBBind. LP-PDBBind (Li et al., 2026) re-splits PDBBind v2020 so that test complexes are
dissimilar to training complexes. It showed that the standard sets share highly similar proteins and
ligands, so gains on the core set overstate generalization. LP-PDBBind bounds protein and ligand
similarity across 11513 training, 2422 validation, and three nested test tiers of 4286 (CL1), 2179
(CL2), and 1348 (CL3) complexes. CL1 removes covalent binders, ligands with rare elements, and
complexes with steric clashes; CL2 keeps consistent Kd/Ki measurements; and CL3 keeps only
refined- and core-set complexes. We further drop entries that RDKit cannot parse.
CASF-2016. CASF-2016 (Su et al., 2019) serves as an additional held-out affinity test. It is built
on the PDBBind core set, with 57 target clusters of five ligands that bind the same protein across
a wide affinity range. Following Wei et al. (2026), we probe learned representations, restricted to
the 92 complexes that have a density map and appear neither in pre-training nor in the LP-PDBBind
training or validation split.
CrossDocked2020. CrossDocked2020 (Francoeur et al., 2020) is our pocket-conditioned ligand
generation benchmark. It docks ligands into similar binding pockets across the PDB, yielding 22.5
million poses grouped by binding-site similarity. Following Luo et al. (2021); Schneuing et al.
(2024); Guan et al. (2023; 2024); Pinheiro et al. (2023), we filter poses by RMSD and sequence
identity, leaving 100,000 training pairs and 100 test pockets from held-out clusters. For each recep-
tor, we retrieve the density map of the crystal structure it derives from by its PDB ID and align the
receptor to that frame. The map therefore shows the native crystal ligand of that entry rather than
the docked ligand pose, which is computational and has no density of its own.
Electron density maps. All maps are the likelihood-weighted 2mFo-DFc maps that PDBe serves as
pre-computed CCP4 grids, paired with the deposited coordinates:
https://www.ebi.ac.uk/pdbe/entry-files/{pdb id}.ccp4
We use them as deposited, without re-refinement, re-phasing, or resolution and B-factor cutoffs.

matched atoms or a fit RMSD above 2 ˚A are dropped. PLINDER and PDBBind use deposited

per complex, stored in 8 bits over [−2.2, 5.0]. Its 6.5 ˚A margin per side lets rotation augmentation
resample the map at load time rather than zero-fill a fixed crop.

B.1
ARCHITECTURE
Encoder. CODE is a 99.47M-parameter Channel-ViT (Bao et al., 2023) that tokenizes each channel
group separately. The 13 input channels form three groups of sizes (7, 4, 2): ligand atoms, pocket
atoms, and the density and gradient magnitude pair. Each group has its own 83 convolution, which
embeds the 643 grid into 512 patch tokens of width 640. Every token receives a learnable positional
embedding, shared across groups, and a learnable embedding of its group. The resulting 1536
tokens pass through 18 pre-LayerNorm (Ba et al., 2016) Transformer blocks (Vaswani et al., 2017)
with 10 heads, an MLP ratio of 4, GELU activations (Hendrycks & Gimpel, 2016), and dropout of
0.1, followed by a final LayerNorm. Since all groups attend to one another, the encoder can relate
density to atoms at every location.
Voxel projection and reconstruction head. The encoder includes a linear voxel projection. For
each patch, the final tokens of its three groups are averaged and linearly projected to a 16-channel
83 feature cube. A separate two-layer reconstruction MLP head with hidden width 67 maps the
16-channel cube of each patch to the 13 input channels. The reconstruction MLP head is used only
for pre-training and discarded afterwards.

elements (C, O, N, S), ρ, and ∥∇ρ∥. Atoms are rendered as soft occupancies following Orlando
et al. (2022), with Ri the van der Waals radius of atom i and σ = 0.93:

DOWNSTREAM TASK ADAPTATION
Binding-affinity probing. We fit a small MLP on frozen, mean-pooled encoder tokens. The 1536
final tokens are averaged into a 640-dimensional complex embedding, and a two-layer MLP re-
gresses affinity from it with mean-squared error (Table 6). We keep the checkpoint with the highest
validation Spearman correlation and use no test-time rotation averaging.
Pocket-conditioned ligand generation. We keep the VoxBind (Pinheiro et al., 2024) walk-jump
sampler and change only its pocket conditioning. The frozen encoder reads the reference complex
with the experimental density. Its patch tokens are averaged over channel groups, and the encoder’s
linear output layer, trained with it during pre-training, maps each token to the features of the voxels
in its patch, restoring the full grid as xdens. This output is fused with VoxBind’s ligand and pocket
features x through a zero-initialized 1 × 1 × 1 convolution, x ←x + W[x∥xdens]. Only the denoiser
and W receive gradients and are updated.
Generation optimization. The denoiser follows VoxBind: a 3D U-Net with base width 32, channel
multipliers [1, 2, 2, 4], two residual blocks per level, and attention at the two coarsest levels (111.6M
parameters). We train it for 350 epochs from scratch with AdamW, a constant learning rate of 10−5,
weight decay 10−2, noise level σ = 0.9, and a batch size of 32 per GPU on four H200 or RTX
PRO 6000 Blackwell GPUs. Sampling uses walk-jump with σ = 0.9, friction γ = 1, step δ = σ/2,
and 400 warm-up and 100 sampling steps from a de novo initialization, with the pocket masked out
of both noise and score. Grids are decoded to atoms by non-maximum-suppressed peak picking at
threshold 0.25, followed by L-BFGS refinement against the predicted grid.

We compare against a broad set of baselines covering sequence-based, structure-based, and pre-
trained protein–ligand complex representation models. For methods that provide pre-trained en-
coders, we follow the same probing protocol as our model and train a lightweight prediction head
MLP on top of the frozen representations. For methods without publicly available pre-trained repre-
sentations, we train the model on the LP-PDBbind training split using its original or recommended
training objective as possible. The baselines considered in our experiments are as follows:
• DeepDTA ( ¨Ozt¨urk et al., 2018) uses 1D convolutional networks on a ligand SMILES and
the amino acid sequence of the target protein.
• MolTrans (Huang et al., 2021) uses Transformer encoders to model substructural tokens
extracted from the ligand SMILES and the full protein sequence, without using 3D coordi-
nates.
• HonestAffinity (Wei et al., 2026) uses frozen ESM-2 650M embeddings (Lin et al., 2023)
for the full protein sequence, with a binary indicator specifying pocket residues. It does not
use 3D coordinates as input.
• Nesso-1 (Shenoy et al., 2026) takes the full protein sequence and a ligand represented by
SMILES or molecular structure, following a co-folding style architecture stemming from
Boltz-2 (Passaro et al., 2025). It does not require a pre-defined pocket or experimentally
determined protein coordinates as input.
• HBGSA (Kong et al., 2026) uses the 3D protein–ligand binding complex, including explicit
coordinates of the protein–ligand hydrogen bonds. It models the spatial topology with a
graph neural network and self-attention. Additionally, it uses the binding-site structure
rather than the full protein sequence.
• EGNN (Duval et al., 2023) represents the protein pocket and ligands as atom features and
3D coordinates, and process them using an E(3)-equivariant graph neural network.
• EGNN + TargetDiff (Guan et al., 2023) uses the same protein-pocket and ligand atoms
with 3D coordinates as above, while initializing the encoder with TargetDiff pre-trained for
protein-conditioned 3D ligand generation.
• GET (Kong et al., 2023) represents the 3D protein–ligand complex as a hierarchical geo-
metric graph and applies an E(3)-equivariant Transformer to model intra- and intermolec-
ular interactions.
• CheapNet (Lim et al., 2025) uses the protein binding pocket and ligand with their 3D
atomic structures and learns hierarchical atom- and cluster-level representations with cross-
molecular interactions.
• IPNet (Huang et al., 2024) encodes the 3D protein pocket and ligand with SE(3)-
equivariant networks and cross-attention layers, pre-trained with binding affinity super-
vision on PDBBind 2016 (Liu et al., 2015). Additionally, it is used as the interaction prior
component of IPDiff.
• AEV-PLIG (Valsson et al., 2025) uses atoms from the protein binding site and the ligand,
along with their 3D coordinates, represented as atomic environment vectors and a protein–
ligand interaction graph.
• GeoSSL (Liu et al., 2022) pre-trains molecular representations from 3D atomic coordinates
using SE(3)-invariant denoising distance matching.

POCKET-CONDITIONED LIGAND GENERATION
We compare our model against autoregressive, diffusion-based, and voxel-based pocket-conditioned
molecular generation methods. We use the publicly released generated samples or official imple-

• AR (Luo et al., 2021) sequentially samples ligand atoms from a learned spatial distribution

• TargetDiff (Guan et al., 2023) uses an equivariant diffusion model to jointly generate lig-

• VoxBind (Pinheiro et al., 2024) represents molecules as atomic-density voxel grids, and
generates ligands through conditional voxel denoising.
• FuncBind (Kirchmeyer et al., 2026) represents molecules as continuous neural fields
for target-conditioned all-atom generation, trained on three modalities: small molecules,
macrocyclic peptides(MCPs), and antibody complementarity-determining region (CDR)
loops.

RIGID-FRAGMENT CONSISTENCY
We measure whether rigid fragments keep their force-field geometry, following Guan et al. (2023).
A rigid fragment, such as a benzene ring, an amide, or a fused bicycle, has one correct shape, so

algorithm (Kabsch, 1976). The configuration is as follows:
• Version: RDKit 2024.03.06
• Force Field: MMFF
• Fragmentation of every rotatable bond

E.4
MACROCYCLES IN LIGAND GENERATION
We ask whether the favorable docking scores of generated macrocycles come with favorable geom-
etry. We define a macrocycle as a molecule with a ring of at least 12 atoms in RDKit’s SymmSSSR,
and characterize it by the size of that largest ring, which counts ring atoms rather than all heavy
atoms in the molecule. Figure 13 compares TargetDiff, VoxBind, and CODE on the same 79 test
pockets. At every shared ring size, CODE attains lower median strain, fewer clashes, and a higher
PoseBusters pass rate than TargetDiff, and its median Vina Dock score is the lowest of the three for
ring sizes of 18–21 atoms; against VoxBind the comparison varies with ring size.
These curves are descriptive rather than controlled. The three methods generate macrocycles in
different pocket subsets (30, 40, and 60 pockets, with 24 shared by CODE and VoxBind), and
conditioning on ring size matches neither heavy-atom count nor pocket composition; a comparison
matched on both leaves too few strata to support the pooled advantage over VoxBind. We also
note that PoseCheck and PoseBusters assess the generated poses whereas Vina Dock scores follow
re-docking, so passing PoseBusters does not validate the re-docked pose.

afterwards with the same OpenBabel-based construction (O’Boyle et al., 2011), which does not use
aromaticity. To assess the contribution of molecular reconstruction, we classify failing molecules
into four diagnostic categories. These categories describe geometric and reconstruction properties;
they do not establish a unique underlying cause for each failure.
• Flat non-aromatic ring. A genuinely planar saturated or partially saturated ring, which is
the failure the test is designed to catch.
• Signed-distance rule. A ring atom lies at least 0.05 ˚A from the plane, so the ring is puck-
ered; PoseBusters nevertheless fails it because it compares the largest signed distance with
the threshold, and the orientation of the plane normal is arbitrary.
• Benzene-like, no valid aromatic assignment. The failing ring has benzene-like geometry
(ring bonds between 1.20 and 1.60 ˚A, mean internal angle between 116◦and 124◦, every
atom within 0.1 ˚A of the plane), but re-assigning it as aromatic cannot be kekulized with
the rest of the molecule.
• Valid aromatic reassignment. The failing ring meets the benzene-like geometric criteria
above, and an alternative aromatic bond assignment yields a valid, kekulizable molecule
after adjusting attached hydrogens. Such cases are consistent with a mismatch between the
generated geometry and the reconstructed molecular graph. However, the availability of an
aromatic alternative does not establish that the original ring was intended to be aromatic.
For CODE, 87.3% of the failing molecules fall into the valid-aromatic-reassignment category, while
11.6% have benzene-like geometry without a valid aromatic assignment under our diagnostic pro-
cedure. The flat non-aromatic category accounts for 0.9% of its failing molecules, or 0.20% of all
generated molecules, compared with 0.23% of all generated molecules for VoxBind. These results
suggest that molecular reconstruction contributes substantially to the observed ring non-flatness fail-
ures. The categories are diagnostic, however, and do not establish the physical validity of the original
molecular graphs.
