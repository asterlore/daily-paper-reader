Abstract 

Drug discovery remains a resource-intensive and time-consuming process, involv-ing billions of dollars and a decade of development to advance a drug to market. While many in silico tools have accelerated this process, operating them still demands substantial time, effort, and expertise from medicinal chemists. Autonomous systems built on Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as a promising solution, yet current designs limit their performance. Existing single-agent systems scale poorly to long-horizon, multi-step, and realistic tasks, whereas multi-agent systems typically execute predefined workflows that cannot capture the iterative, non-linear nature of a real drug discovery campaign. Here, we present MADDIE , a multi-agent drug discovery engine. It comprises a cen-tral agent, Orchestrator , and four specialized worker agents: HitIdentifier ,

LeadOptimizer , Synthesizer , and Evaluator , each representing a critical step in a drug discovery campaign. Under Orchestrator ’s orchestration and coordination, these worker agents are composed into flexible workflows, enabling 

MADDIE to adapt its strategy and navigate the drug discovery pipeline end-to-end. Across 50 protein targets, MADDIE produces molecules satisfying key property requirements and their synthesis routes, outperforming existing agentic systems, generative models, and general-purpose LLMs by a wide margin. Our analysis reveals how these agents collaborate and that MADDIE can employ different strategies across targets to achieve the campaign’s goal, while still being 

1arXiv:2610.16975v1 [cs.AI] 12 Oct 2026 efficient in time and tokens. Together, they demonstrate MADDIE as a low-cost, effective, and efficient autonomous engine for accelerating drug discovery. Code and dataset is available via https://github.com/ninglab/MADDIE. 

# 1 Introduction 

Drug discovery is a complex, capital-intensive, and time-consuming process: bringing one approved drug to the market typically costs more than one billion of dollars and takes more than a decade [1–4]. To expedite this process, many in silico tools have been developed [5–10], each designed for a specific task in the pipeline (e.g., predicting binding affinity). Using them, however, has traditionally fallen to medicinal chemists. Autonomous systems capable of intelligently using these tools have gained considerable attention recently [11–19], substantially reducing the time and effort demanded of medicinal chemists while showing promising results. These systems are typically built upon Large Language Models (LLMs), reasoning engines capable of intelligent decision-making and planning over complex tasks [20]. LIDDIA [11] is one of the first such systems: a single -agent capable of autonomously navigating drug discovery in silico . Following LIDDIA, several systems have been introduced, with more attention towards multi -agent systems [12, 14, 15, 18, 19]. Some of them are designed for a specific task under the drug discovery pipeline, while others for end-to-end drug discovery [14, 18]. While promising, these agentic systems leave room for improvements. Single-agent systems such as LIDDIA [11] and AgentD [19] demonstrate how LLMs can intelligently coordinate different in silico tools for drug discovery, yet their performance does not scale well to more realistic and complex tasks (Section 4.1). Multi-agent systems such as PharmAgents [14], Prompt-to-Pill [18] and F.A.D.E. [17] are designed for complex tasks, but they typically assume a predefined workflow. This assumption does not reflect real-world drug discovery, which is a highly non-linear process governed by many requirements. For instance, successful drugs must bind well to the target, while maintaining favorable pharmacokinetic and physicochemical property profiles [21]. However, identifying such molecules is highly difficult, requiring numerous iterations of searching the expansive chemical space [22]. Other multi-agent systems have also been proposed that enable more flexible workflows but are still limited in adaptability [13, 16]. Building upon these insights, we propose MADDIE , a Multi-Agent Drug 

DI scovery Engine. As illustrated in Figure 1, the system is composed of five agents, a central agent called (1) Orchestrator , and four specialized worker agents: 

(2) HitIdentifier ; (3) LeadOptimizer ; (4) Synthesizer ; and (5) Evaluator .

Orchestrator serves as an intelligent orchestration component that governs the whole campaign. Unlike static workflows, Orchestrator dynamically determines the workflow through its actions, sustains a long-horizon campaign using its memory module and monitors the progress by maintaining its evolving repository of candi-date molecules. contributes the most. Together, these findings indicate that dynamic orchestration of specialized worker agents through a central agent offers an effective design for autonomous drug discovery, and MADDIE can serve as a promising system in this domain. 

# 2 Related Works 

## 2.1 Agents for Drug Discovery 

Agents powered by LLMs are capable of coordinating in silico tools across broader drug discovery workflows. Existing systems operate at different levels of scope and autonomy, from task-specific assistants to end-to-end drug discovery. These systems are typically classified into two categories: (1) single-agent system and (2) multi-agent system. CACTUS [24] focuses on assistive cheminformatics tasks such as estimating molec-ular properties, retrieving structurally similar molecules, and evaluating drug-likeness. TxGemma [15] also proposes a conversational assistant for therapeutics predic-tions and question answering, allowing better interaction with medicinal chemists. AgentD [19] spans a wider range of the early-stage computational pipeline in drug dis-covery, from target and ligand information retrieval, to molecular generation, property prediction, and refinement. LIDDIA [11] provides an autonomous workflow where LLM-based reasoning guides the iterative process of finding satisfying molecules that fit a specific profile. These examples demonstrate that LLMs can coordinate in sil-ico tools toward a larger drug discovery goal. However, their performance is typically limited and does not scale well for more complex and realistic tasks (Section 4.1). Multi-agent systems distribute their tasks across specialized agents, enabling them to tackle complex problems, such as drug discovery, that can exceed the reach of any single-agent system. Their system designs and assigned roles for the agents vary among systems. DrugAgent [12] coordinates planner and instructor agents to develop, implement, and refine machine-learning solutions using drug discovery domain knowl-edge. Robin [25] assigns literature-search and data-analysis to separate agents, using their output to loop through hypothesis formulation, experiment planning, and result interpretation. More directly focused on target binding site identification and candi-date generation, F.A.D.E. [17] uses a hierarchical multi-agent setup to produce ranked candidates and demonstrates its effectiveness through case studies on targets EGFR and CRBP1. Prompt-to-Pill [18] and PharmAgents [14] work at a broader scope, coor-dinating agents across drug discovery phases, from preclinical stage to clinical-trial simulation. Mozi [16] proposes a dual-layer multi-agent architecture for drug discov-ery, a layer for control and governance and another one for workflow. FROGENT [13] 4shows an example of a decentralized multi-agent system, using an orchestrator agent that decomposes the task into a high-level plan, and the other agents to execute each subtask in the plan. Together, these systems demonstrate the overall capability of collaboration between different specialized agents on drug discovery tasks. However, their coordination generally centers on predefined workflows, and their system evalu-ations often focus on individual case studies. This is in contrast to MADDIE , which enables flexible workflow adaptation across different campaigns (Section 4.3). 

## 2.2 Generative Models for Drug Design 

Generative models have recently been employed for de novo drug design to accelerate drug discovery. These models generally fall under two categories: (1) structure-based drug design (SBDD) and (2) ligand-based drug design (LBDD). SBDD methods generate ligand molecules conditioned on the three-dimensional structures of target binding pockets. Early approaches such as Pocket2Mol [26] use autoregressive sampling to generate molecular atoms sequentially, while more recent approaches are diffusion-based [27, 28]. Notably, conDitar-dev [10] decouples pro-tein encoding from the diffusion process through a separately pretrained pocket encoder and performs inference-time noise optimization to improve molecular proper-ties beyond binding affinity. Several works augment SBDD models with LLM-based refinement [29–31]. ELILLM [31], one of the state-of-the-art methods, encodes the initial molecules into the LLM latent space and performs Bayesian optimization to identify promising molecular representations. LBDD generates molecules conditioned on the shape of the ligand that binds to the target structure’s pocket. Previous approaches include SQUID [32], using a fragment-based generative model to decode fragments and a scoring model to adjust the angles of rotatable bonds between fragments, and DESERT [33], an image-to-sequence model with an encoder-decoder architecture that generates molecular structures conditioned on voxelized shapes. DiffSMol [8], one of state-of-the-art methods for LBDD, encodes the ligand shape into pretrained embeddings and uses these embeddings to condition a diffusion-based molecular generation model. Together, these generative models provide effective means of finding promising molecules. Generation alone, however, is only the first step of drug discovery, and the molecules these models produce often demand substantial manual downstream filtering and selection [10, 34]. MADDIE addresses this gap by integrating the fil-tering and selection into the system itself: HitIdentifier and LeadOptimizer 

propose the molecules, while Evaluator and Synthesizer assess them—all under 

Orchestrator ’s coordination. 

# 3 Experimental Settings 

Dataset 

We curate a dataset to evaluate MADDIE . Concretely, we collect (1) a set of 50 protein targets, along with their 3D binding pocket structures, and (2) a set of drugs approved by U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), each is associated with a target in our dataset. These drugs are used to define what molecular property 5requirements MADDIE ’s generated molecules should meet (Table 1). We provide the full protein targets, their annotations, the curated FDA-approved drugs, and the protocol to collect them in this paper (Appendix A). The targets span major therapeutic areas including autoimmune disease, can-cer, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, infectious disease, and neurological conditions. The dataset includes 42 targets from existing datasets: (1) LIDDIA [11] (12 tar-gets) and (2) CrossDocked2020 [35] (30 targets), and additional 8 targets from OpenTargets [36]. We ensure that these targets do not exist in CrossDocked2020 training set, thus allowing MADDIE to be compared against methods trained on CrossDocked2020. We use the experimentally validated 3D structures available [37] and preprocess them to extract the binding pockets, similar to LIDDIA’s dataset preprocessing [11]. For each target, we identify whether it has drugs that are (1) small molecules, 

(2) FDA-approved, and (3) currently marketed as of July 2026. Out of 50 targets, 22 targets are associated with at least one drug. For the remaining targets (28), no drug associations are identified, indicating opportunities for drug development for these targets. We use these drugs to calculate the property requirements for our experiment (Appendix B). 

Evaluation Metrics 

We use several metrics to evaluate the performance of MADDIE . These metrics are based on (1) the molecular properties often considered in real-world drug discovery, 

(2) the synthesizability of the molecule, and (3) whether the molecule can satisfy multiple property and synthesizability requirements simultaneously. We evaluate the generated molecules on several key properties critical for a suc-cessful drug. We first consider properties commonly used in the literature [10]: (1) 

binding affinity, measured by Vina docking score [5] ( Vina ); (2) drug-likeness, or desir-ability of a molecule using quantitative estimate of drug-likeness [38] ( QED ); and (3) 

synthesizability estimate, using synthetic accessibility score [39] ( SAS ). In addition to common properties, we further assess the toxicities of these molecules. Concretely, we measure the probability that a molecule may (1) induce mutations in DNA [40] ( Mut ), 

(2) fail clinical trials owing to toxicity [41] ( CTx ), and (3) inhibit the hERG potassium channel [42] ( hERG ). Finally, we apply Lilly medicinal filters [43] ( Lly ) to flag whether the molecules have undesirable traits, such as interfering with assay measurements (e.g., fluorescence) or lacking druggability (e.g., molecules lacking both oxygen and nitrogen). We assess the synthesizability of a molecule by determining whether a syn-thesis route can be generated ( Syn ). We employ the same approach as Synthesizer 

in MADDIE to measure the synthesizability of the molecule (Section 7.2.3). This is a more rigorous assessment than synthesizability estimate ( SAS ; Section 7.2.4). For every molecular property and synthesizability defined previously, we identify a threshold as a requirement for a molecule to meet. These thresholds are determined using existing FDA-approved drugs curated in our dataset as references (Appendix B), ensuring that they are realistic rather than arbitrary cutoffs. Baselines 

To evaluate the effectiveness of MADDIE , we compare it with a wide range of base-lines, each cast as a generate-then-filter pipeline: a molecular generation method followed by a filtering stage. The molecule generation methods span three categories: 

(1) Agents for Drug Discovery , (2) Generative Models for Drug Design and (3) Gen-eralist LLMs . The molecules generated from each method are then filtered against each requirement, if the method cannot assess the corresponding property or synthe-sizability during generation. Table 1 presents which property or synthesizability each method can assess during its generation. Every molecule from generative models for drug design and generalist LLMs is filtered against every requirement after genera-tion. In contrast, molecules from agents for drug discovery baselines are filtered only against the requirements that cannot be assessed by the method during generation. Only molecules that pass the filtering are considered as the output molecules of that method. Coupling a generation method with a filtering stage enables a fair comparison with MADDIE , while also being a widely adopted paradigm in drug discovery [10, 34]. 7We use a variety of methods for each category of the baselines. (1) For Agents for Drug Discovery , we compare against two state-of-the-art single-agent systems, LIDDIA [11] and AgentD [19], and one multi-agent system, Prompt-to-Pill [18]. These agents are designed for end-to-end drug discovery workflow, similar to ours, and serve as direct points of comparison for MADDIE . They are also selected since their implementations are publicly accessible (Appendix C). (2) For Generative Models for Drug Design , we compare against a wide variety of models: Pocket2Mol [26], TargetDiff [27], conDitar [10], DiffSMol [8] and ELILLM [31]. These models include both SBDD (Pocket2Mol, TargetDiff, conDitar, ELILLM) and LBDD (DiffSMol) approaches. (3) For Generalist LLMs , we compare against two open-source LLMs, Qwen3-30B [45] and Nemotron-3-Ultra [44]. We focus our comparison of MADDIE 

to open-source LLMs for reproducibility and accessibility: their weights are publicly available and are less likely to be discontinued than proprietary LLMs. Each LLM is competitive on general reasoning and scientific benchmarks [44, 45], making them strong baselines. Qwen3-30B is also used as the reasoning engine for MADDIE , while Nemotron-3-Ultra (550B) is among the largest and state-of-the-art open-source LLMs available. We only select two due to our experimentation budget. Each method is instructed to produce the same fixed number of output molecules per target as MADDIE for a fair comparison. Methods that cannot be instructed in this way, including the five generative models for drug design and AgentD, are instead instructed to produce as many intermediate molecules as MADDIE pro-duces, per target. Specifically, we define an intermediate molecule as any molecule that HitIdentifier , LeadOptimizer , and Synthesizer produce during a cam-paign, whether or not they serve as an output molecule. The number of intermediate 

molecules is used as a basis for how many molecules the generative models and AgentD should generate, and also for analysis (Section 4.2). This ensures that these base-lines receive the same opportunities as MADDIE to explore the chemical space (by generating the same number of molecules), thus supporting a fair comparison. 

Implementation Details 

In MADDIE , we adopt Qwen3-30B [45] as the backbone LLM for Orchestrator 

and all four worker agents, an open-source LLM with strong performance across vary-ing tasks while also small enough to be deployed locally. The model is deployed with vLLM [46] and decoded with a temperature of 0.7, a top-p of 0.8, and a context win-dow of 64K tokens. These are the recommended parameters for Qwen3-30B [45]. We also provide implementation details for the baselines (Appendix D), along with more details on MADDIE (Appendix E).

Agentic baselines employ evaluation tools during generation but still leave requirements unsatisfied. As illustrated in Table 1, each agent is capable of measuring some properties for the requirements: LIDDIA measures Vina , QED and 

SAS during its campaign, Prompt-to-Pill measures Vina , QED , Mut and hERG , and AgentD measures QED . For LIDDIA and Prompt-to-Pill, however, the per-requirement pass rates still fall short on exactly these properties, 83.1% on Vina for LIDDIA 

and 41.0% on Mut for Prompt-to-Pill. These failures likely arise from the reasoning engine powering these agents: the LLMs themselves. LLMs may misinterpret the tool outputs or hallucinated, leading it to accept a molecule that does not satisfy the requirements—a well-known weakness of LLM-based agent systems [47]. In contrast, 

MADDIE addresses this weakness by accepting a molecule only after it has been evaluated by Evaluator and Synthesizer (Section 7). 

Generative models for drug design achieve perfect passing rates by fil-tering, but retain far fewer molecules than requested. For these baselines, filtering is applied against every requirement after generation, and molecules failing any requirement are discarded, leading to 100% pass rates for every target (with at 9least one output molecule). Filtering, however, cannot improve on what the methods produce: conDitar and Pocket2Mol retain only 56.7 and 33.1 satisfying molecules on average, respectively, both well below the requested 100. When the method rarely pro-duces molecules meeting the requirements, filtering may retain none of them, as with DiffSMol for 23 of the targets. This exposes a fundamental limitation for methods without feedback from tools during generation: the pass rate can always be enforced to 100% through filtering, but the effective yield, or the number of satisfying molecules cannot be improved. 

Generalist LLMs produce very few satisfying molecules. For instance, Nemotron-3-Ultra, one of the state-of-the-art open-source LLMs [44], produces on average only 23.2 satisfying molecules on 38 targets. Qwen3-30B, the LLM that drives every agent in MADDIE , covers only 3 targets on its own, emphasizing that the gap to the 50 reached by MADDIE is attributable to its design rather than solely to the capability of the underlying language model. 

## 4.2 Ablation Study 

We conduct ablation experiments to assess the effectiveness of the different agents in the MADDIE framework. Specifically, we remove Synthesizer (Section 7.2.3), 

LeadOptimizer (Section 7.2.2) and Orchestrator (Section 7.1) individually, resulting in three variants of MADDIE : (1) w/o Synthesizer , without the capa-bility to assess synthesizability; (2) w/o LeadOptimizer , which cannot optimize any property; and (3) w/o Orchestrator , which only allows a fixed workflow during a campaign, respectively. In MADDIE w/o Orchestrator , we set the workflow such that each agent is invoked sequentially in this order: HitIdentifier ,

LeadOptimizer , Evaluator , then Synthesizer . The workflow for MADDIE 

w/o Orchestrator is repeated until the requested number of satisfying molecules is obtained. We do not remove HitIdentifier as it serves as the starting point of a campaign and Evaluator as every candidate molecule in MADDIE is required to pass that worker agent. # 5 Discussions 

We discuss the current limitations of MADDIE and highlight several potential directions for future work. 

In vitro validation for MADDIE ’s generated molecules 

MADDIE is an autonomous multi-agent system for in silico drug discovery— with dedicated agents equipped with state-of-the-art in silico tools to generate, optimize, evaluate, and plan the synthesis of molecules. MADDIE has demonstrated its abil-ity to generate molecules that satisfy requirements grounded in these tools. However, such computational tools remain imperfect proxies for laboratory synthesis and in vitro experiments, which are indispensable next steps to validate the real-world poten-tial of molecules generated by MADDIE . Despite their importance, both synthesis and in vitro experimentation remain challenging to perform systematically and at scale [8]. This motivates future research into how to better scale and incorporate in vitro validation into MADDIE . In anticipation of such validation, MADDIE pro-vides synthesis routes, predicted binding affinity, and ADMET profiling for molecules 18 it generates, providing extensive information needed to prioritize which candidates to advance. Furthermore, the results from in vitro experimentation can also be provided back to MADDIE , enabling the system to adapt its workflow and revise its strategy to produce molecules more likely to advance in a real-world drug discovery campaign. 

Human-in-the-loop design for MADDIE 

Domain experts are essential for successful drug discovery—a domain where reliable safety assessments is the highest priority and in-depth understanding is required. In this study, we couple MADDIE ’s evaluation with assessments from a domain expert, serving primarily to verify the quality of the generated molecules, rather than to drive the generation process itself. A natural extension for MADDIE is integrating domain experts’ feedback directly into the system, enabling an interactive framework in which expert judgments can steer the generation process toward more desirable regions of chemical space. The current design for MADDIE is well-positioned to integrate this feedback: because Orchestrator already maintains an evolving repository of can-didates and decides which to pursue, expert judgments can be supplied as additional information for Orchestrator to shape its decision. For instance, domain experts can flag undesirable substructures and scaffolds on grounds that current in silico tools do not capture, or indicate preferences among a set of candidates, thus steering the campaign towards more desirable molecules. More importantly, such feedback need not be time-consuming: a domain expert can provide occasional feedback while still meaningfully shaping the drug discovery campaign. Through a human-in-the-loop design, MADDIE can improve its reliability of producing molecules likely to advance in a real-world drug discovery campaign (e.g., in vitro validation), while serving as a collaborator for domain experts (rather than a system that can only be inspected).  

> MADDIE

as a fully autonomous and self-driving system with laboratory integration 

A more ambitious direction is to integrate automated laboratory platforms directly into MADDIE , enabling a fully autonomous, self-driving drug discovery system. Such a system allows MADDIE to synthesize molecules on automated synthesis platforms and submit the synthesized molecules for in vitro validation, closing the loop between 

in silico design and in vitro validation. Synthesizer and Evaluator are the natural integration points, by equipping both agents with automated synthesis and in vitro 

validation platforms, respectively. Orchestrator can also serve as another point of integration. Since synthesis is costly and time-consuming, this makes candidate selec-tion a resource allocation problem (e.g., selecting only the most promising molecules to synthesize under a fixed budget), a decision that requires a component with a global-level view of a campaign, which Orchestrator provides. Only molecules 

Orchestrator deems satisfactory should proceed to synthesis, with synthetic feasi-bility from Synthesizer and predicted properties from Evaluator grounding that decision. Several safeguards must be implemented before a fully autonomous self-driving system can be deployed responsibly. A system that acts in the physical world intro-duces safety concerns absent from a system that works purely in silico . For instance, 19 a molecule deemed unsatisfactory in silico can simply be discarded, whereas a synthe-sized molecule must be handled, stored, and disposed of responsibly. Safeguards are also needed to ensure molecules generated by MADDIE are not hazardous, whether due to error or misuse of the system. Such safeguards may include structural screening against molecules known to be toxic and hazardous and including medicinal chemists in the critical step of laboratory testing. Even so, the potential benefits are substan-tial. Grounding MADDIE ’s decisions in laboratory feedback can let the system steer its workflow toward real-world outcomes, and the resulting laboratory measurements can accumulate into data well suited to improving MADDIE . More broadly, such a system can shift the role of agents in drug discovery from informing medicinal chemists to driving the drug discovery process itself, with the potential to substantially reduce the time and cost of bringing new medicine to patients. 

# 6 Conclusions 

We present MADDIE , an autonomous multi-agent system for in silico drug discov-ery capable of generating molecules with satisfying properties for drug development. Rather than executing a fixed workflow, MADDIE decides which worker agent to invoke through Orchestrator , drawing on its memory module and evolving reposi-tories of candidates for its decision-making. Across 50 targets, MADDIE outperforms state-of-the-art baselines spanning generative models for drug design, agents for drug discovery, and generalist LLMs, successfully generating 100 satisfying molecules per target that meet key molecular property requirements while also providing their syn-thesis routes. Our ablation reveals that this performance is attributed to MADDIE ’s collaboration between agents, where each worker agent contributes distinctly, while 

Orchestrator contributes the most by concentrating effort on the most promis-ing candidates that greatly reduce the chemical space MADDIE must explore to find the satisfying molecules. Consistent with its design, MADDIE exhibits strong adaptability across campaigns, effectively employing different strategies as each tar-get demands. Notably, MADDIE demonstrates intelligent strategy adaptations for a challenging target with no existing marketed FDA-approved drugs. On PSEN1, a critical target associated with Alzheimer’s disease, MADDIE generates PSEN1-01, a promising molecule with in silico properties outperforming those of the reference ligand and Nirogacestat, while also demonstrating favorable binding interaction with the challenging site of the target. MADDIE achieves strong performance at low cost and time, with one campaign roughly producing 21K output tokens and taking 3.3 hours on average. We envision MADDIE as a foundation for future autonomous drug discovery systems that can carry a molecule in silico to potential drug candidates, ultimately accelerating the timeline and cost of bringing new drugs to patients. 

# 7 Methods 

We introduce MADDIE , a multi-agent system for in silico drug discovery. It is com-posed of a central agent, Orchestrator , and four specialized worker agents. Given a target protein and a set of desired molecular properties, MADDIE aims to identify 20 molecules that bind to the target and satisfy all desired properties. MADDIE per-forms the task by distributing subtasks across worker agents, with Orchestrator 

deciding which subtask to pursue next. This design enables MADDIE to decompose a complex, multi-objective problem typical in drug discovery into manageable subtasks, enabling each agent to collectively advance the drug discovery campaign. 

Orchestrator (Section 7.1) is responsible for the entire workflow of MADDIE ,deciding how a drug discovery campaign proceeds by delegating to a worker agent and processing the molecules. Under its coordination, the worker agents perform distinct subtasks critical to a successful drug discovery campaign [61]: (1) HitIdentifier 

(Section 7.2.1) serves as the entry point of a campaign, providing candidate molecules from which a campaign proceeds; (2) LeadOptimizer (Section 7.2.2) serves as the refinement stage, advancing existing candidate molecules towards those with satisfy-ing properties; (3) Synthesizer (Section 7.2.3) bridges the gap between molecules designed in silico and those that can be made in practice, proposing synthesis routes and finding synthesizable analogs; and (4) Evaluator (Section 7.2.4) functions as a quality control layer, assessing the key properties that define a successful drug, includ-ing binding affinity, drug-likeness, and safety-related properties. Acting in concert, these components allow MADDIE to emulate the iterative, collaborative nature of a real-world campaign within a single autonomous system [61]. Each agent ( Orchestrator and the four worker agents) maintains its own 

workspace , each is used to store relevant artifacts required for their tasks (Table 6). The workspaces are dynamically changing and growing continuously, with more artifacts (e.g., molecules) generated as the campaign progresses. Each worker agent operates within its own workspace, with no access to the others. This prevents any alteration of artifacts from worker agents outside their own workspace, preventing error propa-gation throughout MADDIE . Meanwhile, Orchestrator alone has access to each worker agent’s workspace, granting it a global view required to lead the campaign. 

## 7.1 Orchestrator 

At the center of MADDIE is Orchestrator , an agent responsible for the decision-making of the entire drug discovery campaign. Orchestrator decides how acampaign proceeds: which worker agent to call next and which candidate molecules to focus on. Each decision is conditioned on two sources of information: (1) the existing candidate molecules generated throughout the campaign, which captures the progress of the campaign, and (2) the profiles of the worker agents (Appendix E.2), which describe the capabilities that each worker agent can contribute to the campaign. Together, these information allow Orchestrator to reason jointly about what the campaign needs and which worker agent is best suited to provide it. Furthermore, this information enables dynamic decision-making for Orchestrator , choosing which drug discovery step to focus on next and calling the worker agent dedicated to that step. This design makes MADDIE ’s campaign non-linear and adaptive, mirroring the iterative, feedback-driven nature of real-world drug discovery [61]. Operationally, Orchestrator makes two kinds of decisions. It can choose to advance the campaign by issuing a delegate action to a worker agent, or to collect information (e.g., the properties of existing candidate molecules) by issuing a process 

21 action. (1) Using a delegate action, Orchestrator sends a worker agent a prompt consisting of a task description and references to the artifacts stored in each worker agent’s workspace (Section 7.2). In return, it receives a structured report of what the worker agent has done. These reports, along with the information gathered from 

process action(s) and the profiles of worker agents will determine which worker agent should be called next via delegate . (1) Using a process action, Orchestrator reads the artifacts produced from the worker agent it has just called. What it takes from them depends on the worker agent: the molecules generated by HitIdentifier or modified by LeadOptimizer , the property values computed by Evaluator , or the synthesis routes found by Synthesizer . From these artifacts it updates its view of which candidate molecules meet the requirements and which of them still fall short. With these two actions, Orchestrator drives the campaign forward (through 

delegate) while continually grounding its decision (through process ). Carrying these actions out over many steps rests on two elements of its design: (1) the memory that keeps its own record tractable and (2) the satisfying candidate repositories in which it keeps the molecules worth pursuing, stored in the workspace. 

Memory module 

A defining feature of Orchestrator is its capability to sustain a long-horizon cam-paign for MADDIE (Section 4.3.3). This is enabled by a dedicated memory module that dynamically stores the most relevant information. The module holds the records of the campaign itself—the information of the artifacts produced, the reports the worker agents return and Orchestrator ’s own prior decisions—kept in full. Other information is stored and pruned to the most recent entries, such as system-level messages generated throughout the campaign (e.g., Orchestrator calling a worker agent with incorrect format). When the memory module exceeds what the underlying LLM can hold in its context, the module compresses its records by summarizing them, selectively storing and pruning information based on its recency and relevance. The module thus provides Orchestrator a compact, decision-relevant context, allowing it to condition each decision on the salient history of the campaign while remaining within the effective reasoning capacity of the underlying LLM. 

Satisfying candidate repositories 

Over the course of a campaign, Orchestrator maintains two repositories of molecules. The first repository P holds the candidate molecules that satisfy every property evaluated by Evaluator . Concretely, these are molecules from 

HitIdentifier and LeadOptimizer that pass each property requirement. The sec-ond repository S holds candidate molecules that both satisfy every property and are also synthesizable as verified by Synthesizer . This set has two sources: (1) 

molecules from P that are synthesizable, and (2) their synthesizable analogs (Section 7.2.3) found by Synthesizer that satisfy every property as evaluated by Evaluator .

Orchestrator takes both repositories into account when choosing each subsequent action. These repositories serve several purposes: P tracks which candidate molecules are worth considering for synthesis assessments, while S tracks which of them are both satisfying and also synthesizable. ## 7.2 Worker Agents 

Each worker agent carries out one specialized subtask in a drug discovery cam-paign, each critical for a successful campaign. Each worker agent maintains astatic profile, containing a brief summary of its capabilities and what subtask they support (Appendix E.2). These profiles are used by Orchestrator during its decision-making. Each worker agent receives from Orchestrator a prompt containing a task description with references to the artifacts in its workspace, executes the subtask with its own tools and stores the artifacts it produces in its workspace. The worker agent then issues a report by providing a structured summary of what it has done back to 

Orchestrator . Table 6 lists the artifacts each worker agent requires and produces in its workspace and the tools used. The subsections below describe the strategy with which each worker agent approaches its subtask. 23 7.2.1 HitIdentifier 

HitIdentifier serves as the entry point of MADDIE ’s campaign, supplying the candidate molecules from which the rest of the campaign proceeds. It is the only worker agent that conditions the molecule generation directly on the target struc-ture, enabling MADDIE to propose molecules likely to bind the target. Similar to hit identification step in drug discovery, HitIdentifier prioritizes binding affinity, a key property for molecules to have the intended therapeutic effects [66]. Every molecule MADDIE proposes is derived (e.g., optimized) from what HitIdentifier 

finds, emphasizing its importance. 

HitIdentifier first receives a subtask from Orchestrator containing the ref-erences to artifacts in its workspace (e.g., target structure), a number of molecules to return and a budget of iterations to find them. HitIdentifier then finds these molecules over a series of iterations using two strategies: structure-based drug design (SBDD) [67] and ligand-based drug design (LBDD) [68]. At each iteration, it selects a strategy, invokes the corresponding tool, and accumulates the resulting molecules alongside those from previous iterations. The molecules are then scored using their predicted binding affinities, stored in its workspace, and accumulated to guide the selection of the next strategy. HitIdentifier stops once the molecules satisfying the constraints reaches or exceeds the requested number from Orchestrator or it exhausts the budget of iterations. 

Structure-based drug design 

SBDD generates molecules conditioned on the three-dimensional structure of the tar-get pocket. It enables HitIdentifier to design molecules by exploiting the binding pocket’s spatial configurations, offering a mechanistic approach to molecular discovery grounded in pocket-ligand interactions. SBDD further provides a principled starting point for ligand-based modeling, or LBDD, and thus complements each other. 

Ligand-based drug design 

LBDD instead generates molecules conditioned on the shape of one that already binds to the pocket. Where SBDD generates molecules from the binding pocket, LBDD expands around the known binding molecules. Since it relies on different prior information (e.g., shape-conditioned instead of pocket-conditioned), LBDD broadens 

HitIdentifier ’s exploration when combined with SBDD and thus, further increases the likelihood to find more and stronger binding molecules. 

7.2.2 LeadOptimizer 

LeadOptimizer is the molecular-refinement component of MADDIE . It comple-ments the molecular generative stages as done by HitIdentifier (e.g., generates candidates from a binding pocket) and Synthesizer (e.g., generates synthesizable analogs from existing candidates). Specifically, their candidate molecules are used as starting points by LeadOptimizer to produce derivatives with improved properties. 

LeadOptimizer addresses the different property requirements a campaign requires through two different families of optimizer: (1) pretrained controllable multi-property 24 optimizer [9] and (2) oracle-guided single-property optimizer [63], each is distinct and complements each other. They provide both adaptability and control over the prop-erty improvements to whatever a campaign demands, enabling LeadOptimizer to transform molecules in MADDIE to those with satisfying properties. 

LeadOptimizer performs a subtask by first formulating a plan and then select-ing the most suitable optimizer. Concretely, after Orchestrator identifies which candidate molecules are needed to improve, what properties require improvement, and the number of derivatives to produce for each molecule, LeadOptimizer turns the request into a structured plan with objectives. It contains both properties to be improved and the required values or ranges. The plan then guides LeadOptimizer to select the best optimizer from one of two families, based on which properties to opti-mize, how many of them, and the performance of that optimizer, when such records are stored by LeadOptimizer . Once an optimizer is selected, LeadOptimizer applies it to each candidate molecule, producing molecule derivatives that are then vali-dated, de-duplicated, stored in its workspace, and reported back to Orchestrator .If the initial attempt doesn’t achieve the requested property improvement(s) or pro-duce enough valid derivatives, LeadOptimizer may run follow-up attempts including retrying or switching optimizers, subject to an execution budget. 

Pretrained controllable multi-property optimizer 

The pretrained controllable multi-property optimizer family is well suited when the subtask requires improving multiple properties while attempting to preserve other desirable molecular properties. This family offers LeadOptimizer greater con-trol over which properties to maintain and improve. In contrast to using multiple single-property optimizers (the second family), a pretrained multi-property optimizer provides more balanced improvement (e.g., improving while maintaining) and effi-ciency (e.g., one instead of multiple runs). However, because this family is often pretrained on specific properties [9], it limits their adaptability for the wide range of properties a drug discovery campaign may require, necessitating LeadOptimizer to use another family of optimizers. 

Oracle-guided single-property optimizer 

Oracle-guided single-property optimizers refine molecules using an in silico property predictor available to LeadOptimizer (Table 6). This family is primarily used when the properties to optimize are not supported by the first family. LeadOptimizer has access to more than one optimizer in this family. To select the best one, it uses its available property predictors, prior optimization results, and historical performance records of each tool as guidance (Appendix E.3). 

7.2.3 Synthesizer 

Synthesizer investigates the synthesis of molecules, assessing whether the candidate molecules in MADDIE can be made in practice. This is critical for in silico drug discovery, as synthesizability is a major bottleneck to development of AI-generated molecules [69]. Synthesizer assesses synthesizability based on whether it can gener-ate a synthesis route for a given molecule, grounding its assessments in interpretable 25 reaction chemistry rather than relying solely on estimated synthetic accessibility scores [39]. Synthesizer uses two complementary strategies to generate synthesis routes: (1) retrosynthesis planning [64], and (2) synthesizable analog generation [65]. This two-strategy approach mirrors conventional practice in drug discovery, where molecules that are difficult to synthesize directly are used to identify analogs from established libraries of synthesizable molecules. 

Retrosynthesis planning 

Synthesizer uses retrosynthesis planning to generate synthesis routes by starting from a given candidate molecule and working through the reverse logic of synthesis reactions to reach purchasable starting materials. Using this approach, Synthesizer 

can identify synthesis routes to a specific molecule, mimicking the retrosynthetic anal-yses performed by chemists in conventional drug discovery. If this planning process can identify a route for a given molecule, Synthesizer uses the route as the evidence to conclude that the molecule is synthesizable and terminates. However, if no route is found, Synthesizer investigates further using the synthesizable analog generation strategy. 

Synthesizable analog generation 

In synthesizable analog generation, Synthesizer identifies analogs by constructing synthesis routes to molecules that are structurally similar to the given candidate molecule. Unlike retrosynthesis planning, the route generation starts from purchasable starting materials and works through the forward logic of synthesis reactions to pro-duce analogs. This forward-synthesis approach naturally complements retrosynthesis planning, and can uncover synthesis routes that retrosynthesis planning may strug-gle to identify. In some cases, the product of the generated synthesis route is not an analog, but the given candidate molecule itself. When this occurs, Synthesizer 

concludes that the candidate molecule is synthesizable. Otherwise, Synthesizer con-cludes that the candidate molecule is unsynthesizable and provides the analogs and their routes to Orchestrator as potential alternatives to the candidate molecule. Thus, Synthesizer goes beyond synthesizability assessment, converting unsynthe-sizable molecules into potentially promising new leads to further the drug discovery campaign. 

7.2.4 Evaluator 

Evaluator serves as the quality control mechanism in MADDIE . Every deci-sion made by Orchestrator regarding the candidate molecules is grounded in 

Evaluator ’s assessments, thus shaping the trajectory of MADDIE ’s campaign. 

Evaluator performs a comprehensive assessment of each candidate molecule and supplies the property values on which the rest of a campaign depends. The prop-erties Evaluator supports include binding affinity, drug-likeness, synthesizability, ADMET and structural liabilities. Each is critical to a successful drug and com-puted with its own respective tool (Table 6). Its synthesizability assessment differs from Synthesizer since Evaluator uses a rapid heuristic scorer [39] rather than 26 finding an entire synthesis route from purchasable starting molecules to the candi-date molecule. The score complements the synthesis route—it can convey information on the ease of synthesis [39] as a quantitative number that can help in prioritizing molecules.

# Ethics Statement 

MADDIE is an autonomous system for drug discovery, enabling users to generate molecules that meet the specified property requirements prompted in natural lan-guage. We acknowledge that not all generated molecules are safe, and MADDIE 

can potentially be used to generate harmful molecules. We take a step to minimize the potential negative impact and design MADDIE to operate purely in silico , with no capability for the system to execute synthesis and produce physical molecules. Nonetheless, we highly encourage supervision by responsible domain experts when using MADDIE . All users of MADDIE are advised to exercise discretion and to com-ply with applicable safety guidelines, regulations, and established professional best practices.

# A Dataset and Annotations 

We provide the protocols when curating our dataset here. 

Protein targets . First, we curate a list of targets from LIDDIA [11], Cross-Docked2020 [35] and OpenTargets [36], ensuring that these targets don’t exist in CrossDocked2020 training set. We then annotate the targets with disease associa-tions through cross-referencing with OpenTargets [36] and GeneCards [70]. For the 3D target structures, we first identify their experimentally validated structures with a small molecule ligand from RCSB Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) [37] and extract their binding pockets using the ligands as references. The annotations for the protein targets are provided in Table A1. The disease categories column indicates whether the target is associated with autoimmune disease (A), cancer (CA), cardio-vascular disease (CV), Diabetes (D), Infectious Disease (I), or Neurological Conditions (N). 

Approved FDA drugs . We curate a set of approved drugs associated with the targets, starting from ChEMBL [71] phase 4 target-mechanism records and DrugCen-tral [72] records. The drugs are then mapped to FDA Drugs@FDA active ingredients using openFDA [73], filtered to currently marketed products (as of July 31, 2026), and collapsed to one active-family representative per target. This process identifies cur-rent marketed FDA-approved small-molecule drug associations for 22 of the targets in our dataset. Table A2 lists the associated drugs with targets in our dataset. 

# B Property Requirements 

We use our curated FDA-approved drugs to find the thresholds used in the experi-ments. We set the thresholds according to two criteria: (1) at least 80% of reference drugs pass each individual property requirement and (2) at least 40% of reference drugs pass all the requirements simultaneously. The first criterion ensures that no sin-gle property requirement is too stringent, while the second criterion guards against the joint requirement becoming too unrealistic when all requirements are imposed together. We calculate the thresholds in two stages. For each property, we first set an initial threshold such that (almost) 80% of the reference drugs pass that requirement in isolation. This leads to less than 30% of the reference drugs passing all the require-ments. We therefore calibrate these thresholds manually, relaxing them as needed so that each property still retains the per-property pass rate of around 80% while the fraction of drugs passing all of them is at least 40%. # D Baseline Implementation 

For each baseline here, we use their publicly available implementations. We provide the parameters and settings of each baseline in more details in this section. 

## D.1 Agents for Drug Discovery 

To ensure fairness across methods for experiments: (1) we use Qwen3-30B [45] as their underlying LLM, similar to MADDIE , and (2) we replace some of the compu-tational evaluation tools to align with what is used for experimental evaluation. More implementation details for each agent baseline is provided below. 

LIDDIA . We use the publicly available code for implementation in https://github.com/ninglab/LIDDiA. We replace the docking module in the Evalua-tor with UniDock [6]. We set the number of iterations to 30 to yield similar number of intermediate molecules as MADDIE .

Prompt-to-Pill . We use the publicly available code for implementation in https://github.com/ChatMED/Prompt-to-Pill. We replace some the ChemFM model used to compute ADMET properties and the docking module with ADMET-AI [7] and UniDock [6], respectively. 

AgentD . We use the publicly available code for implementation in https://github.com/hoon-ock/AgentD. The agent is also configured to use Qwen3-Embedding-4B embedding model. For each of the 50 protein targets, the baseline starts with the protein name and associated disease. These pairings were derived from the MADDIE ’s dataset annotations including OpenTargets [36] and GeneCards [70] disease associations as described in Appendix A. Each run is set to produce up to 400 molecules in the initial pool. This setting is selected to yield a comparable interme-diate molecule pool size to MADDIE . We use two refinement iterations consistent with paper-reproduction workflow, and we apply filter thresholds of minimum QED to 0.36 and predicted affinity pKd to 5.0. Runs that fail to identify a usable ligand seed or target SMILES are retried using an alternate relevant disease association. DeepPK [74] failures caused by invalid input or service delays are handled through documented checkpoint recoveries where invalid or fragmented input are removed, 39 while DeepPK requests are serialized and given a longer polling period. Other adjust-ments to the main AgentD pipeline include disabling paid Serper web-search service and Boltz structure prediction for this testing setup. 

## D.2 Generative Models for Drug Design 

We provide the parameters for generative models for drug design here. 

Pocket2Mol . To implement Pocket2Mol [26], we use the publicly available code and pretrained checkpoint provided by the authors at https://github.com/pengxingang/Pocket2Mol. Pocket2Mol generates molecules autoregressively, so a sampling run terminates as soon as its beam is exhausted, and the number of molecules obtained per pocket cannot be specified directly. Since many candidates either terminate early or fail chemical reconstruction, the authors’ default settings yielded fewer valid molecules than required for our evaluation. To match the setting of MADDIE , we therefore enlarged the sampling budget: we increase the beam size from 300 to 1,000, the maximum number of generation steps from 50 to 100, and the requested number of samples from 100 to 2000. For all other parameters, we use the default settings provided by the authors. 

TargetDiff . To implement TargetDiff [27], we use the publicly available code and pretrained checkpoint provided by the authors at https://github.com/guanjq/targetdiff. Because TargetDiff samples each molecule independently from the diffusion prior, the generated number of molecules per pocket can be requested directly, and only this quantity is adjusted: we increase it from 100 to 2000 so that enough chemically valid molecules remained per pocket after reconstruction. All generative parameters follows default values provided by authors. 

conDitar . To implement conDitar [10], we use the publicly avail-able code and pretrained checkpoint provided by the authors at https://github.com/ninglab/conDitar-dev. As with TargetDiff, the only parameter we change is the number of generated samples. We increase it from 100 to 2000 for the same purpose of retaining enough chemically valid molecules per pocket after reconstruction. 

DiffSMol . To implement DiffSMol [8], we use the publicly available code and pre-trained checkpoints provided by the authors at https://github.com/ninglab/DiffSMol. DiffSMol is primarily a shape-conditioned model, and by default it samples without information about the protein pocket. We use the ligands associated with the pockets in our dataset as inputs to DiffSMol. To make it comparable to the structure-based methods, we enable its pocket guidance mode with a pocket threshold of 0.5. We also increase the number of generated samples from 100 to 2000. All remaining settings follow the released configuration. 

ELILLM . To implement ELILLM [31], we use the publicly available code provided by the authors at https://github.com/hxnhxn/ELILLM. ELILLM has two settings: ELILLM-rand, which explores starting from a random sampling of the chemical space, and ELILLM-diff, which explores starting from the output of a diffusion-based SBDD model. In our experiments, we report results from ELILLM-rand as a reflection of its intrinsic ability to explore the chemical space without bias towards a specific SBDD model’s results. To match the setting of MADDIE , we prompt ELILLM to generate 40 100 molecules per pocket. To maximize the probability of ELILLM’s success, we allow up to 2,000 iterations of generation, which is more generous than the original 500 specified by the ELILLM authors. This setting allows for up to 10,000 opportunities for ELILLM to generate satisfying molecules. We also set its docking timeout to 7,200 seconds (increased from 1,800) to accommodate setting the number of docking CPUs to 8 (decreased from 30). For all other parameters, we use the settings provided by the authors.

# E MADDIE ’s Implementation 

Orchestrator is given a budget of 100 actions per campaign to ensure sufficient budget for a long-horizon campaign. Its memory module compacts the working mem-ory once it exceeds 32K tokens, about half the context window. Once fewer than 8 actions remain, Orchestrator stops searching for new molecules and delegates the 41 molecules in P to Synthesizer . When it delegates to LeadOptimizer , it at most forwards the 40 candidate molecules that fail the fewest requirements. Per delegation, HitIdentifier is given a budget of 3–10 iterations and samples 100 molecules at each of them. LeadOptimizer produces 10 derivatives per candi-date molecule and is allowed at most two optimizer executions per delegation, the second used only to retry or to switch optimizers when the first returns too few valid derivatives. Synthesizer allows 30 seconds of retrosynthesis search per molecule, under a limit of 500 iterations, and returns up to five synthesizable analogs for each molecule for which no route is found. Evaluator computes the seven property val-ues listed in Table 1 (except Syn ) for every molecule it receives. We use different tools for predicting binding affinity in HitIdentifier (AutoDock [62]) and Evaluator 

(UniDock [6]). Evaluator requires fast computation for docking, which UniDock sup-ports (e.g., via GPU). In contrast, HitIdentifier only requires a tool that predicts affinity given a molecule pose (e.g., without docking). More implementation details, including Orchestrator ’s prompts and the worker agent profiles, are provided here. 

## E.1 Orchestrator Prompt 

E.1.1 Orchestrator System Prompt 

You are MADDIE, the orchestrator of a multi-agent drug discovery system. You PLAN, DELEGATE, and MONITOR. You never perform specialized analysis yourself -- it happens only through delegation to specialists. Your context lists the available agents and their capabilities, your Actions, and any Automatons. Always consult it before delegating; do not assume an agent's inputs, formats, or limits -- read its description. Each agent only accesses its own directory, so files you pass must already exist there. A shared database holds all molecules, targets, and data. Automatons are automatic scripts you never call; one may append output inside <AUTOMATON name>...</AUTOMATON>. Use it when it appears. Follow any rules, constraints, or warnings inside <TASK_SET>...</TASK_SET>. # Judge each action by what it adds A valid action produces something you didn't already have: (a) NEW molecules in your pool, (b) a measurement, pass/fail, or fact about the pool that informs your next move, or (c) a routing decision that unblocks the next step.
