# ABSTRACT 

Fast, high-fidelity molecular generation requires accurate chemical choices and efficient sampling. Chemical building blocks reduce graph size, but make each node a choice among tens of thousands of related identities. We introduce MIRRA (Molecular Identity Representation, Refresh and Assembly), a discrete flow-matching generator that learns from chemical knowledge and evaluates identity probabilities only when needed, while keeping every identity in its vocabulary available. Chemical features share information across related blocks, and learned identity corrections distinguish them in molecular context. By deciding which positions will refresh before computing their probabilities, the sampler avoids unused predictions while preserving each sampling transition. Against the strongest baselines in each task, MIRRA reduces chemical and graph distribution errors on ZINC250k by 29–31% and 53–60%, respectively, with generation up to 8.6 times faster. Property targeting yields an estimated 7.8–20.8 times more distinct novel hits per second. On a held-out polymer test set, MIRRA achieves 100% periodic validity with known chain topology, versus 57.0% for a polymer variational autoencoder. These results demonstrate fast, high-fidelity generation with the full chemical vocabulary available throughout sampling. 

# 1 Introduction 

Molecules reuse chemistry. Rings and functional groups recur across compounds, so selecting one building block can specify several atoms and bonds at once. This makes substructure-based generation attractive: it reduces the number of graph variables while preserving chemically meaningful units (Degen et al., 2008; Jin et al., 2018; Han et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026). Compression of the graph, however, relocates the difficulty to the individual nodes. Instead of choosing among a few atom types, a model must predict an identity from tens of thousands of building blocks, each in the context of a different partially formed molecule. Two requirements follow. First, the predicted probabilities must reflect both chemical similarity and individual difference. Two aromatic blocks may share useful structural information while differing in ring size, heteroatoms or attachment pattern, and these differences can determine which surrounding structures they fit. Second, evaluating those probabilities must remain affordable, since the vocabulary is scored at every position of every sampling step; a large vocabulary can otherwise erase the computational benefit of the smaller graph. We ask: can a generator retain detailed chemical choices and still be fast? MIRRA (Molecular Identity Representation, Refresh and Assembly) answers by combining chemistry-informed prediction with refresh scheduling (Figure 1). Every identity in its vocabulary remains available throughout generation. The representation assigns each block a probability from two terms: fixed chemical features shared across related entries, and learned per-entry corrections that depend on molecular context. The refresh schedule fixes which positions will be resampled before any scoring occurs, so only those positions evaluate the full vocabulary while the complete graph still supplies context. # 2 Related work 

2.1 Molecular generation 

GDSS, DiGress, GruM and SID generate graphs through diffusion and iterative denoising (Jo et al., 2022; Vignac et al., 2023; Jo et al., 2024; Boget, 2025). SyCo maps molecular graphs to synthetic Euclidean coordinates and generates in that latent space (Ketata et al., 2025). GraphBFN evolves continuous beliefs over graph categories (Song et al., 2025), while HDFM and HOG-Diff organize generation coarse-to-fine (Boget et al., 2026; Huang and Birdal, 2026). For property control, STGG+ combines conditional spanning-tree generation with self-criticism (Jolicoeur-Martineau et al., 2025), while TreeDiff guides diffusion through inference-time tree search (Zhao et al., 2026). MIRRA builds on discrete flow matching (Gat et al., 2024), with chemically structured output sharing and refresh scheduling that preserves the specified finite-step transition. For conditional generation, it measures useful molecular yield as distinct novel target hits per unit time. Fragment-based generators pair compact molecular graphs with assembly. The junction-tree VAE generates substructure scaffolds and assembles molecules, while HierVAE predicts larger motifs and their attachments (Jin et al., 2018; 2020). Functional-group coarse-graining provides chemically meaningful graph nodes (Han et al., 2025), and NSGGM combines neural scaffold proposals with symbolic constraint solving for final assembly (Geng et al., 2026). FragFM learns fragment embeddings, predicts identities through contrastive fragment bags, and resolves attachments by constrained matching (Lee et al., 2026). SynFormer generates synthetic pathways and selects building blocks by generating molecular fingerprints and retrieving nearby entries from a database (Gao et al., 2025). MIRRA assigns an explicit probability to every fragment identity at each refresh and uses terminal probabilities to select a connection tree and compatible attachment pairs. Section 4.2 compares full-vocabulary execution with a prior-sampled candidate subset, isolating how restricting the available identities affects fidelity and cost. Sharing information across output identities provides a complementary approach. Label Embedding Network learns relationships between labels (Sun et al., 2017); deep residual output layers share learned label mappings in language generation (Pappas and Henderson, 2019); and LGSA aligns hierarchical label geometry for text classification (Zhang et al., 2026). MIRRA combines fixed chemical output features with context-dependent identity corrections and trains probabilities over the full vocabulary. Chemical structure shares learning across molecular identities, while learned corrections express their differences in context. Matched training controls test chemical alignment, and generation/assembly interventions identify how the corrections improve fidelity. 

2.2 Efficient discrete sampling 

Structured discrete diffusion supports categorical and absorbing-state corruption (Austin et al., 2021). MELD learns element-specific corruption schedules to reduce collisions between molecular diffusion trajectories (Seo et al., 2026), while Categorical Flow Maps distill flows for generation in fewer steps (Roos et al., 2026). MDLM and RADD accelerate absorbing diffusion by caching time-independent network outputs while the partially revealed sequence remains unchanged (Sahoo et al., 2024; Ou et al., 2025). First-hitting samplers directly sample unmasking times (Zheng et al., 2025); EDGE and DeFoG study sparse active-node and adaptive graph sampling (Chen et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2025). DDPD separates a learned planner that selects positions from a denoiser that predicts their values (Liu et al., 2025). MIRRA selects refresh positions from prediction-independent Bernoulli gates, allowing this decision to precede identity scoring while preserving the finite-step kernel. Identities can refresh repeatedly, and full-vocabulary probabilities are computed only at selected positions. The time-conditioned backbone still processes each active graph, so the main saving comes from avoiding unused vocabulary projections. The harmonic count HT measures per-node refresh evaluations; absorbing-diffusion caching counts whole-network evaluations between changes to the sequence. 

2.3 Polymer generation 

BigSMILES describes stochastic macromolecular structures (Lin et al., 2019), and G 2RINS provides a generative string-and-graph representation of hierarchical polymers (Zaldivar et al., 2026). HiPoly builds on G 2RINS for polymer property prediction and generative design (Sun et al., 2026). The Open Macromolecular Genome links repeat-unit generation to polymerization chemistry (Kim et al., 2023), while Graph DiT studies multi-property-conditioned polymer generation (Liu et al., 2024). MIRRA extends chemically structured prediction to periodic repeat units and evaluates structural fidelity and periodic validity on a reserved polymer split with known chain topology. 3MIRRA: Fast, High-Fidelity Molecular Generation 

# 3 MIRRA: representation, refresh and assembly 

3.1 Representation: chemically structured identities 

A molecule is represented by building-block (molecular fragment) identities X = ( x1, . . . , x n) and a symmetric binary adjacency matrix E. Blocks expose typed attachment sites obtained from BRICS-derived decompositions (Degen et al., 2008), while their identities specify internal atoms and bonds. The generator therefore predicts only the block graph. Distinct substitution and attachment patterns remain distinct choices. The benchmark vocabulary contains 37,821 entries; its finite size also bounds the chemical space this representation expresses. Each entry v has a fixed vector sv describing chemical structure and attachments. A graph transformer reads these features, the noisy edges and the time to produce a contextual state zi for each node, and the model scores every entry as

The model is trained with cross-entropy over probabilities normalized across the full vocabulary. The two terms serve complementary purposes. 3.2 Refresh: scoring only at scheduled positions 

For T sampling steps, we use the grid tk = k/T , k = 0 , . . . , T − 1. The finite-step node transition is

T − k . (3) The transition is a mixture of retaining the current identity and drawing a new one from the model, and a conventional implementation evaluates pθ,i before sampling from it. The mixture weight αk, however, is fixed by the schedule and independent of the prediction, so the two operations may be reordered and the Bernoulli refresh drawn first. When the refresh is zero the identity is retained, and its vocabulary probabilities cannot affect the transition, so they need not be computed; when it is one, all 37,821 entries are scored and an identity is sampled, which may coincide with the current one. Edges are governed by analogous gates, all predictions use the incoming graph, and updates are simultaneous. Because the gate is independent of the prediction, this ordering preserves Equation 3 in real arithmetic, and expected full-vocabulary projections per node decrease from T to HT = PTj=1 1/j , approximately 6.1 instead of 250. Measured refresh counts agree with this prediction (Appendix B.5). Early refreshes are sparse and the final step refreshes every node, while a graph in which nothing refreshes requires no forward pass. Within each active graph, the backbone and edge predictor remain dense; end-to-end speed is measured separately. 

3.3 Assembly: from block graph to molecule 

The assembly decoder converts the sampled building blocks into a connected molecule. It reuses the node and edge probabilities computed at the final sampling transition without a new prediction, and selects connections from the terminal edge probabilities rather than the sampled adjacency. The decoder joins the blocks into a tree and uses every attachment site exactly once; joined sites must also have compatible types. 4.2 Scheduling, not restriction, makes the vocabulary practical 

We run the benchmark checkpoint in three ways at 250 steps: score every entry at every position; score every entry only at scheduled positions; or score only a candidate subset at scheduled positions. The subset contains 1,536 distinct prior-sampled entries plus the current identities, renormalized over that set, and its runtime includes candidate construction. All three modes supply assembly with the same complete terminal scores. Scheduling reduces median time from 131.6 to 43.7 ms per attempt, a 3.0-fold speedup , with nearly identical observed FCD (0.3680 versus 0.3665). The candidate subset behaves differently: its FCD rises to 0.9469 and NSPDK ×10 3

to 0.8780, and at 46.2 ms it is marginally slower than scoring all 37,821 entries under the same schedule (Figure 3), while saving 34.7% of peak allocated tensor memory. For this implementation and checkpoint, restricting the choices therefore yields memory savings rather than speed, at a substantial cost in fidelity (Appendix D). 

Keeping the full vocabulary preserves chemical fidelity. Candidate restriction can preserve almost all valid outputs while changing molecular composition: in the three-model controls, paired comparisons reveal a molecular-size shift before assembly. An elementary argument explains why. # 5 Why chemical sharing and identity corrections work together 

Chemical alignment improves what the model learns. Chemical features could in principle help only by adding parameters. To separate chemical information from parameter count, we independently train predictors with correct or shuffled output-feature assignments, holding input features, individual labels, capacity and the training recipe fixed. Correct alignment improves FCD by 11–16% across three matched training pairs . A narrower shuffle within attachment-type and frequency-bin groups also worsens fidelity, the more informative outcome: the benefit follows not from a structured output space in general, but from one that is chemically correct. At a common final update, MIRRA outperforms both shuffles and a conventional learned predictor on FCD and NSPDK (Table 2), while that predictor has slightly higher novelty (Appendix E). 

Identity corrections guide the generation trajectory. The corrections could in principle act only at the end, where assembly reads the terminal probabilities. We therefore enable them independently during generation and during terminal assembly (Figure 4). Holding full assembly scores fixed, enabling generation corrections changes median FCD from 7.638 to 0.366, with paired reductions of 95.1–95.4%. Assembly-only corrections cannot recover this loss: they raise validity from 80.03% to 88.10% while FCD remains near 7.6. The model therefore uses contextual identity preferences during generation. # 7 Discussion 

MIRRA makes a large chemical vocabulary practical for fast, high-fidelity molecular generation. Chemical features let related building blocks share learning, while identity corrections preserve their differences in molecular context. Refresh scheduling makes these detailed choices affordable: it computes full-vocabulary probabilities only at positions where the transition will use them, preserving the finite-step kernel. Every identity remains available whenever a position refreshes. The experiments connect this design to both better distribution matching and faster generation. Training controls estab-lish the value of chemical alignment, and generation/assembly interventions show that contextual identity preferences guide the trajectory itself. In property targeting, higher throughput yields higher estimated rates of distinct novel hits. This connects generation speed to the practical yield of molecular search. Generation uses a finite vocabulary; broader transfer and candidate-specific training remain open. The central result is that detailed chemical choices can support both fidelity and speed: chemical knowledge improves their probabilities, and refresh scheduling removes the cost of predictions that generation does not use. 9MIRRA: Fast, High-Fidelity Molecular Generation

# A Evaluation protocols 

A.1 ZINC250k benchmark 

ZINC250k is a molecular-generation benchmark derived from the ZINC database (Irwin and Shoichet, 2005). For results evaluated in this study in Table 1 and Figures 2–4, we score valid molecules among the first 10,000 attempted outputs against all 24,887 reference molecules, with reference stereochemistry retained. Failures are not replaced; duplicate valid molecules remain in FCD and NSPDK. Validity divides valid outputs by attempts, uniqueness divides unique identities by valid outputs, and novelty divides unseen unique identities by unique identities against the common training reference. FCD measures ChemNet feature distributions; NSPDK is the squared maximum mean discrepancy of labeled local graph features, reported as MMD 2 × 10 3 (Preuer et al., 2018; Costa and De Grave, 2010; Jo et al., 2022). MIRRA250 reports per-metric medians over three sampling draws of one checkpoint. FCD is 0.3665 (range 0.3514– 0.3745); NSPDK is 0.1537 (0.1530–0.1723). MIRRA25 and FragFM500 use single draws with FCD 0.3766 and 0.5281, giving reductions of 28.7% and 30.6% for MIRRA25 and MIRRA250, respectively. A fixed 10,000-molecule training draw defines the training-data floor: FCD 0.3361 and NSPDK 0.1096. ZINC informed architecture and correction-rank selection; MOSES and the reserved polymer reference assess the fixed design.

B.6 Assembly decoder 

At k = T − 1, every node refreshes. The complete node and edge probabilities evaluated on the final incoming graph are retained for assembly. The decoder receives these probabilities, the sampled terminal identities and the graph size. Terminal edge probabilities supply connection scores for the tree search. 

Attachment-count feasibility. For n > 1, the current identities must expose at least one attachment site per block and 2( n − 1) sites in total (Equation 4). When these requirements are unmet, the decoder attempts at most n identity adjustments. Positions are initially ordered by increasing maximum node probability. Blocks with no attachment sites are considered first; otherwise, the decoder seeks a position whose replacement can balance the total count. It selects the highest-probability identity in the chosen attachment-count group and moves the edited position to the end of the order. The desired count is restricted to 1, . . . , n − 1. If the count requirements remain unmet, the attempt fails before tree search. A single-block output requires no attachment sites and a valid molecule. 

Tree search and site pairing. Connection scores are the terminal edge log-odds, wij = log pEij (1) − log pEij (0) , with probabilities floored at 10 −9 for this calculation. Candidate trees have degrees equal to the attachment counts. A greedy construction first considers edges in decreasing score order, subject to degree limits and cycle avoidance. When this produces a spanning tree, it is placed first in the candidate list. Additional degree-exact trees are obtained from 96 randomized Prüfer-sequence draws, deduplicated and ordered by summed edge score. They are added to the list up to a total of 48 candidates. Each tree is checked in order, stopping at the first successful assembly. For each candidate tree, the decoder enumerates at most 1,000 compatible attachment pairings. The primary policy scores these pairings using training-derived attachment preferences and tests the highest-scoring pairing for valid molecular assembly. The canonical policy instead considers the valid molecules produced by the enumerated pairings and selects the one with the lexicographically smallest canonical SMILES. This policy also defines the alternate assembly arm used in the controls. 

Identity repair and failed attempts. If the attachment counts are feasible but tree assembly is unsuccessful, the decoder tries single-identity replacements at up to three positions with the lowest maximum node probabilities. At each position, it considers up to eight highest-probability identities with the same attachment count, skipping the current identity. Each candidate is tried separately and the first successful replacement is accepted. These repair attempts use canonical assembly, including when the initial search used the attachment prior. Unsuccessful attempts remain failures and are not replaced. # G Property-conditioned generation 

G.1 Adaptation and baseline training MIRRA adaptation. The conditional split contains 207,114 unique training identities and 10,800 validation identities. The property vector holds training-standardized QED (Bickerton et al., 2012) and logP together with a two-entry indicator naming the requested property; the unrequested property’s standardized value and its indicator are both zero. For each of the 12 frozen transformer layers, a separate 4 → 64 → 544 MLP with SiLU activation maps this four-dimensional input to offsets of widths 384/96/64 for the node, edge and graph states, broadcast across valid nodes and edges after that layer’s output. The final adapter projection starts at zero and a missing condition multiplies every offset by zero, so the unconditioned computation is preserved exactly. Conditioning changes neither the categorical vocabulary nor the linear probability path. Training updates only the approximately 0.43M adapter parameters. The pretrained backbone and the output predictor stay frozen and in evaluation mode. G.2 Targets, metrics and matched timing 

Targets are the 10th, 50th and 90th training quantiles (q10, q50 and q90): approximately 0.529, 0.763 and 0.885 for QED, and 0.571, 2.626 and 4.186 for logP. All target tolerances and guidance choices were fixed before final scoring. Each external method uses one trained model per property. A hit is a valid output whose property lies within ±0.05 

QED or ±0.5 logP of the requested value, endpoints included. Raw hit rates divide hits by all 4,096 attempts, so invalid attempts and repeated hits stay in the denominator and in the cost accounting. Distinct novel hits count each hitting molecular identity once and additionally require it to be absent from the conditional training identities; identity ignores stereochemistry, and all methods use the same validity, identity and property checks. Target-window FCD uses the first 1,000 valid outputs from each eligible cell, including valid misses, rather than a subset selected for hitting the target. It scores them against the first 1,000 held-out reference identities inside the same target window; generated duplicates remain and there is no refill. Conditional identity and reference preprocessing differ from the unconditional protocol, so these values are not numerically comparable with the unconditional ZINC evaluation. 

Matched conditional timing protocol. The matched comparison measures every primary 500-step setting: three targets for each property, three MIRRA adaptations, both FragFM guidance regimes and Graph DiT. Checkpoints and validation-selected guidance strengths are those used for the evaluation samples. Each setting has three 256-attempt timing repetitions, with method order rotated across repetitions and each setting run in an isolated process with its model’s software versions at batch 128 after 128 warmup attempts, with four CPU threads. Within a target comparison, all methods run on the same physical GPU. The primary timer starts before random-state and graph initialization and ends after generation, decoding and the shared property, identity, novelty and hit checks. Guidance, failed assemblies and invalid outputs remain inside this interval. A second endpoint before annotation records generation and decoding alone. Loading, warmup, file writing, FCD scoring, training and guidance search are excluded for every method, and GPU work is synchronized at the generation boundaries. FragFM timing uses its strict assembly decoder. # H Polymer generation 

H.1 Data, representations and baseline adaptations 

We use the Open Macromolecular Genome authors’ selected all-reactions training set of 99,500 source products across 17 reaction labels rather than the complete OMG database. 1 Canonicalization identifies 99,476 distinct periodic objects. Rotation, reversal and repeated-unit aliases share one split group, so no reserved identity can reappear in training under a relabeling. The split holds 79,580 training, 9,947 validation and 9,949 reserved test objects. Internal generation controls use validation only, including 996 objects with unseen blocks. Checkpoints and generated samples are fixed before evaluation on the 9,949 held-out test objects (Table 3). This experiment evaluates structural generation on a known linear chain topology. The comparison uses two 4,096-attempt draws per model and setting. MIRRA and Graph DiT use 500 sampling steps, with MIRRA also evaluated at 250 steps from the same checkpoint. The VAE uses native autoregressive decoding. Validity is computed over all 4,096 attempts in each sampling run; structural distances condition on scorable outputs, whose fractions are reported separately.

Representations and information supplied to each method. MIRRA predicts oriented building blocks on a supplied linear chain, using circular attention and reversal-compatible features; its 14.5M-parameter model trains for 200,000 updates and uses the validation-selected checkpoint. The external models instead generate complete repeat-unit encodings: OMG-VAE emits SELFIES strings (Krenn et al., 2020), and Graph DiT emits atom and bond labels including wildcard attachment atoms. Neither receives MIRRA’s fixed chain connectivity. The external comparison therefore measures complete modeling approaches under a common split and scorer, including the advantage of a task-specific representation. The paired internal controls reported below use the same representation in both arms. 

OMG-VAE adaptation. The 3.95M-parameter model retains the OMG authors’ CNN encoder, 152-dimensional latent representation and four-layer bidirectional GRU decoder called one token at a time, with an auxiliary network predicting standardized logP normalized by backbone atom count. Training uses the authors’ reconstruction and IMQ-MMD objective with MMD weight 4.345, Adam learning rate 0.002, weight decay 10 −5, batch 32 and 60 epochs (149,220 updates); the learning rate falls tenfold at 40 epochs. Autoregressive reconstruction over all 9,947 validation objects under fixed latent draws selects the checkpoint, epoch 45. The SELFIES alphabet and maximum length come from training data only, and the fixed initial attachment token is removed for learning and prepended on decode. Generation samples a Gaussian latent prior and selects tokens greedily. Decoding truncates before the first no-operation token or the second generated attachment token, with the initial attachment token prepended afterward. Outputs are prior samples rather than reconstructions or optimized latent vectors. 

Graph DiT adaptation. The 160.1M-parameter model retains six transformer layers of width 1152 with 16 heads, 500 diffusion steps and Adam learning rate 2 × 10 −4. It trains from scratch for 100 epochs (62,200 optimizer updates) at effective batch 128. Wildcard attachment atoms are included as nodes when estimating graph sizes and no-edge frequencies. Graph DiT uses normalized time during training and sampling. Fixed noising on up to 2,048 validation objects selects the checkpoint, epoch 100. The maximum graph size in the training set defines model support. Generation uses the trained size prior and predicts the complete graph without requesting any property. 

Periodic validity. Ordinary chemical validity requires only a chemically parseable output. Periodic validity is stricter: the repeat unit must be connected, must carry the two required attachment ports, and must canonicalize periodically, with rotation, reversal and repeated-unit aliases collapsing to a single identity. Valid baseline outputs containing building blocks absent from MIRRA’s vocabulary are accepted. A periodically valid output is scorable if its seven-copy chain can be assembled and sanitized and its central-unit fingerprint and all six structural descriptors can be computed. Structural MMD and descriptor distances use these scorable outputs; validity and the scorable fraction are reported separately over all attempts. 

H.2 Structural metrics and internal controls 

The aligned and permuted models are sampled at 250 sampling steps (primary) and 1000 steps (sensitivity) with 4,096 raw attempts per population, no rejection or replenishment, matched requested lengths, the selected checkpoints and the full 41,266-token training vocabulary. The reference is the complete 9,947-object validation population, all scorable. Structural features are ℓ2-normalized 2,048-dimensional Morgan counts (radius two, chirality enabled), rooted at real atoms of the central unit in a seven-copy chain. For feature vectors x, y , the kernel averages exp[ −∥ x − y∥2/(2 s2f )] 

over f ∈ { 0.5, 1, 2}, with s2 = 1 .158 fixed as the median squared distance of 4,096 unequal-index pairs drawn from 2,048 distinct training objects. The unbiased MMD 2 estimator (Gretton et al., 2012) excludes within-population self-pairs and retains duplicates, so it may be negative. The main external table reports MMD 2 in units of 10 −3; the smaller internal differences below use 10 −5. Descriptor distances are Wasserstein distances over primitive-unit mass, central topological polar surface area (TPSA), and heteroatom, aromatic-heavy, branched-heavy and nonring-single-heavy-bond fractions, each divided by its training-calibration sample standard deviation. These are structural descriptors, not measured bulk polymer properties. 

Polymer prediction with shared outputs. Both arms have approximately 14.52M parameters and train for 200,000 updates from matching initial weights on the same data. Meaningful output sharing lowers the checkpoint-selection objective throughout training, with a 2.9% reduction at the final update. Selection uses mean endpoint cross-entropy on 1,024 validation objects, averaging each object’s token-average loss so that long objects receive no greater weight.
