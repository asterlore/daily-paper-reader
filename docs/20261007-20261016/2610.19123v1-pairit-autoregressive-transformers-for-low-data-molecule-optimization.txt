Abstract 

Generating novel molecules with desired properties is a central challenge in drug discovery. Although foundational work focused on de novo generation, practical use cases require a more complex setting: conditioning on an initial seed molecule, optimizing multiple properties, and operating in data-sparse regimes. We introduce 

PairIT , a flexible training framework for autoregressive transformers over SMILES sequences that enables seeded generation by learning from pairs of structurally similar molecules. By leveraging matched pairs, the model learns to generate local, chemically meaningful edits that improve a property of the seed molecule. Prompt tokens extend this framework by specifying the target property, desired direction, and approximate magnitude of change. Multiple prompt tokens can be concatenated to enable multi-property optimization at inference time. For properties where pair supervision alone is insufficient, reinforcement learning post-training can further improve optimization, providing a tunable trade-off between proximity to the seed and the optimization objective. PairIT naturally supports pretraining on a large dataset and fine-tuning on a smaller dataset, which is critical for performance in data-sparse regimes. Surprisingly, we observe diminishing returns to the scale of the pretraining dataset. We demonstrate empirically on MoleculeNet and Therapeutics Data Commons datasets that, with appropriate paired-data curation, a standard transformer model of modest size (68M parameters) can achieve robust seeded molecular optimization in low-data regimes.

In practice, computational and medicinal chemists often approach this problem through local molecular edits informed by cheminformatics tools and domain expertise [Kramer et al., 2014]. A full discovery campaign has an “outer loop” in which candidates are synthesized and experimentally measured and an “inner loop” in which candidates are proposed and scored against a surrogate model. We focus on seeded generation 0 in this “inner loop”, in the scarce-label regime common in early-stage discovery, where experimental measurements are expensive and datasets often contain only hundreds to thousands of labeled molecules [Wu et al., 2018]. While substantial prior literature exists on molecular optimization, the small-dataset regime re-mains under-addressed, with few works evaluating seeded optimization on biological properties. Many recent models are developed and evaluated either in de novo settings, where there is no seed molecule [Pinheiro et al., 2023, Joshi et al., 2025, Xu et al., 2023], or with far more labeled data than is typically available in early-stage discovery [Dey et al., 2025, Lee et al., 2025, Jin et al., 2020]. In contrast, we focus on seeded optimization in the low-data regime. Generated molecules must remain close enough to the seed to preserve relevant structure while introducing enough novelty to improve the desired property. Moreover, constraining generation to a local search space is restrictive, by definition: the model must introduce enough novelty to improve the requested properties while still remaining close to the seed, and such conditioning can collapse toward near-reconstruction rather than diverse one-to-many outputs [Kumar et al., 2024]. In small labeled datasets, limited examples further increase the risk of overfitting and memorized edits [Wu et al., 2018]. Yet even small datasets can contain useful local structure–property relationships, motivating training objectives that expose these transformations directly. One way to expose such local transformations directly is to match structurally similar molecules from a dataset and train the model to “translate” from one molecule to the other. If the pairs are directed so that the target molecule has an improved value for a property of interest, the model learns local, property-improving edits. For example, Damani et al. [2019] apply this sequence-to-sequence approach to small-molecule optimization, while Tagasovska et al. [2024], Pinheiro et al. [2025], Chen et al. [2025] use related pair-based approaches for protein sequence optimization. Recent work also applies pair-based training to large language models for controllable molecular optimization [Dey et al., 2025]. We propose PairIT (Figure 1), a decoder-only autoregressive transformer over SMILES 1 trained on matched seed–target pairs of structurally similar molecules with differing properties. The base model learns to generate local, property-improving edits directly from pair supervision. To extend optimization beyond the property changes learned in a single generation step, we also consider iterative re-seeding, in which high-scoring generations are fed back to the model as new seeds for further optimization. We refer to this variant as PairIT-Beam. For properties that remain difficult to optimize from pair supervision alone, we further explore reinforcement learning (RL) post-training

However, pair-only approaches tie the optimization objective to the constructed pair set. Changing the target property or direction, or composing multiple objectives, can require constructing new pairs and retraining. This becomes increasingly restrictive in small datasets, where suitable pairs for specific combinations of property changes may be sparse or absent. To provide finer control over how much a seed property is changed, we additionally introduce structured control tokens specifying the property, direction, and approximate magnitude of change. This also extends naturally to multi-property optimization, where constructing similar pairs that improve several properties simultaneously can be especially difficult in very small datasets. Multiple property prompts can instead be composed at inference time without constructing a separate pair set and retraining for each combination. Because the downstream pair sets are very small, we first pretrain PairIT on ZINC-250K [Irwin et al., 2012] before fine-tuning on each target dataset. This raises two practical questions, whether the gains come simply from molecular pretraining or specifically from pretraining on directed pairs, and how much paired pretraining is needed in terms of pair diversity and training duration. We therefore compare no pretraining, single-molecule pretraining, and directed-pair pretraining, vary the amount and diversity of paired data, and track validation and downstream performance over the course of training. We find that directed-pair pretraining outperforms pretraining on individual molecules, but that its benefits emerge surprisingly early, with the best validation checkpoint reached before one full epoch over the pair dataset. We study this behavior with training-dynamics and data-scale ablations, finding that repeated molecular coverage across many pairs helps explain the early saturation and that additional pair data yield diminishing returns. PairIT deliberately combines existing components in a way tailored to low-data seeded molecular optimization, rather than introducing a new architecture or dataset. Rather than relying on a large general-purpose language model, we use a compact 68M-parameter transformer with molecule-specific tokenization, matched-pair construction, and the training and inference strategies described above. We evaluate this setting on MoleculeNet, where, to our knowledge, seeded generation has not previously been studied systematically. We formulate the task, define metrics that capture property improvement, novelty, and fidelity to the seed, and compare to diverse baseline methods under the same limited-data setting. Our main contributions are (i) a compact 68M-parameter matched-pair autoregressive model for low-data seeded molecular optimization that is competitive with substantially larger or more elaborate baselines; (ii) iterative re-seeding and optional RL post-training that can further improve property optimization when additional inference compute or a reliable property predictor is available; (iii) structured property-control tokens that provide control over the direction and magnitude of property change and support multi-property optimization without retraining for each objective combination; and (iv) an analysis of paired-pretraining dynamics showing that pair structure provides benefits beyond exposure to the same molecules individually, and characterizing why useful pretraining can emerge before a full pass over the pair dataset. 

2 Related Work 

The methods most closely related to our approach are matched-pair methods for seeded molecular optimization. PropEn [Tagasovska et al., 2024] is a non-autoregressive encoder 2 trained to predict the output sequence in a pair; the authors show that this objective approximates the property-gradient direction. Follow-up work extends the framework to conditional density estimation [Pinheiro et al., 2025] and multi-objective optimization via a non-domination ranker applied during pair construction [Tagasovska et al., 2025, Park et al., 2024]. Earlier small-molecule work frames optimization as graph-to-graph or sequence-to-sequence translation [Jin et al., 2019, 2020, Kim et al., 2025]; Damani et al. [2019] introduce recursive inference, in which generated molecules are repeatedly fed back as inputs. GeLLM4O-C [Dey et al., 2025] is the closest language-model baseline. It instruction-tunes 7– 8B-parameter LLMs on approximately 256K molecule pairs with natural-language instructions specifying which properties to improve or maintain. PairIT similarly makes the requested property

Other molecular language-model work includes DrugAssist [Ye et al., 2023] and SmileyLlama [Cavanagh et al., 2024]. RL provides another route for molecular optimization. REINVENT [Olivecrona et al., 2017], LLOME [Chen et al., 2025], and MOLRL [Haddad et al., 2025] apply reinforcement learning to molecular or biological sequence generation under property-based rewards. Preference optimization, including DPO [Rafailov et al., 2023], provides a related pairwise-learning approach, with multi-objective and molecular extensions [Xiong and Singh, 2025, Zhou et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2024, Gupta et al., 2025, Park et al., 2023, Calanzone et al., 2025]. Property-model-guided search provides a complementary approach, including latent-space Bayesian optimization [Gómez-Bombarelli et al., 2018, Lee et al., 2025] and gradient-based steering [Lee et al., 2023]. These methods could in principle be combined with PairIT, while vanilla PairIT learns the optimization objective from paired data and does not require a property oracle in the generation loop. 

3 Proposed Method: PairIT 

Let D = {(xi, y i)}ni=1 be a dataset of molecules, where each xi is a molecular representation (e.g., SMILES string) and yi ∈ Y is its property, which may take discrete, continuous, or mixed values. We refer to the unknown property function as g : X → Y such that yi = g(xi). Our goal is seeded molecular generation : given an initial molecule x, we seek to generate one or more candidate 

molecules x′ such that s(x′, x ) ≥ τ and g(x′) > g (x), where s(·, ·) is a molecular similarity measure (e.g., Tanimoto similarity) and τ is the chosen similarity threshold. This formulation reflects a realistic task in drug discovery where the aim is to propose local, structure-preserving modifications that improve a property of interest while remaining in the chemical neighborhood of the input molecule. During training, we instantiate this objective with directed matched seed-target pairs; the corresponding dataset-specific pair-construction procedures are described in Section 4.

Because ppair is supported on directed pairs that are both structurally similar and property-improved, minimizing this objective fits qθ (x′ | x, c ) to the empirical distribution of local property-improved neighbors of x. Thus, pair construction encodes both structural locality and the desired direction of property change, while the conditional model allows multiple plausible improved targets per seed. PairIT provides a base matched-pair generator that can be extended in two complementary ways: iterative re-seeding or RL post-training can strengthen property optimization, while structured prompts provide explicit control over which properties should change and by how much. 

3.2 PairIT implementation and variants Model architecture. PairIT uses a 68M-parameter decoder-only autoregressive transformer over SMILES strings, instantiated with a Llama-style architecture [Touvron et al., 2023] and adapted for molecular sequence generation following prior implementations [Frey et al., 2024, Ismail et al., 2025]. We deliberately use a small model: SMILES generation has a substantially narrower vocabulary than natural language, and our model-size ablation shows little benefit from scaling across the 11M–200M range tested (Appendix B.2). For paired training, we concatenate seed and target SMILES separated by a <sep> token, with generation conditioned on the seed. Full architecture and training details are in Appendix B.1. 4PairIT and iterative PairIT-Beam. Vanilla PairIT samples property-improved candidates directly from the matched-pair model. PairIT-Beam additionally applies iterative refinement: at each of three rounds, it generates 8 candidates per seed and retains the top 4 as seeds for the next round, yielding up to 72 candidates per original seed. This follows the recursive-inference idea of Damani et al. [2019]. 

PairIT-RL. When pair supervision alone provides insufficient signal for property improvement, we optionally post-train PairIT using a property model as a reward. We use Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) [Shao et al., 2024] with a property-model-based reward and seed-similarity regularization to limit exploitation of the surrogate model. Full reward and training details are provided in Appendix F. 

3.3 Prompt-conditioning Single-property conditioning. Without prompting, the desired property change is implicit in each training pair. To make this control explicit, we prepend structured property tokens to the seed molecule [Kotsias et al., 2020]. For a single conditioned property, the prompt specifies the property identity and a signed delta bin: the sign gives the desired direction and the bin magnitude gives the approximate requested change. Prompting changes only the conditioning sequence and training uses the same autoregressive objective as above. 

Multi-property conditioning. The same mechanism extends directly to multiple properties by concatenating one prompt block per requested property. During pretraining, a generic target slot can be trained using available properties and reassigned to a new downstream property during fine-tuning. For example, we pretrain on ZINC-250K using only RDKit properties and assign the generic target slot to the downstream MoleculeNet property during fine-tuning. We also apply property dropout during training so that subsets of the trained properties can be specified at inference time. 

4 Experimental Results 

Datasets and pair construction. We evaluate on three low-data MoleculeNet benchmarks (BACE, BBBP, HIV) using the class-balanced splits of GraphDiT [Liu et al., 2024] which are binary tasks corresponding to β-secretase 1 inhibition, blood–brain barrier permeability, and HIV antiviral activity, respectively. We additionally evaluate on hERG [Wang et al., 2016], a low-data toxicity task from Therapeutics Data Commons (TDC) [Huang et al., 2021] (Appendix D.6). For auxiliary pretraining, we construct ZINC-250K pairs by 2D Tanimoto similarity on Morgan fingerprints (ECFP4), directed toward a minimum logP improvement of ∆logP > 1, using 100 target neighbors per seed. For MoleculeNet fine-tuning, we construct matched molecular pairs (MMPs) using the 

mmpdb toolkit [Dalke et al., 2018], directed from inactive (class 0) to active (class 1) molecules. The resulting MoleculeNet training sets contain only 88–662 pairs from 517–1,420 molecules per dataset. Full pair construction details, including the rationale for the dataset-specific pairing strategies, and dataset statistics are in Appendix B.4. 

Inference and evaluation. Each method produces 64 candidates per seed (PairIT-Beam: up to 72). We canonicalize and deduplicate the candidates, then rank them using validity and novelty together with property predictions from the random forest used in GraphDiT [Liu et al., 2024] and Tanimoto similarity to the seed, retaining the top five candidates per seed for each method. These top-five selections are then independently scored with MiniMol [Kläser et al., 2024] for the final reported results. Both property models are trained only on the corresponding MoleculeNet training splits. The same random forest is also used for NF-BO search, PairIT-Beam refinement, and PairIT-RL rewards. Agreement between the property predictions of these two oracles on held-out MoleculeNet test molecules and on generated molecules is analyzed in Appendix B.5.1. Inference uses temperature 0.5; see Appendix B.3 for a hyperparameter sweep.

We also include MMP enumeration [Kramer et al., 2014] as a non-learning local-edit reference and GraphDiT [Liu et al., 2024] as a de novo reference baseline. 4.2 Pretraining ablations and training dynamics 

We first analyze the effects of pretraining and paired supervision separately. Without any ZINC-250K pretraining, training only on the small MoleculeNet pair sets leads to a large drop in VIN across all three tasks. Failure-mode analysis shows that these models generate many invalid SMILES (Appendix E.1, Figure 18), suggesting that a major role of pretraining in this low-data regime is to learn the SMILES grammar and generate valid molecules before downstream fine-tuning. Pretraining on molecules alone is not sufficient, however. Single-molecule pretraining on the same ZINC-250K distribution underperforms pretraining on directed pairs (Appendix E.1, Table 14). Thus, the benefit is not simply exposure to more molecules; pair supervision additionally teaches the seed-conditioned local transformations beneficial for molecular optimization. Within paired pretraining, performance tends to improve with the number of pairs per seed, although gains are not monotonic and eventually diminish. More diverse pair sets also increase seed similarity and expose the model to more unique molecules. Surprisingly, the useful signal from paired ZINC-250K pretraining saturates very quickly. The best checkpoint by validation loss occurs before one full epoch over the pair dataset. Importantly, a pair epoch is not equivalent to a full pass over the unique molecules: because each molecule can be included in many matched pairs, the model repeatedly encounters the underlying molecules while seeing only a fraction of the constructed pair examples. Appendix E.2 explicitly quantifies the number of unique molecules encountered at the point of early stopping (Figure 19), separating molecular coverage from pair-dataset coverage. Thus, early saturation does not imply that the model has seen little chemical diversity; rather, it suggests that additional pair examples become redundant relatively quickly. Consistent with this, increasing the size of the paired pretraining set yields rapid initial gains followed by diminishing downstream improvement (Figure 4). Single-property magnitude control. We first test whether the signed delta token carries information beyond a binary increase-or-decrease signal already available from the training pairs. On the small MoleculeNet datasets, we fine-tune with both orderings of each pair and sweep 16 signed delta bins at inference. Figure 5 shows the QED results: across all three datasets, larger requested increases generally produce larger realized changes. Although the realized changes do not match the requested bins exactly, this suggests that the prompt can support graded control even in the low-data MoleculeNet setting, despite each delta bin being trained on only a small subset of the available examples. Appendix G.2 reports additional single-property results using logP, together with aggregate VIN and MSE values for both QED and logP (Figure 37, Table 15).

One likely reason is that, unlike continuously valued descriptors such as QED, logP, or TPSA, MoleculeNet bioactivity is binary and therefore provides a much coarser control signal. The small matched-pair datasets also contain relatively few examples of each joint property configuration. Many-property control. Finally, we evaluate whether prompt dependence is retained when the conditioning space becomes substantially larger. We train a single model on nine computable RDKit properties plus a tenth target slot that is assigned to downstream bioactivity during MoleculeNet fine-tuning. With all 10 properties prompted simultaneously, mean direction adherence across the nine non-activity properties reaches 0.739–0.773, compared with 0.433–0.559 under randomized prompts for matching the prompted increase/decrease direction (Table 2). Thus, even in the 10-property setting, the generated edits remain substantially sensitive to the requested property directions rather than ignoring the prompt. Many-property control is nevertheless sensitive to which objectives are included. Appendix G.5 compares the full 10-property prompt with smaller property subsets and evaluates all  93

 = 84 choices of three auxiliary RDKit properties alongside bioactivity. These results show that conditioning on more properties is not necessarily better: performance can improve when the prompt is restricted to a smaller subset, and varies substantially depending on which auxiliary properties are included. 

5 Discussion 

PairIT supports a progression from simple pair-supervised generation to more strongly guided optimization. Vanilla PairIT learns property-improving local edits directly from matched seed–target pairs. PairIT-Beam extends this by iteratively re-seeding high-scoring generations, allowing further property improvement at inference time at the cost of additional compute. When a reliable property oracle is available, PairIT-RL can push optimization further, but it is more sensitive to reward-model errors and can exhibit reward hacking under weak similarity constraints. Prompt conditioning adds a complementary form of control by making the desired direction and magnitude of property change explicit and extending the same framework to multi-property optimization. PairIT also provides several ways to trade off property improvement against similarity to the seed. Sampling temperature changes this tradeoff without retraining (Fig. 7), while the similarity-reward weight in PairIT-RL directly controls the balance between property optimization and seed similarity. Pretraining is particularly important in the low-data setting. ZINC-250K pretraining substantially improves reliable molecular generation, while directed-pair pretraining is more effective than pre-training on the same molecules individually. More diverse pretraining pairs also change the locality of the learned edits (Appendix E.1). At the same time, useful paired pretraining saturates quickly. In practice, this makes aggressive early stopping an important part of the training recipe. On the surface, it may appear that the role of specialized domain knowledge has been diminished, yet it reappears in how the optimization problem is defined. The distance function determines what constitutes a “local” molecular edit, the construction of the paired data determines which transformations are learned, the prompted properties determine which objectives can be controlled, and the reward function determines what is optimized during post-training. As machine learning methods become increasingly effective at solving the problems we set before them, it becomes increasingly important that we formulate those problems well. 9Limitations and broader impact. PairIT addresses the “inner loop” of generating high-scoring candidates under a scoring model, but lacks “outer loop” experimental validation. Any property oracle outside the wet lab is imperfect and susceptible to distribution shift, so oracle-dependent components inherit this uncertainty. Performance also depends on training-pair diversity, and multi-property control is less reliable when requested changes conflict with empirical property correlations or are weakly represented in the paired data (Table 1). We do not address synthesizability of generated candidate molecules, which we leave to future work. Generated candidates should be subject to expert review and safety screening before synthesis. 10 References

B.3 Inference-temperature sweep 

The temperature is an important sampling parameter for PairIT that must be tuned. We vary the temperature at inference across a range of 0.1–1.0. It modulates the randomness of softmax sampling during generation. As shown in Figure 7, lower temperatures reduce diversity and lead to near-repetition of the seed, while higher temperatures increase novelty but degrade validity. Higher temperatures tend to yield larger improvements in the target property, at the cost of reduced fidelity. We use the temperature of 0.5 for all datasets. 

Figure 7: Inference-time temperature sweep ( 0.1–1.0) showing the trade-off between diversity and validity: lower temperatures favor similarity to the seed, while higher temperatures increase novelty and property improvement at the cost of validity. 18 Figure 8: Examples of generations with different temperature settings. 19 B.4 Pair construction details 

For ZINC-250K auxiliary pretraining, we construct directed logP-improvement pairs using 2D Tanimoto similarity [Bender and Glen, 2004] on Morgan fingerprints (ECFP4, radius 2, 2048-bit) [Rogers and Hahn, 2010]. A pair (x, x ′) is directed from seed x to target x′ when the two molecules are structurally similar and x′ has higher logP than x. We use this fingerprint-based pairing on ZINC-250K because it provides dense local neighborhoods and many improving examples per seed, which is useful for learning SMILES syntax and local property-improving edits before downstream fine-tuning. For MoleculeNet fine-tuning and evaluation, we instead construct directed matched molecular pairs (MMPs), which share a common core and differ by a localized fragment replacement [Kramer et al., 2014]. For the binary BACE, BBBP, and HIV tasks, pairs are directed from inactive to active molecules, so the seed is class 0 and the target is class 1. We use MMPs on MoleculeNet to emphasize localized medicinal-chemistry-style edits in the low-data downstream setting, whereas fingerprint similarity is a broader notion of locality and can be affected by fingerprint bit density and molecular size [Wang et al., 2007]. We invoke mmpdb fragment and mmpdb index [Dalke et al., 2018] with default parameters (single-bond cuts), then post-filter to pairs whose seed and target have a heavy-atom ratio min( |x|, |x′|)/ max( |x|, |x′|) ≥ 0.6 to avoid pairing a small seed with a much larger target (or vice versa). For the two- and ten-property signed-prompt models, MoleculeNet fine-tuning instead includes both orderings of every retained training pair, as in the augmented single-property training construction. Property changes are recomputed after swapping seed and target: a nonzero change becomes its negative, whereas a zero change remains zero. This supplies supervision for both signs of observed changes, without requiring every bin to be populated. For signed-prompt ZINC pretraining, we retain the original directed nearest-neighbor pairs without a positive-change filter; the reverse of every pair is not explicitly appended. B.6 Baseline methods 

All baselines use the same task splits, task directions, metrics, and final selection procedure as PairIT; below we report method-specific implementation details. 

NF-BO. We compare against NF-BO, a normalizing-flow-based Bayesian optimization method that combines a SELFIES-based autoregressive generative model with a Gaussian-process surrogate and trust-region Bayesian optimization. The generative model is an LSTM normalizing flow trained on SELFIES sequences, pretrained on GuacaMol [Brown et al., 2019], and fine-tuned separately for each task. Models were fine-tuned for 50 epochs using Adam with learning rate 10 −4 and batch size 32. For BACE, the Random Forest classifier returns pclass 1 = 0 for every input class-0 test seed; this drives the initial Gaussian-process fit to a degenerate 1 ×1 kernel with NaN Cholesky, halting BO before any candidate is written. We therefore run NF-BO’s inner BO loop with the Random Forest 21 classifier trained on the full dataset, including training and test sets, for BACE only; this provides sufficient variation in the surrogate scores for BO to proceed. All 64 generations per BACE seed are otherwise produced by the same NF-BO procedure, and are re-scored downstream with the shared MiniMol oracle at evaluation time. BBBP and HIV use the correct train-only Random Forest classifier throughout. At inference time, NF-BO performs iterative Bayesian optimization in the learned latent space with a property model budget of 500 evaluations per seed molecule. A deep-kernel Gaussian-process surrogate is trained for 5 epochs at each iteration on accumulated observations, and candidate molecules are proposed using TuRBO-based Thompson sampling with acquisition batch size 100; the top-10 current observations are used as trust-region centers at each step, and a running top-64 set of valid generations is maintained across the budget. Generated molecules are decoded via SELFIES and filtered for validity. Optimization objectives maximize either logP improvement or predicted class-1 probability, subject to a structural similarity constraint enforced via Tanimoto similarity (Morgan fingerprints, radius 2, 2048 bits) with a minimum threshold of 0.4 and a soft penalty below that threshold. Generation terminates upon exhausting the property model budget. 

DDSBM (Bridge and SB variants). We compare against DDSBM, a discrete diffusion–based Schrödinger Bridge model for graph-to-graph molecular transformation. DDSBM operates directly on molecular graphs and learns stochastic forward and reverse diffusion processes that transport molecules from a source distribution to a target distribution using entropy-regularized optimal transport. The model is implemented with a graph transformer backbone. We evaluate two DDSBM variants that differ only in the number of Schrödinger Bridge refinement steps. DDSBM-Bridge performs a single forward training pass from source to target (one outer loop), providing a fast but coarse approximation of the bridge. DDSBM-SB applies 5 outer iterative-proportional-fitting loops, corresponding to 10 training runs alternating backward and forward updates, yielding a tighter approximation to the true Schrödinger bridge at increased computational cost. Both variants are fine-tuned from a ZINC-250K paired pretraining checkpoint (Bridge and SB pretrained checkpoints are kept separate). Each training run is performed for 300 epochs, resulting in 300 and 3,000 effective training epochs for Bridge and SB, respectively. At inference time, generation is performed via 100-step discrete reverse diffusion from the input molecule without explicit similarity constraints or property model budgets. We generate 64 stochastic samples per seed and evaluate generations using the metrics described in Appendix B.5. 

HierG2G. We compare against Hierarchical Graph-to-Graph (HierG2G), a variational graph trans-lation model that generates molecules by hierarchically assembling substructures into molecular graphs. HierG2G represents molecules using coupled tree and graph representations and performs conditional translation via a hierarchical variational graph neural network. We use the public HierG2G implementation and fine-tune models separately for each task, starting from a checkpoint pretrained on ZINC-250K. For MoleculeNet tasks (BACE, BBBP, and HIV), we fine-tune task-specific translation models for 12 epochs using Adam (learning rate 10 −3, batch size 32, latent dimension 4), following default hyperparameters from prior work. At inference time, conditional translation is performed by sampling multiple candidate molecules per input via stochastic decoding in the learned latent space, with an increased maximum decoding length to reduce fragment generation. We generate 64 candidates per seed molecule using the checkpoint at epoch 11 and evaluate generations using the metrics described in Appendix B.5. 

MMP baseline. We include a property-aware matched molecular pair baseline (MMP-P ROP ), a non-learning cheminformatics enumeration method. Starting from each seed molecule, we queried an 

mmpdb database of matched molecular transformations built from the training set. We augmented the database with training-set property scores from a task-specific classifier, so that each transformation carried an estimated property shift. For each test seed, we first enumerated candidate edits using 

mmpdb transform –property , which ranks transformations by their learned average property change; when needed, we supplemented the candidate pool with additional mmpdb generate pro-posals. This produced up to 64 candidate molecules per seed. As in the other 64-sample baselines, we then evaluated the candidate pool with the common benchmark metrics and reported the best-5 subset under the same selection procedure used for all methods. 22 GraphDiT. We used the publicly released pretrained GraphDiT checkpoints for the MoleculeNet datasets (BACE, BBBP, and HIV) as released by the original authors, without any retraining or fine-tuning on our side. The pretrained models use dropout for classifier-free guidance regularization during training ( drop_condition = 0 .01 ). For generation, we sampled 10,000 molecules per dataset using classifier-free guidance with guide_scale = 2 . These generations are de novo samples from random noise and are therefore not conditioned on an input molecule. 

GeLLM4O-C. We compare against GeLLM4O-C [Dey et al., 2025], an instruction-tuned 7B-parameter Mistral LLM (Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3) trained with LoRA adapters on the C-MuMOInstruct multi-property optimization corpus. We use the authors’ released BBBP, logP, and QED adapters untouched. For BACE and HIV, we fine-tune task-specific LoRA adapters (r = 16 , α = 16 , dropout 0.05 , targeting q/k/v/o/gate/up/down projections and the LM head; 0.58% trainable parameters, no quantization) using their trainer on directed mmpdb pairs converted to C-MuMOInstruct JSON format ( source = inactive molecule, target = active molecule; direction encoded categorically as "increase" via the instruction template, with numeric activity from the RF oracle not surfaced in the prompt). Models were trained with AdamW at learning rate 10 −4, cosine schedule, warmup ratio 0.1, effective batch size 16 (per-device 1 with 16-step gradient accumulation), and cutoff_len=4096 . Training uses train_on_inputs=False so loss is computed only on the response SMILES. At inference, we sample 64 candidates per seed via stochastic decoding and evaluate under the shared selection protocol described in Appendix B.5. 23 C Theoretical Comparison to DPO 

Because PairIT is trained on paired examples, a preference objective such as DPO [Rafailov et al., 2023] is a natural alternative. We instead use conditional likelihood: prior work has found DPO to underperform SFT and SFT followed by RL on related biomolecular sequence-optimization tasks [Chen et al., 2025], and DPO’s contrastive objective can shift probability mass toward outputs outside the observed preference pair [Razin et al., 2025]. Below, we provide a complementary analytical comparison of the training signals produced by the two objectives. F.1 Reward and training details Reward Model For each generation g produced from seed s, the reward has a gated structure that assigns fixed penalties to degenerate outputs before scoring valid, non-trivial molecules: 

Rgated (g, s ) = 



−1.0 if g is not a valid SMILES 

−0.5 if g = s (exact copy of the seed) 

−0.5 if g ∈ D train (memorised from any training set) 

−0.5 if Tan( g, s ) < τ sim (below similarity gate) 

robj (g, s ) otherwise. 

robj (g, s ) = ∆ property model (g, s ) + λact 1[pproperty model (g) > 0.5] + wsim Tan( g, s ).

The first three gates enforce basic quality, assigning fixed penalties to invalid, copied, or memorized outputs rather than property-model rewards. The fourth gate (controlled by threshold τsim ∈ { 0, 0.2})optionally penalises generations that diverge too far from the seed before any positive reward is computed. For molecules that pass all gates, the reward combines three terms. ∆propertymodel (g, s ) =

ppropertymodel (g) − ppropertymodel (s) is the change in predicted activity probability relative to the seed, rewarding improvement in the target property. λact · 1[ppropertymodel (g) > 0.5] is a threshold bonus ( λact = 2 .0) for generating molecules the property model classifies as active, providing a discrete incentive beyond the continuous ∆ signal. wsim · Tan( g, s ) rewards structural similarity to the seed, where wsim is the primary hyperparameter we sweep ( wsim ∈ { 1, 3, 5, 10 }). This term serves both its obvious purpose — preserving structural fidelity — and an implicit one: keeping generations within the distribution where the activity property model was trained and can be trusted. For multi-property models, the total reward adds a prompt-adherence term: 

R(g, s ) = Rgated (g, s ) + 2 .0 · Radhere (g, s )

where Radhere averages, over each prompted property, whether the generation changed that property in the prompted direction and by the prompted magnitude. The activity property is not prompted. It is driven entirely by the property model reward — so Radhere covers only the nine RDKit descriptors (logP, QED, SA, MW, TPSA, HBD, HBA, rotatable bonds, fraction sp 3 carbons). The key point is that wsim and τsim are doing the work that KL regularisation alone is insufficient to do. The KL penalty (with βKL = 0 .01 ) constrains the policy to stay near the SFT distribution in token space, but it doesn’t directly constrain the output molecules to stay near the seed in chemical space. The Tanimoto terms provide that constraint explicitly, and the right operating point ( wsim = 5 ,

τsim = 0 .2) is effectively a statement about how far from the seed the property model remains reliable. 

Training setup. PairIT-RL is initialized from the real-only SFT checkpoint and fine-tuned with GRPO [Shao et al., 2024] separately for each dataset. Common hyperparameters: temperature 1.0, 

βKL = 0 .01 , lr = 5 × 10 −5, batch size 32, num_gen 8, LoRA rank 16, 10 epochs. 

F.2 Similarity regularization and reward hacking. 

Without a similarity penalty ( wsim = 0 ), the policy collapses to property model-fooling attractors at near-zero seed similarity, with BBBP VIN dropping to 0% and HIV VIN to 22%. Setting wsim = 5 

recovers seed similarity while maintaining high activity; a hard penalty that rejects generations with 

Tanimoto < 0.2 works similarly and trades a small amount of activity for greater similarity.

G.1 Prompt tokens 

At inference time, the prompt tokens and seed SMILES are provided as context, and generation begins after <sep> . The prompt vocabulary consists of a start-of-prompt marker <pc> , a property identifier such as <p_logp> , and a property-specific signed delta-bin token encoding the desired change. The seed property value is not included in the prompt; direction and approximate magnitude are encoded jointly by the sign and magnitude of the delta bin.

G.4 Two-property control on MoleculeNet 

The figures below show the full scatter plots for selected low-correlation two-property control and joint QED+logP control. In both cases, both properties are prompted simultaneously using signed delta bins derived from matched test pairs. Each point shows a valid generation; points are not filtered by whether the other property also moves correctly. We selected MW/HBD, TPSA/logP, and HBA/logP as weakly correlated descriptor pairs using Pearson correlations of molecular property values across the pooled ZINC training and validation molecules ( n = 249 ,455 ; r = −0.005 , −0.162 , and +0 .056 , respectively), and used them for BACE, BBBP, and HIV, respectively. For each held-out matched test pair, we compute the requested property changes as reference-target minus seed values and convert them to signed-delta-bin tokens. Generation is conditioned only on the prompt tokens and seed SMILES; the reference-target SMILES is not provided to the model. The two-property evaluations use 51 BACE, 14 BBBP, and 46 HIV test pairs with inactive seeds and active reference targets. We generate 64 outputs per pair with the same prompt, without top-five 51 selection. Results pool generated attempts across pairs; prompts follow the observed pair distribution rather than a balanced bin sweep. For each descriptor, VIN is the fraction of all generated attempts with a nonzero raw requested change that are valid, novel relative to the task’s training molecules, and move in the requested direction. Combined VIN includes attempts with at least one applicable requested change and requires every applicable criterion to succeed, together with validity and novelty. A zero requested descriptor change imposes no success condition, and pairs with both descriptor changes zero are excluded from Combined VIN. For bioactivity co-control, success means an inactive seed yields a generation with train-only random-forest activity probability greater than 0.5. The scatter plots show valid generations with a nonzero request for the displayed property, without requiring success on the other property. Their MSE pools squared errors against the raw matched-pair change across valid generations with finite values and nonzero target changes; novelty is not required. The per-pair medians are display summaries and do not enter MSE. For the randomized-prompt control, we independently sample each property’s signed-bin index uniformly from 00–15, with replacement, while keeping the property identities and prompt-block order fixed. No separate direction token is sampled. One reproducible randomized assignment is fixed per ordered seed–target pair and shared by its 64 generations. The control uses the same trained checkpoint as the correctly prompted arm. Both arms are scored against the original requested changes. 200 100 0 100 200 300

We train a single model on all nine computable RDKit properties simultaneously (logP, QED, SA, MW, TPSA, HBD, HBA, rotatable bonds, and fraction sp 3 carbons) plus a tenth target prompt field. During ZINC pretraining, the target prompt field mirrors a randomly chosen real property for each training example, without committing it to a fixed property during pretraining. Property dropout (rate 

= 0 .3) is applied during pretraining, randomly omitting properties from the prompt so the model can be conditioned on only a subset of properties at inference time. At MoleculeNet fine-tuning, the target prompt field is assigned to the dataset-specific bioactivity property ( Class for BACE, p_np for BBBP, and HIV_active for HIV). The target-slot change is the reference target’s label minus the seed’s label, discretized with the activity bin range. The model is fine-tuned with this reassigned target meaning and the dataset-specific bin limits. Every retained training pair is used in both orderings, with all signed changes recomputed after reversal, and all ten property blocks remain included.
