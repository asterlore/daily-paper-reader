ABSTRACT
Continual adaptation of large language models (LLMs) to evolving task distributions while mitigating
catastrophic forgetting remains an open problem. Existing strategies often struggle to scale or lack a
robust mechanism for zero-shot task-to-expert assignment. We introduce CaLLM, a framework that
resolves these tensions by combining metric-based meta-learning with modular parameter isolation
via lightweight adapters. First, CaLLM projects each input batch into a task-agnostic embedding
space to find its nearest prototype. It then routes the batch to the specific adapter specialized for
that data distribution. This formulation provides (i) controllable cross-task interference by design,
(ii) constant computational complexity regardless of task count, (iii) seamless integration of novel
distributions through prototype expansion, and (iv) regime agnosticism as the local routing decision
enables a unified approach to both online and offline settings. Our experiments demonstrate that this
routing mechanism discovers semantically coherent task groupings while facilitating constructive
interference between similar tasks, and keeping overhead at a minimum. Across diverse and fine-
grained continual learning benchmarks, CaLLM consistently outperforms traditional isolation and
replay methods, establishing prototype-based modular routing as a principled and scalable approach
to lifelong adaptation of LLMs.

INTRODUCTION
Large language models (LLMs) have become remarkable generalists, but real-world deployment often requires them to
acquire task-specific expertise without sacrificing their broader capabilities. The same underlying model needs to be
continually adapted to different use cases ranging from closely related to radically different, without retraining from
scratch. However, the standard practice of sequential fine-tuning typically triggers catastrophic forgetting (Kirkpatrick
et al., 2017), where adaptation to a new domain erodes performance on previously learned ones. A recent survey (Shi
et al., 2025) formalizes this challenge along two axes: vertical continuity, moving from broad pretraining to task-specific
fine-tuning, and horizontal continuity, adapting to shifting tasks and data distributions over time. Without careful
management, adaptation along either of these dimensions typically results in severe performance degradation.
This problem is particularly pronounced for aligned, instruction-tuned LLMs. Wang et al. (2023b) demonstrate that
even parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) can severely degrade an aligned model’s general
abilities and instruction-following quality after only a few rounds of sequential adaptation. Classical continual learning
(CL) strategies such as regularization, experience replay, and orthogonal subspace isolation address specific aspects of
this problem but they entail fundamental trade-offs. Regularization methods collapse when the model must transition
between radically different domains (e.g., from natural language to code). Replay requires growing memory buffers
that become unscalable as the task stream lengthens. Meanwhile, full retraining after each new task is computationally
prohibitive, and real-world data often arrives as a continuous, non-stationary stream rather than the neatly delineated
task blocks assumed in most CL benchmarks. Ideally, CL methods should adapt efficiently to new tasks in both online
and offline settings.
We present CaLLM (Continual Adaptation of LLMs), a modular CL method that sidesteps these limitations through two
interlocking components. First, CaLLM maintains a pool of modular experts (e.g., lightweight adapters implemented as
LoRAs), each learning to specialize to a distinct region of the observed input distribution, ensuring that new knowledge
is internalized without interfering with prior knowledge. Second, a lightweight non-parametric router, inspired by

Prototypical Networks (Snell et al., 2017), maps each incoming batch to its most relevant adapter by comparing its
(frozen-backbone) embeddings against stored prototypes. This mechanism dynamically and efficiently manages the
adapter pool: it spawns new adapters when the data is sufficiently novel and re-purposes the least-used modules when
the pool reaches full capacity. While Prototypical Networks use class-mean embeddings for few-shot classification,
CaLLM uses distributional prototypes for task-level routing. This approach inherits the non-parametric simplicity of
metric learning, where no gradient-based router optimization is required, and the model absorbs novel distributions
simply by initializing new prototypes. A core architectural advantage of CaLLM is its constant computational overhead:
only one adapter is active per forward pass, while the remaining adapters reside on disk.
We evaluate CaLLM on two complementary benchmarks: TRACE-8, which tests robustness to radical cross-domain
shifts, and CITB-191, which tests scalability on a long horizon dialogue stream, under offline (task-incremental) and
online protocols. CaLLM outperforms CL baselines on the majority of tasks in both benchmarks, with the largest gains
observed on specialized domains where the pretrained backbone is weakest. Furthermore, a systematic analysis of pool
capacity reveals that moderate pooling outperforms both full isolation and single-adapter training in online settings. In
this regime, we observe that interleaved gradients from semantically grouped tasks function as implicit replay, fostering
constructive interference that enhances generalization without the overhead of a physical replay buffer.
In summary, our primary contribution is the complete CaLLM system, which unifies non-parametric routing with
dynamic adapter isolation. Specifically:
• We design and validate an end-to-end modular framework that achieves robust performance while maintaining
a constant computational overhead by activating only one adapter per forward pass.
• We introduce a training-free routing mechanism that scales dynamically to multiple tasks without requiring
gradient-based router optimization.
• We provide a comprehensive evaluation under both task-incremental and long-horizon online streaming
scenarios, uncovering how constrained pool capacities foster constructive cross-task transfer.

task-incremental and online results, and pool capacity effects. Section §5 summarizes our findings and future directions.

RELATED WORK
Continual learning foundations.
CL aims to accumulate knowledge from non-stationary streams without catastrophic
forgetting. In this paradigm, each newly introduced task represents an inherently out-of-distribution shift relative to
the historical training data seen by the model so far. Existing methods generally utilize replay (Rolnick et al., 2019;
Lopez-Paz & Ranzato, 2017), regularization (Farajtabar et al., 2020), or parameter isolation (Rusu et al., 2016; Kang
et al., 2023). Beyond architectural design, evaluation is a critical aspect. Recent studies advocate for per-iteration
metrics to detect the “stability gap”—a transient but severe forgetting phase during training (De Lange et al., 2023)—and
transferability-aware embeddings to leverage inter-task relationships (Wu et al., 2025). Online Continual Learning
(OCL) (Cai et al., 2021) further emphasizes learning efficacy via a single-pass “test-then-train” protocol. This regime
requires optimizing for immediate adaptation rather than long-term convergence, tracking performance through temporal
moving averages instead of static end-of-task scalars.
Continual learning for LLMs.
Adapting LLMs involves navigating vertical (pretrain-to-fine-tune) and horizontal
(temporal shift) continuity. We focus on Continual Fine-Tuning (CFT), evaluated via benchmarks like TRACE (cross-
domain robustness) and CITB (long-horizon instruction streams). LoRA is the primary vehicle for CFT, with methods
managing interference via subspace isolation, enforcing orthogonality through geometric constraints (Wang et al.,
2023a; Liang & Li, 2024) or gradient projection (Qiao et al., 2024). TreeLoRA (Qian et al., 2025) organises per-layer
LoRAs in a gradient-similarity tree, exploring task relationships via lower-confidence-bound bandits. However, these
often sum branches with equal weight at inference, failing to select the most relevant parameters. While GainLoRA
(Liang & Li, 2025) and SwitchCIT (Wu et al., 2024) introduce gating or switch networks, they require specialized
optimization. Conversely, replay-based methods (Scialom et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024; He et al., 2024) maintain
performance but add memory overhead. Notably, recent work suggests that much degradation in LLMs stems from
disrupted task alignment rather than true knowledge loss (Ma et al., 2025).

Routing and mixture-of-experts approaches.
Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures decouple model
capacity from per-input computation by routing each token to a small subset of parallel experts (Fedus et al., 2022;
Jiang et al., 2024). Learned routers discover interpretable data partitions aligned with latent clusters (Dikkala et al.,
2023). In parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), LoRAMoE (Dou et al., 2024) and MoRAL (Yang et al., 2024) use
balancing losses to preserve world knowledge, while MoA (Feng et al., 2024), SEE (Wang et al., 2025), and MLLM-CL
(Zhao et al., 2025) assigns specific LoRA modules to different tasks or modalities, routing them at inference time to
minimize interference. Despite their modularity, these methods generally rely on gradient-based gating and specialized
training objectives and they are highly susceptible to routing drift and catastrophic forgetting within the router itself.
Furthermore, they require complex balancing and regularization losses to prevent expert collapse. CaLLM overcomes
this by using a non-parametric metric-based router, effectively decoupling router scaling from gradient optimization.
Prototypical networks and metric-based routing.
CaLLM’s router is grounded in metric-based meta-learning
(Vettoruzzo et al., 2024), drawing inspiration from Prototypical Networks. By computing distances to centroids in a
learned embedding space, this non-parametric bias enables robust adaptation without inner-loop optimization. While
prototypes have traditionally been used for low-level class-incremental classification (De Lange & Tuytelaars, 2021;
Zhu et al., 2021), CaLLM transposes this to high-level system routing. Rather than using embeddings to represent a
single class, CaLLM uses embedding prototypes to capture the geometric boundaries of entire multi-task streams. This
allows the model to map task-level representation to isolated parameter spaces that are assigned to specific adapters.
This yields a training-free router that inherits the modularity of MoE but avoids the overhead of learned gating. By
combining parameter isolation with an online, no-replay constraint, CaLLM provides a scalable alternative that handles
novel tasks via prototype expansion and bounds memory through an repurposing policy.
3
CONTINUAL ADAPTATION OF LLMS (CALLM)
CaLLM addresses catastrophic forgetting through a prototype-based routing mechanism coupled with dynamic, task-
specific parameter-efficient adapters. The central idea is to maintain a pool of lightweight adapter modules, each
specializing in a region of the task embedding space. As illustrated in Figure 1, the system pipeline integrates four
primary processes: (i) a non-parametric router that extracts embeddings from incoming data and maps data batches to
adapters; (ii) an adapter pool that manages a collection of P parameter-efficient adapters and their associated prototypes;
(iii) a model trainer that performs localized SFT on selected adapters; and (iv) an evaluation pipeline that assesses
performance across task streams to quantify learning progress and monitor for catastrophic forgetting. By routing data
based on semantic similarity or spawning new adapters for sufficiently novel inputs, CaLLM decouples task capacity,
allowing selective reuse or isolation as the task distribution evolves.
CaLLM operates in two modes: Batched (CaLLM-B), where tasks arrive sequentially, and Online (CaLLM-O), where
small, randomly interleaved chunks from multiple tasks arrive in a non-stationary stream. Both share routing, training,
and evaluation components but differ in data presentation order, evaluation protocol, and CL metric computation.

CaLLM supports two CL regimes that share the training objective above but differ in data presentation, evaluation
timing, and metric computation. Full pseudocode for both protocols is provided in Appendix B.1.
Batched task-incremental protocol. In Batched mode, tasks arrive sequentially as complete datasets D1, D2, . . . , DT ,
each with train, evaluation, and test splits (Algorithm 1). For each task with dataset Dt, the system first extracts an
embedding from a sample of the training split and queries the router (with prototype updates enabled) to select or create
an adapter. The selected adapter is then loaded onto the base model and fine-tuned on the full training split using the
SFT objective (Eq. 5), with the evaluation split used for early stopping and checkpoint selection. After training, the
adapter is saved to disk.
Evaluation proceeds in two phases. First, the trained adapter is evaluated on the current task’s test set to measure
adaptation quality. Second, to assess backward transfer, all previously seen tasks j < t are re-evaluated: for each prior
task, the router operates in read-only mode (no prototype updates) to find the best-matching adapter under the current
prototype set, and that adapter is loaded and evaluated on task j’s test set. Backward transfer is then computed as the
average normalized performance change across prior tasks.
Online continual learning protocol. In Online mode, data arrives as a stream of small chunks randomly interleaved
across multiple tasks in a non-stationary distribution. (Algorithm 2). Each chunk contains a fixed number of examples
(chunk size) from a single task.
Following the test-then-train paradigm, the protocol begins with the router extracting a chunk’s embedding ei to select,
create, or repurpose an adapter according to the logic described above. Once the parameter subspace is determined, the
chunk is immediately evaluated using the selected adapter, or the frozen backbone if a new adapter is being initialized,
prior to any gradient updates. This pre-update score provides an unbiased measure of the system’s current knowledge
and is used to update the continual learning metrics defined below. After evaluation, the selected adapter is fine-tuned
on the input chunk via SFT (Eq. 5). This single-pass procedure ensures structural consistency and strictly avoids
post-update evaluation to prevent information leakage, allowing CaLLM to satisfy the core assumptions of online
learning while tracking knowledge accumulation in real-time.

4
EXPERIMENTS & RESULTS
We organize our experimental analysis around a progression from defining setup to understanding mechanism, followed
by performance evaluation and stress-testing.
4.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Benchmarks. We evaluate CaLLM on two complementary benchmarks that test different axes of CL difficulty:
1. TRACE-8 (Wang et al., 2023b) (Diversity & Shift) comprises 8 heterogeneous tasks spanning domain-
specific QA (ScienceQA, FOMC, MeetingBank), multilingual processing (C-STANCE, 20Minuten), code
completion (Py150), and math reasoning (NumGLUE-cm/ds) testing robustness to radical distribution shifts.
We utilize the variant with 5000 training examples per task.
2. CITB-19 (Zhang et al., 2023) (Scale & Granularity) consists of 19 dialogue-centric tasks derived from Super-
NaturalInstructions (Wang et al., 2022), categorized into Dialogue State Tracking (4), Dialogue Generation
(11), and Intent Identification (4). This suite is used to test long-horizon streams and fine-grained intra-domain
discrimination. To ensure training stability, we utilize a refined version of this benchmark augmented with
3000 high-quality synthetic examples per task (see Appendix A.5 for the generation and filtering protocol).
This is the dataset version used in all our experiments.
Task performance is measured using standard metrics appropriate to each task type: accuracy for classification and
multiple-choice tasks, ROUGE-L for summarization and all CITB-19 tasks, SARI for text simplification, and EDIM
(edit-distance fuzzy match ratio) for code completion. In label-free stream settings, metric assignment is handled
automatically via LLM-based task classification (Appendix A.6).
All input data is formatted using an Alpaca-style instruction template (Instruction / Response / <eos>),
ensuring a uniform interface across diverse task types. Following the protocols defined in §3.3, the Batched setting
processes tasks as complete sequential blocks, while the Online setting uses a non-stationary stream of max chunks
steps. Each step consists of chunk size examples, with chunks randomly interleaved across tasks using a fixed seed to
ensure reproducibility.
Baselines. We compare CaLLM against five representative CL strategies: Sequential Adapter Fine-tuning (Seq-FT),
a single LoRA adapter fine-tuned sequentially on the task stream; Inference-only, the zero-shot performance of the
frozen backbone; Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017), a regularization-based method
penalizing shifts in important weights; Experience Replay (ER) (Rolnick et al., 2019), a replay-based method with a
fixed memory buffer; Orthogonal LoRA (oLoRA) (Wang et al., 2023a) a parameter-efficient CL baseline that learns
tasks in orthogonal low-rank subspaces. TreeLoRA (Qian et al., 2025): layer-wise LoRAs organised by a hierarchical
gradient-similarity tree with bandit-based task-relationship exploration.
All experiments use gemma-3-12b-it (4-bit quantized) as the backbone, selected for its superior zero-shot performance
across all tasks (see Appendix A.1). Appendix A.2 shows implementation details and hyperparameter configuration.
The code will be openly released upon paper acceptance.

4.3
TASK-INCREMENTAL RESULTS
We first evaluate CaLLM’s end-to-end performance in the standard batched task-incremental setting, where tasks arrive
one at a time and the system trains on each complete dataset sequentially.
Final performance by individual task for CALLM-B and other baselines for TRACE benchmark is shown in Table 1.
CaLLM-B achieves the highest score among all baseline methods on 4 of 8 tasks. The most significant gains over the
inference-only baseline appear on specialized domains where the pre-trained backbone is weakest: code completion
(Py150, +2.33 in EDIM), science reasoning (ScienceQA, 0.35 →0.81 in accuracy), and math (NumGLUE-ds,
6× improvement in accuracy). TreeLoRA’s wins concentrate on the two arithmetic splits, where its hierarchical
gradient-similarity tree consolidates closely related skills (mean accuracy 0.60 across NumGLUE-cm/ds, versus 0.28
for CaLLM-B), but it collapses on Py150 (5.52 vs. 17.24 in EDIM), where the code-generation gradient signature
is dissimilar from the natural language tasks and the tree potentially mis-routes the adapter, a failure mode that
CaLLM-B’s prototype router avoids by treating each task as a first-class cluster. Conversely, on C-STANCE and
20Minuten, performance is largely inherited from the backbone; on the former, fine-tuning slightly degrades pre-existing
competence, while on the latter, all methods fall within a narrow 2-point SARI band. Because 20Minuten is the final task
in the sequence, no method suffers from forgetting, narrowing the performance gap between CaLLM-B and baselines.
Notably, CL baselines underperform Inference-Only on several tasks (e.g., C-STANCE, FOMC, MeetingBank, Py150).
We attribute this to Inference-Only’s strong large-scale backbone—whose pre-trained competence makes it a strong
baseline rather than a trivial lower bound—and to its avoidance of the catastrophic forgetting that erodes CL baselines as
the task stream grows (Appendix A.3 shows this gap persists even with per-baseline hyperparameter tuning). CaLLM-B
avoids this trade-off by routing task-specific updates to isolated experts, preserving the backbone’s zero-shot competence
while enabling specialization.

4.5
THE ROLE OF POOL CAPACITY
The router embedding analysis (§4.2) showed that the router is capable of separating task clusters both in diverse and
fine-grained task distributions. When pool capacity is unconstrained, each cluster maps to a unique expert, achieving
full isolation. But in practice, memory budgets are limited and the number of tasks to be seen is not always known in
advance. We now investigate what happens as pool size P is progressively reduced, revealing a spectrum of routing
behaviors from perfect isolation to system collapse.
4.5.1
EXPERT ASSIGNMENT DYNAMICS IN TASK-INCREMENTAL LEARNING
Figure 5 visualizes the evolution of expert assignments and the corresponding BWT curves on TRACE-8 as pool size
varies from P = 8 to P = 2 (see Appendix D.2 for extended plots).
• Ideal Isolation (P = 8): The router assigns unique experts to each task, resulting in zero BWT loss. This
confirms that the router’s embedding clusters (Figure 2(a)) translate directly into correct expert assignment.
• Constructive Clustering (P = 6): Semantically related tasks (e.g., NumGLUE-cm/ds) are co-routed to
Expert 5, while other tasks retain dedicated experts. Because these merged tasks share similar gradient
directions, parameter sharing is beneficial rather than destructive. The BWT curve remains relatively stable
with a slight dip at Step 7 when the merge occurs.
• Destructive Overload (P = 4): A “super-expert” absorbs disparate tasks (QA, Stance, Math), leading to
conflicting updates and a sharp BWT drop to −0.19. Notably, the router still identifies Py150 (code) as
maximally distinct, preserving its isolation in a separate expert even under severe constraints.

4.5.2
POOL CAPACITY CONSTRAINTS IN THE ONLINE SCENARIO
The batched analysis reveals a clear capacity-stability trade-off which we investigate here for the online scenario.
We evaluate CaLLM-O over 500-chunk streams with varying pool sizes on TRACE (P ∈{8, 6, 4}) and CITB
(P ∈{19, 10, 3}), against SeqFT (P=1), revealing a non-monotonic relation between performance and capacity.
Stability over capacity.
On TRACE, P=4 achieves the highest final OMA (0.42) and the best BWT, outperforming
both P=8 (0.39) and P=6 (0.38) (see Appendix C.1 for full plots). Routing stability—the consistency of task-
to-expert assignment—outweighs raw capacity (see Appendix D.3.1 Fig. 12). At P=4, the router converges on
four semantically coherent groups: (1) C-STANCE; (2) quantitative tasks (NumGLUE-ds, NumGLUE-cm, Py150);
(3) FOMC; (4) long-form understanding (ScienceQA, MeetingBank, 20Minuten). Interleaved chunks within each group
provide implicit replay via alternating gradient signals, fostering constructive interference. Conversely, P=6 suffers
from task displacement: 20Minuten is scattered across multiple experts, preventing specialization. P=8 achieves
perfect isolation but forgoes the co-assignment regularization that benefits the streaming regime.
Similarity and forgetting.
In more homogeneous streams like CITB-19, performance is less sensitive to capacity;
since similar tasks tolerate compression well, though smaller pools still cause deeper forgetting spikes. Task similarity
ultimately determines how sensitive a stream is to pool size, diverse streams need larger, more carefully tuned pools.
5
CONCLUSION
We presented CaLLM, a modular continual learning method for LLMs that pairs a bounded pool of lightweight adapters
with a non-parametric, prototype-based router operating entirely in the frozen backbone’s embedding space, requiring
no gradient-based training or task labels at inference. Incoming data are matched to the nearest expert via cosine
distance, with new experts spawned or repurposed automatically when the data is sufficiently novel. Our experiments
on TRACE-8 and CITB-19 demonstrate that CaLLM is effective across both batched and online CL settings, where
it reliably discovers distributional boundaries from input features alone, and translates this geometric separation into
accurate, task-agnostic expert assignments. Although CITB-19 shows that the router can separate fine-grained tasks
that share a language and format, we do not test whether it can keep tasks together when they overlap conceptually but
differ lexically (e.g. the same task across languages), which we leave to future evaluation. Notably, we observe that
limiting the adapter pool size can boost performance by forming semantically coherent groups and using interleaved
gradients as implicit replay, without requiring memory buffers.
Future directions include integrating online AutoML to adapt currently fixed hyperparameters as the stream unfolds,
explicitly merging related adapters to consolidate shared knowledge, and extending prototype-based routing to multi-
modal streams spanning vision, language, and other modalities simultaneously.

A.2
IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS AND HYPERPARAMETERS
As detailed in Appendix A.1, all experiments use gemma-3-12b-it (4-bit quantized) as the primary backbone. We main-
tain this fixed model for all comparisons, though CaLLM is backbone-agnostic and compatible with any Transformer-
based LLM. The router utilizes the same frozen backbone as a feature extractor, where the last hidden states are
mean-pooled over valid tokens to obtain a sample embedding and then averaged across the input batch to produce a
single representation for routing.
LoRA adapters are trained using the HuggingFace SFTTrainer with an 8-bit AdamW optimizer, gradient clipping at
norm 1.0, and bfloat16 mixed precision without sequence packing. Additional training constants include a maximum
sequence length of 1024, temperature of 1.0, LoRA dropout of 0.05, and a warmup ratio of 0.05. In the online setting,
we use max chunks = 500 and chunk size = 40, with chunks generated via random resampling. All input follows
the Alpaca prompt style detailed in §4.1, and the router uses the adaptive threshold defined in Eq. 4.
Batched experiments are reported as the mean and standard deviation across three random seeds with optimal configura-
tions selected based on final mean validation performance. Online experiments are instead performed as a single run
due to the significant computational cost of 500-chunk streams. We rely on the OMA metric, which averages over 500
evaluation steps, to provide inherent statistical stability. Table 3 summarizes the specific hyperparameters selected via
grid search over learning rate ∈{10−4, 5 × 10−4, 10−3}, LoRA rank ∈{4, 8, 16}, and epochs ∈{3, 5}. To mitigate
the substantial computational overhead of tuning long-horizon streams, hyperparameter optimization for the online
setting was restricted to a truncated proxy window of 200 evaluation steps under a standard pre-update (test-than-train)
evaluation protocol. All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU with 40GB of VRAM
using Unsloth (Han et al., 2023) and the trl, peft and transformers libraries from HuggingFace.

DETAILS ABOUT THE BASELINES
To evaluate CaLLM, we compare it against several representative strategies from the CL literature, adapted for the
PEFT regime. To ensure a fair and rigorous comparison, all baselines share the same core architectural parameters as
CaLLM and the same LoRA configuration (rank, alpha, and dropout). We then utilize the optimal hyperparameters
(such as regularization strength or buffer size) specific to each method as reported in their respective original papers,
unless stated otherwise.
To understand the potential impact of this design choice on the fairness of baseline comparisons, we conducted an
ablation study of hyperparameter search on selected continual learning baselines, namely ER and EWC. The search
follows the CaLLM search space over learning rate and number of training epochs, using a grid search over learning
rate ∈{10−4, 5 × 10−4, 10−3} and epochs ∈{3, 5}. The ablation focuses on the TRACE benchmark, due to its
higher variation across tasks and smaller number of tasks. Results of this search are presented in Table 4, including the
original hyperparameter values and performance results. As shown in the table, the search selects a hyperparameter
combination different from the originally chosen values for each baseline (where the per-task performance does not
always improve compared with the original setting). However, the magnitude of the performance difference is small,
and may also be explained by the inherent randomness of LLM generations. Importantly, this does not change the
ordering of baseline–method comparisons for any task in the analysis, supporting the original design choice.
• Inference only is a simple baseline where the base model (e.g. gemma-3-12b-it) is used during inference in a
continual/online way, every time a new task/chunk is presented to the model.
• Sequential Adapter Fine-Tuning (Seq-FT): A single LoRA adapter is fine-tuned sequentially on the task
stream. This represents the lower bound for stability, as it is highly susceptible to catastrophic forgetting.
• Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017): A regularization-based approach that
penalizes changes to parameters that were important for previous tasks. Importance is estimated via the
diagonal of the Fisher Information Matrix. We apply EWC to the LoRA weights specifically.
• Experience Replay (ER) (Rolnick et al., 2019): A memory buffer stores a subset of samples from previous
tasks. During the adaptation to a new task, each training batch is augmented with replayed samples from the
buffer to preserve past knowledge. In our experiments, we use a fixed buffer size of 1000 samples.
• Orthogonal LoRA (OLoRA) (Wang et al., 2023a): A subspace isolation method that constrains the LoRA
weight updates to be orthogonal to the pre-trained weights or previous task directions, aiming to prevent
gradient-based interference. To ensure compatibility with the Gemma backbone and avoid out-of-memory
(OOM) errors, we modified the original OLoRA implementation as follows: (i) reduced the evaluation batch
size to 4, instead of using the batch size, and (ii) offloaded the orthogonal loss calculation to the CPU. For
the TRACE-8 online stream, we utilized batch size = 8, r = 4, α = 8. For CITB, we used batch size = 2,
r = 4, α = 8, and gradient accumulation step of 8.
• K-D Tree of Low-Rank Adapters (TreeLoRA) (Qian et al., 2025): Introduces layer-wise adapters to
construct hierarchical tree based on the criteria of gradient similarity, exploring task relationships via lower-
confidence-bound bandits.

GEMMA-3-12B-IT TRAINING DATA AND CITB OVERLAP
Gemma 3 models are well-suited for text-generation tasks such as question answering, summarization, and reasoning.
The 12B model used in our work was trained on 12T tokens consisting of web documents (in over 140 languages),
code, mathematics, and images. The pre-training data is not released openly and there is limited information about the
data sources. Additionally, the IT variant has been instruction-tuned using knowledge distillation from larger teacher
models (likely Gemini models).
15


Published at 5th Conference on Lifelong Learning Agents (CoLLAs), 2026
CITB is built on top of the Super-NaturalInstructions dataset (Wang et al., 2022). In this work, we focus on the
InstrDialog task stream, which consists of 19 dialogue-related tasks spanning three categories: dialogue state tracking
(4 tasks), dialogue generation (11 tasks), and intent identification (4 tasks). All tasks are evaluated using the ROUGE-L
metric.
While there is no clear information about the data used by Gemma 3 for pre-training or fine-tuning, it is plausible that the
Super-NaturalInstructions dataset (on which CITB is based) was included in the training data of either Gemma 3 or the
Gemini teacher models. This would explain why LoRA fine-tuning on CITB does not improve over the inference-only
baseline. Despite this, all other baselines suffer from sequential fine-tuning, suggesting that CaLLM maintains the
original performance even when the data has already been seen.
To investigate potential data contamination, we applied the method of Shi et al. (2023), which computes per-token
probabilities under the hypothesis that “seen” examples have fewer low-probability outliers with respect to “unseen”
words. We compared 500 CITB samples against 309 “old” Wikipedia pages (likely within Gemma 3’s training data)
and 97 Wikipedia pages created in the following 60 days (unlikely to be in the training data). The results are as follows:
• Member mean: 0.005722
• Non-member mean: 0.003475
• CITB mean: 0.002848
From these results we can conclude that CITB has an average probability closer to the non-member samples than the
(assumed) member samples, suggesting that CITB is likely not in the training data of Gemma 3.
This appears to contradict our earlier hypothesis that Gemma 3 has already seen CITB data. Indeed, Figure 3b shows
that fine-tuning with CITB data does not improve over the inference-only baseline. However, it is important to note that
the method of Shi et al. (2023) is only an empirical heuristic, and the candidate member and non-member sets are based
on assumptions about Wikipedia page creation dates rather than ground-truth training data membership.

Each task contains only 500 training instances, which is insufficient for effectively training LoRA adapters and creates
an imbalance when compared to the TRACE-8 benchmark (5000 instances per task). To ensure a fair comparison and
improve adaptation stability, we generated synthetic training data for each CITB task. Inspired by self-synthesized
rehearsal strategies (Huang et al., 2024), we utilized the frozen gemma-3-12b-it model as a data generator. We employed
a few-shot prompting strategy, providing the model with two randomly sampled real instances in-context and tasking
it to generate a novel example following the same Alpaca-style format. By iterating this process with randomized
in-context examples, we produced an initial pool of 6000 synthetic instances per task.To ensure data quality and
distributional consistency, we applied a two-stage refinement pipeline:
1. Deduplication: We removed all exact string matches and near-duplicates to ensure diversity within the
synthetic pool.
2. Centroid-based filtering: To retain only high-quality, representative samples, we converted the synthetic
instances into embeddings using the frozen base model. We then applied K-means clustering to the embeddings
of each task. Finally, we calculated the Euclidean distance between each instance and its respective cluster
centroid, selecting the 3000 instances closest to the centroid for the final training set.
This procedure resulted in a refined CITB dataset containing 3000 examples per task. This refined version maintains
the semantic integrity of the original tasks while providing sufficient signal for parameter-efficient fine-tuning, and it
serves as the standard CITB data stream used for all experiments and baseline comparisons presented in this paper.

AUTOMATIC METRIC SELECTION
When ground-truth metric labels are unavailable (i.e., in label-free stream configurations), CaLLM dispatches an
automatic metric selection procedure at the start of each task’s evaluation phase. This adds only a negligible time
overhead from a single short inference call and no memory overhead, as it reuses the already-loaded backbone. The
procedure works as follows.
Model.
The same base LLM used for task inference (gemma-3-12b-it, 4-bit quantized) serves as the zero-shot
classifier for metric selection. This avoid the overhead of loading an auxiliary model and the system simply executes a
single generation call before the model training and evaluation on each task.
Meta-prompt template.
A single representative prompt is sampled from the task’s test split, truncated to 700
characters to manage context, and embedded into a structured meta-prompt. The prompt instructs the LLM to categorize
the task based on its linguistic and structural features (e.g., presence of code-specific keywords, multiple-choice
indicators, or summarization requests). The model is constrained to a closed set of responses: ACCURACY, ROUGE,
SARI, or EDIM.

Training compute.
The central result is that CaLLM’s training cost is independent of pool size: all four configurations
(P = 2, 4, 6, 8) consume exactly 7,339 TFLOPs. This is a direct consequence of the modular architecture—only one
adapter is active per forward pass, so the computational graph is identical regardless of how many adapters exist in
the pool. Training throughput is also stable across pool sizes (3.67–3.87 samples/s), confirming that adapter switching
introduces negligible latency.
CaLLM and Seq-FT share identical training FLOPs (7,339 TFLOPs), as expected—both perform the same number of
gradient updates on the same data with the same adapter rank. The difference lies entirely in where gradients are routed,
not in how many are computed. CaLLM achieves slightly higher throughput (3.81–3.87 vs. 3.53 samples/s) and lower
wall-clock time (59–60 h vs. 68 h), likely because isolated adapters encounter simpler optimization landscapes than a
shared adapter accumulating all tasks.
Routing overhead.
Unlike the baselines, CaLLM performs an additional forward pass through the frozen backbone
for prototype matching (§ 3.2). Because this routing pass is encoder-only, i.e., no decoding, over a short input and
reuses the already-loaded backbone, its marginal cost is small relative to the per-task training and inference compute.
Using the standard transformer estimate of approximately 2N FLOPs per forward token, where N ≈12 × 109 for
gemma-3-12b-it, and noting that each routing call processes n ≈20 prompts of approximately 10 tokens each,
one routing call costs 2 · (12 × 109) · 20 · 10 ≈4.8 × 1012 FLOPs, i.e., 4.8 TFLOPs. Over the full TRACE-8
batched run, with one routing call per task across 8 tasks, the router consumes 8 · 4.8 ≈38.4 TFLOPs, or roughly
(38.4/7339) × 100 ≈0.5% of the 7339 TFLOPs reported above for training. This is consistent with the observation
that CaLLM and Seq-FT report identical training TFLOPs in Table 6: the router’s contribution falls below the reporting
precision. The same logic applies at inference time, where each routing call is amortized over all subsequent evaluation
queries within a task, giving per-query overhead ≪0.1%, and during the online continual-learning phase, where routing
19


Published at 5th Conference on Lifelong Learning Agents (CoLLAs), 2026
is invoked once per chunk rather than per query. The routing module is, moreover, a tunable component: a smaller
encoder or cached prompt embeddings could reduce this already-small overhead further, which we leave to future work.
Baseline comparison.
Among the baselines that explicitly address forgetting, each pays a distinct overhead. EWC
trains on the same data with the same adapter rank, so its per-step FLOPs are expected to be comparable to CaLLM;
the additional wall-clock cost (76.9 h) comes from Fisher information computation after each task. ER augments each
training batch with replay buffer samples, which increases the effective data budget and thus is expected to incur higher
total training FLOPs—this is reflected in its 2.1× wall-clock overhead (125.8 h). oLoRA uses a smaller rank (r=4 vs.
r=16), which should reduce per-step FLOPs, but must maintain orthogonal projection matrices across all subspaces
simultaneously, making it the most memory-intensive baseline at 25.2 GB (2.1× CaLLM).
These results confirm that CaLLM achieves its anti-forgetting benefits at zero additional training compute relative to
na¨ıve sequential fine-tuning, while the baselines that attempt to mitigate forgetting through regularization, replay, or
orthogonality constraints each pay a substantial cost in time, memory, or both.
Memory management.
CaLLM maintains a constant 12.15 GB GPU memory footprint regardless of pool size,
because exactly one adapter is loaded on GPU at any time. Inactive adapters are offloaded to CPU memory and swapped
in on demand, so the GPU overhead does not grow with the number of adapters in the pool. The maximum pool capacity

Online regime.
The online streaming configuration uses lighter adapters (r=4, α=8) and single-epoch chunk updates,

EXTENDED ROUTER ANALYSIS
This section provides a deep dive into the router’s internal logic, visualizing how it represents tasks and how capacity
constraints (P) dictate expert assignment.
D.1
TASK SEPARABILITY IN THE EMBEDDING SPACE
Figure 9 illustrates the inherent separability of the task distributions within the router’s embedding space. Using t-SNE
projections of the frozen backbone’s mean-pooled features, we observe that diverse tasks (both for TRACE-8 and
CITB-19) form well-defined, distant clusters.
Figure 9: Router Embedding Space (2D Projection). t-SNE projections of the router’s embeddings, coloured by task
identity. (a) TRACE-8: 8 tasks spanning diverse domains. (b) CITB-19: 19 dialogue sub-tasks.
D.2
PROTOTYPE DYNAMICS AND CAPACITY PROGRESSION
Figure 10 demonstrate how the router dynamically partitions the embedding space under varying capacity constrains,
studied in §4.5.1, for TRACE-8 . Each panel overlays P prototypes (yellow stars) on the task-cluster structure; grey
lines connect each data point to its nearest prototype. The prototype positions determine which tasks share an expert
during training.
24


Published at 5th Conference on Lifelong Learning Agents (CoLLAs), 2026
At P=8 (Figure 10a), every prototype settles at the centroid of one task cluster, giving zero-interference isolation. At
P=6 (Figure 10b), the two NumGLUE math tasks and the C-STANCE dataset are pulled toward a shared prototype, a
constructive merge that incurs negligible BWT. At P=4 (Figure 10c), one prototype absorbs five semantically diverse
tasks on the right side of the space, producing the conflicting gradients that drive destructive overload. Even here, Py150
(code) retains its own isolated prototype (in red), confirming the router’s sensitivity to the most extreme distributional
gap. At P=2 (Figure 10d), only 20Minuten keeps a dedicated prototype; all other tasks collapse onto a single expert.

(b) P=6: NumGLUE-cm/ds share a prototype—constructive
merge, negligible BWT.
(c) P=4: one prototype absorbs 5 diverse tasks—destructive
overload, BWT = −0.19.
(d) P=2: system collapse, 7 of 8 tasks share one prototype,

ONLINE STREAMING HEATMAP
While the previous plots show the router assignment in the Batched scenario, Figure 11 shows the complete 500-chunk
